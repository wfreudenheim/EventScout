Last month, our members stepped inside one of New York’s most storied galleries. 🖼️

In honor of Gen’ichirō Inokuma’s NYC Salon, Barbara Bertozzi Castelli led a private tour of Castelli Gallery’s fall exhibition, Arakawa: Do You Like This Painting? The connection runs deep: during his two decades in New York, Inokuma moved within the same artistic circles Leo Castelli helped build, forming friendships with artists like Jasper Johns and Robert Rauschenberg. Arakawa, who arrived from Tokyo in 1961, brought his own avant-garde sensibility into that same world.

Thank you to Barbara Bertozzi Castelli and the Castelli Gallery team for welcoming us, and to our members for joining!

Happy Friday the 13th from the cat that lived too long. 🐈⬛

In the West, the black cat crossing your path is a daunting omen. In Edo-period Japan, the fear ran deeper: a cat that lived long enough could become a bakeneko, a shape-shifter that walks on two legs, speaks like a human and takes the form of someone unassuming.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi captured exactly that moment in this 1850 print. It illustrates a kabuki ghost story set at Okazaki, a post town on the Tōkaidō road, where a weary traveler stops for the night at a temple and is welcomed by a kindly old woman. She is not what she seems. Kuniyoshi reveals the truth: a monstrous cat, eyes glowing, filling the entire room.

Here’s the twist: in Japan, black cats are fukuneko, bringers of good fortune, and the black maneki-neko is the one that keeps evil away.

"Kanshasai" is Thanksgiving. After the feast, your "onaka ga ippai" (your stomach is full!), or even "panpan" if you went back for thirds. In December, Japan wishes you "merii kurisumasu" and lines up for "kentakkii" (yes, KFC is a Christmas tradition there). And "kabocha", Japanese pumpkin, shows up on tables at the winter solstice.

Teens learn all this and more in Festivities & Phrases: Holidays in Japanese, an online class for middle and high school students. Beginners and experienced learners are welcome.
