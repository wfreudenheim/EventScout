Dear readers, 

I joined n+1 as a volunteer in the fall of 2005, roughly a year and a half after the first issue appeared. An office had been secured on the Lower East Side, and some evenings Mark Greif, one of the founding editors, would host an occasional series where he invited contributors and friends of the fledgling magazine to answer off-kilter, probing questions, to which we were encouraged to give honest answers. “Theory or practice?” was one. A barrage of divided answers followed. “Where did your life go wrong?” he followed. “College,” one person answered, bluntly. “Reading The Catcher in the Rye,” volunteered another. With only a little hesitation: “When I developed secondary sex characteristics.” 

I think of this simultaneously lighthearted and serious interrogation every year that the weather starts to cool and the n+1 Bookmatch quiz arrives in my inbox. All the alternately silly and bookish questions in this nonpareil personality quiz seem to hearken back to the magazine’s founding impulses to think clearly and write honestly (but not humorlessly). 

Bookmatch announces the holiday season in uproarious fashion, but it supports a project that is of enduring significance. Bigger magazines and journalistic institutions are struggling, under-edited newsletters proliferate and flood inboxes alongside endless fundraising emails sent from politicians (😉). 

But n+1, discerning, sparing, and always well-edited, is alive and well, publishing the profoundest work of some of our best writers—some of whom first achieved prominence in our pages. From the thick argot of Texas immigrant life in Paul Soto’s fiction to the personal travels down the forking paths of carceral and academic life in America in Dawn Lundy Martin’s award-winning essays, n+1 makes room for an entire experience of contemporary life. As spaces for reviewing diminish, it also does the important work of keeping up to speed with what is current, reviewing the fiction of Deborah Levy and the feminist theory of Sophie Lewis and—quite literally—the artistic and photographic and political use of the color orange.

Having left the magazine to become a Pennsylvania state senator and pursue the relentless demands of politics, I’ve found that n+1—with its broad encompassing horizon, and its willingness to ask all the right questions (and also some of the weird right ones)—has only become more important to my life and more indispensable. In this moment of political opening on the left, the less instrumental, more imaginative approach to contemporary life that n+1 offers, unequaled, in superabundance, is just what I need. This work is only possible with your support—so please donate today.  

Yours,
Nikil Saval
Former coeditor, n+1
