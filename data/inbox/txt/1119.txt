THERESA HAK KYUNG CHA: MULTIPLE OFFERINGS

BY MAX LEVIN
NOVEMBER 10, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

“The landscape exists inside the screen. On the other side of it and beyond,” wrote Theresa Hak Kyung Cha in Dictée (1982), a book combining poetry, prose, imagery, history, and grammar exercises. Here, she is both writing about an opaque haze lingering in summer humidity and also describing her approach to art-making, where language, images, and gestures can pass through and be animated by something else, somewhere else, surviving by shifting in and out of bands of legibility. In film, video, writing, drawing, performance, installation, ceramics, and even stamps, Cha developed cadences that short-circuited and rewired spectated constructions of meaning. “Multiple Offerings” is a fitting title for a retrospective of Cha’s work, which lovingly pursues the possibilities of messages being sent and received across time and space.

Rather than isolated in black-box galleries, most of the moving image works in the exhibition are dispersed among objects and ephemera. This results in magnified dialogues between the works themselves in lieu of wall texts. Secret Spill (1974) is installed on the floor near framed documentation of a performance, while Ripples (1970s) is at eye level beside a slide projector advancing images of Cha’s glazed ceramics. The latter is a silent short film comprised of black text that scrolls across a white background. The lowercase cavalcade of sentence fragments narrates disappearance to the audience/reader. Secret Spill finds Cha approaching a bag with a horizontal seam. She unzips it and reveals soil, which she scoops and pours out on the earth below. More than incidental, Cha’s undoing of enclosure enacts an ecological return that is documented as if it were a crime scene, with camouflaging close-ups of barbed wire and knots of rope.

Sitting on the single bench in the exhibition, opposite the video and film installation Exilée (1980), one faces a monitor in the center of a wall-sized projection. Sped-up footage of a sunny, windy room overlays the video screen, which softly dissolves images and text. The soundtrack is Cha’s warm voice, which carries even when her words become muddled in the distance between the speakers and the bench, and because Cha’s intonations match the resonant frequency of the room. Five monophones in the exhibition, which can be lifted like a landline off a wall, amplify Cha’s voice directly from speaker to ear. Even still, there is diffusion: entanglement plus estrangement. The displacement inherent in translation is key to Cha’s work, and she addresses “you” in Audience Distant Relative (1977-1978): “neither you nor i are visible to each other / i can only assume that you can hear me / i can only hope that you can hear me.” We can. Then the reverb effects are turned up and the words slip away.

Theresa Hak Kyung Cha: Multiple Offerings is on view through November 21 at Artists Space. On November 19, “Cinematic Correspondences: Theresa Hak Kyung Cha and her Filmic Influences” will be presented at Anthology Film Archives.
