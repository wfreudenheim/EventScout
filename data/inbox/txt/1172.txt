NEXT WEEKEND: Nikkatsu’s Golden Age of Eros on 35mm and 4K

Series Passes are SOLD OUT! Individual Tickets are still available for select screenings.

Running November 20–December 6, 2026

Celebrating 55 years of the widely influential Roman Porno (roman poruno) film line (1971-1988), Nikkatsu’s Golden Age of Eros explores the legacy of Japanese erotic cinema. Opening next Friday, Nov. 20th—exactly 55 years to the date of Roman Porno’s 1971 launch—this is the first extensive American retrospective on the legendary genre that cultivated a wave of new talent and pushed the boundaries of expression to demonstrate that erotic cinema could be art.

The Rules of Roman Porno

Not to be confused with pinku (independent, low-budget softcore films), the higher budget, elevated studio-backed Roman Porno derived its name from either the French literary genre roman pornographique or a shortening of the word “romantic”. Its primary conventions required the following:

 * A sex scene every 10-15 minutes

 * Runtime of around 70 minutes

 * 10-day shoot with a medium budget

Outside of this, Roman Porno afforded remarkable artistic freedom to filmmakers, giving new opportunities to young and first-time directors.

On The Oldest Profession

Excerpt from essay Osaka Eros for Film Movement

By Alexander Fee

No lines were ever drawn for Kamagasaki. Osaka’s notorious poverty-stricken tract of slums has a history torn from legitimacy, its name erased from official maps and still unacknowledged by the Japanese government which, in 1966, ironically renamed the area Airin (“beloved neighborhood”) to improve its reputation. An impoverished yoseba (day-laborer neighborhood), Kamagasaki was crowded with homeless vagrants, laborers and squatters. The site of numerous riots, prostitution, and yakuza activity, Kamagasaki would retain the highest population density within Osaka by the early 1970s. Doya, cheap hostels catering to the temporary housing needs of its single, male day-laborers, would populate its edifices while neighboring quarter Tobita, a red-light district dating back to the Taisho period, was lined by ryotei (traditional-style Japanese restaurants), a front for Tobita’s brothels upon the 1958 enactment of the Occupation-era instated Anti-Prostitution Act.

Kamagasaki and its surrounding areas were incorporated into The Oldest Profession from the very beginning with Noboru Tanaka choosing to shoot nearly all of the film on location. Its black-and-white cinematography, innately forecasting light and darkness with intimate handheld shots, was often taken surreptitiously on the streets, documenting not only the actors but the actual, living residents of Kamagasaki at the time. Tanaka initially sought permission on shooting locations from local police, only to receive the response, “Can you take responsibility if there’s a riot? If anything happens, we’ll arrest you.”
