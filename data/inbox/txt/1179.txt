Dear friends,

One of my favorite aspects of n+1 office life is hearing about what my comrades and coeditors are reading—about the novels, nonfiction books, newsletters, tweets, best-of lists, and magazine articles that people are enjoying, or hate-reading, or enviously wishing n+1 had thought to commission. 

Specifically I love hearing about this stuff from my colleague Alan Dean, who is probably the n+1 staff’s most expansive reader and surely its most entertaining describer. (He is also an outstanding sports writer.) Pretty much every day Alan treats us to a baroque and mesmerizing account of some colorful intra-left dispute or online subculture (one from the other day involved the website www.seafoodsource.com); in recent months his enthusiasm has driven members of the n+1 staff to read Seth Harp’s The Fort Bragg Cartel, Andrei Platonov’s Chevengur, Robert Coover’s The Universal Baseball Association, Inc., J. Henry Waugh, Prop., and the Martin Beck books, a totally delightful series of socialist crime novels by a Swedish husband and wife duo that I have in turn pushed on everyone I know. It’s fun and edifying to read these texts, but it’s nearly as fun and edifying to hear them recommended by a master recommender.

Whether or not your workplace has its own Alan—and I hope it does—you can, for the moment, experience the exquisite pleasures of an Alan recommendation yourself via Bookmatch, n+1’s annual fundraiser, which matches every supporter of the magazine with a list of reading suggestions based on your answers to a personality quiz. By now everybody knows that taking the quiz is a good time, but in my opinion the best part is what comes after the quiz, when you get to read everyone’s little mini-essays about why they picked the books they picked. This is where a master recommender can really shine, and the writing in these descriptions is worth your time (and your money). Here for example is Alan’s impassioned, slightly scoldy defense of James Fenimore Cooper, whose The Pathfinder may or may get recommended to you, depending on whether the quiz deems it up your alley:

> Despite his foundational role in our national literary tradition, James Fenimore Cooper doesn’t get much love. The novelist, who shredded his own reputation with constant invective penned against his literary and political critics (a cautionary tale), was later trashed in the grave by fellow canonista Mark Twain as having committed “114 offenses against literary art out of a possible 115” in The Deerslayer and, by the end of the 20th century, was largely remembered only by products of the Soviet education system, Michael Mann completionists, and Baseball Hall of Fame visitors who had accidentally wandered down to the lakeshore and read the plaques. That’s too bad, because Last of the Mohicans is one of the genuinely great American novels, and if you haven’t read it, shame on you! The Pathfinder is a less great but more interesting novel, one that begins with an enragingly similar premise: a young woman, called to frontier by her Scottish soldier father, is betrayed by her wily Indian guide only to be rescued by Natty Bumppo and his Delaware comrade, Chingachgook—what the hell! What follows, however, is a unique blend of nautical adventure, frontier romance, and romantic romance, as old Leatherstocking awkwardly tries his hand at courtship while sailing the waters of Lake Ontario. Sure, it’s bursting at the seams like a well-trod moccasin, but just try and read Cooper’s orgiastic descriptions of the terrors and delights of early America's boundless wilderness without feeling something, I dare you.

That is just good, stylish writing! I’d publish some of those sentences in the magazine, happily. For more passionately argued recommendations, from Alan and from many other luminaries, you’ll have to make a donation of any amount and take the Bookmatch quiz. I hope you like what you read. 

Yours,
Lisa Borst
