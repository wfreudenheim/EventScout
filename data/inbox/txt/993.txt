LAST DAYS

BRIAN EVENSON

A religious sect that practices self-amputation hires a private investigator to find their missing leader. Why choose him? It may have something to do with his own forays into self-mutilation. The best part of this hard-boiled horror mystery is the unexpected humor nestled within the gruesome details.

—Andrew, Prince Street

ELECTRIC SHAMANS AT THE FESTIVAL OF THE SUN
MÓNICA OJEDA, TRANS. SARAH BOOKER

No one else can write from multiple quirky one-of-a-kind weirdo perspectives like Mónica Ojeda (expertly translated by Sarah Booker). Her writing is always challenging, always teetering on the edge of madness, always surprising. I'm definitely not up for the IRL festival but taking the wild trip from the comfort of my own couch? A demented delight!

—Jess, Williamsburg

THE MAN IN THE WATCH STILL VISITS ME AT NIGHT

JOSHUA CHAPLINSKY

Chaplinsky has become a master architect of the horror genre—proving not only that he’s capable of building skyscraper-sized scares, but more impressively demonstrating the terrors of structural collapse. As the lives of three women in his newest novel overlap inside the infamous Vogel house, you’ll find that this haunted house may actually be his tallest skyscraper yet.

—Hannah A., Rockefeller

It’s a tale as old as time. Boy meets girl. Girl is a writer; Boy is a writer, too. Boy and Girl fall in love. Boy and Girl read books, write about books for big name publications, and buy books from McNally Jackson. And eventually, Boy and Girl are named the Rewarded Readers of the Month for October.

Meet Will Gottsegen and Greta Rainbow, cherished SoHo shoppers and handsome couple. Last week, they popped by to talk about to us about book parties, Sebald second chances, and which one of them is going to read Yesteryear.

— Jack

McNally Jackson: How'd you two meet?

Greta Rainbow: Technically, we met on the beach at Rockaway, at my 25th birthday party. Mutual friends brought him. Later, when I got a gig writing for a web3 company, I asked Will, a longtime crypto reporter, out to lunch to ask him how one writes about things that aren’t real. No comment on if I had ulterior, nonprofessional motives, but that contract was short-lived while the relationship is not.

McJ: Do you have a favorite New York book?

Will Gottsegen: How New Yorky does a book have to be to be a New York book? I’ll pick Underworld, which I’m halfway through right now and which is clearly a great New York-ish book.

GR: A Far Rockaway of the Heart by Lawrence Ferlinghetti is dear to me. The Odd Woman and the City (by Vivian Gornick). Walking Through Clear Water in a Pool Painted Black (by Cookie Mueller). 

McJ: What books do you guys disagree on?

WG: Austerlitz. I am a Sebald obsessive, and I think when Greta tried starting this one and it just didn’t click. I bet she’ll return! She’s good at knowing whether I’ll enjoy something before I actually start it, which tends to mean that I avoid books that she loves and she thinks I won’t like.

GR: It’s a bit of a gender issue, but I have a higher tolerance for fiction veering into the realms of fluff or frivolity, and for writing that references or replicates the internet and social media. I’m the kind of reader who is going to see what the hype is all about with Yesteryear, and Will is not. I had a big Tao Lin phase and I don’t think he ever did. He also didn’t put himself through a season of cozies.

McJ: Greta, you’re from Seattle. Do you have a favorite Seattle book?

GR: Lindy West was a cultural moment and you had to be there. I wanted to work at The Stranger, Seattle’s alt-weekly, when I grew up! Before I moved to Montreal, my parents gave me This Boy’s Life by Tobias Wolff. Expanding to the PNW, on my to-read list is Nobody Broke Your Heart: An Intimate Biography of Elliott Smith. I’m a little nervous because I love him so much.

McJ: Will, your office is a few floors above us right now. Have you ever discovered a book here on your lunch break?

WG: I have! Greta’s dad recommended Angel Down by Daniel Kraus; I picked it up during my mid-day wanderings.

McJ: You’re both writers. Besides each other, who are some other young writers you’re excited about?

WG: I’ll be selfish and name a pal, Daniel Kolitz, who is beginning work on a book that is going to be amazing.

GR: I’m bullish on Stephanie Wambugu (who referenced McNally in my interview with her).

McJ: What makes a good book party?

GR: An open bar. The most interesting people are going to be the broke assistants and random plus-ones and you’ve got to throw them a bone. On the other hand, one that’s more realistic about publishing budgets, it just needs to be the thing for the industry, for that one night. Drinks weren’t free at the infamous Canongate party at the London Book Fair last year, where the roof literally caved in. But everyone there was wonderful, exactly who I wanted to see or to meet so it didn’t matter (read: they had company cards). Benedict Cumberbatch was also present. It’s a good idea to get a celebrity.

WG: A moratorium on career talk.

McJ: What’s your favorite thing about the other’s writing? 

GR: Will’s voice—curious, funny, attuned to the most delicate and delightful details—always comes through, even in nitty gritty political commentary or tech world insider baseball. He sees humanity everywhere. He is also good at cutting the fat (and would say this last line is unnecessary) and I’m so lucky to have him as a personal editor.

WG: I love that her writing is totally unafraid.
