VIRGINA

BY SHELBY SHAW
OCTOBER 23, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Because her breasts would obscure the image that 16-year-old Judy Garland was playing the part of pre-adolescent Dorothy in The Wizard of Oz (1939), she was forced to tightly wrap her chest beneath her clothing while filming. The sight—or rather, shape—of breasts is so commonly used to quickly gender and sexualize women that audiences would not have believed a pre-teen girl could have developed early or fully without first seeing her as a mature woman rather than as an inexperienced child. This sight would have distracted from the wholesome fantasy of the virginal good girl discovering that there's no place like home, but being at home never historically guaranteed anyone a happy ending.

In Srđan Karanović's Virgina, home is a place where girls are raised to be bosomy virgins until marriage, unless they’re raised to be sworn virgins for life: socially accepted “men” who were secretly assigned female at birth, but can now claim to be a male heir for their family. A Yugoslavian film, Virgina was released in 1991 when the country began to dissolve and fracture into various states; a year later, Yugoslavia no longer existed, but the practice of sworn virgins is believed to still continue today.

The 19th-century Balkan villagers in Virgina tend to their poverty by farming whatever they can on their bleak mountainous lands. They live in a deeply patriarchal society and are fearfully superstitious of straying from their traditions, particularly the belief that bearing a son is essential for having a good life and a good soul, according to the folklore associated with their patron saint, St. George; bearing nothing but daughters, in turn, represents a humiliating curse. Timotije (Miodrag Krivokapić) is one such cursed man, so when his wife Dostana (Ina Gogálová) gives birth to their fourth consecutive daughter, they agree to desperate measures and swear that this fourth girl will be raised as a virgina, naming her Stevan and using only he/him pronouns. “The unfortunate one got a son” the townspeople cry at the celebration of Stevan’s birth. “We thought that God had abandoned you,” a woman tells Dostana with relief for her finally bearing a son. The neighbors leave bountiful gifts of milk, bread, vegetables, and cheese. The starving family of daughters, ignored by the gods, now feasts with the presence of a male heir among them.

Stevan (Marta Keler) grows into adolescence, receiving the priority for food among his family members and privileges forbidden to his sisters and mother. He is taught to chop wood with his father, to expect respect from women, and to carry and handle his own gun. His lanky body and short hair look just like the other boys' traditional attire in the village. He knows that he is a girl like his sisters under their dresses, and although they know it too, the family must keep this secret or risk being shunned by their community and punished by God. Stevan was not asked to be raised as a son, nor did he volunteer to martyr his choice of gender, identity, or expression for the sake of saving his family from ridicule and misfortune. As his breasts begin to grow under the fabric Dostana tightly wraps around his chest each morning in order to hide them, so too grows Stevan’s hunger to discover who and what he is so as to not have to hide himself: a son known by his family and bound to live a life of male celibacy, or a non-existent daughter ungrateful for being made into a secret?

Virgina screens this evening, October 23, and until October 25, at Metrograph as part of the series "Jenni Olson Selects: Mini Masc." Olson will introduce and hold a post-screening discussion after this evening’s screening and introduce the screening on Saturday, October 24th.
