The 17th Annual Brooklyn Folk Festival is almost here! 
 

"DIVERSE, SPONTANEOUS, COUNTERCULTURAL, AND TRENCHANT... ONE OF THE MOST VITAL AND SPIRITUALLY SOUND FOLK-MUSIC EVENTS IN THE COUNTRY"

- AMANDA PETRUSICH, THE NEW YORKER MAGAZINE

This year’s festival will be headlined by the iconic freak folk artist Bonnie “Prince” Billy, The Late Show with Stephen Colbert bandleader and Louis Cato, and longtime Brooklyn Folk Festival favorites The Deslondes.

The full festival lineup showcases artists and folk traditions from around the world, including Gamelan, Son Jarocho, Rumeli, and Cumbia music, alongside workshops where festival attendees can learn about a wide range of topics including African Origins of the Banjo and Songs of the Delmore Brothers.

Hear a playlist of artists performing at the festival.

As part of Jalopy’s 20th anniversary celebration, Saturday evening’s program will feature a special performance by the puppet company Boxcutter Collective, followed by a set from the bluegrass trio of Michael Daves, Bruce Molsky, and Tony Trischka.

For us at Jalopy, the festival represents not only the best of what we do from a programming perspective, but also an opportunity to bring our community together. Each year, we look forward to reuniting with old friends and artists who have passed through Jalopy over the years and helped make it what it is today, while welcoming new faces into the fold.

A Discussion on the Mayrent Collection

Jalopy is so proud to help facilitate the Mayrent Collection of Yiddish Recordings- it is one of the world's largest collections of early Yiddish music recordings. It was assembled by musician and collector Sherry Mayrent and is housed at the University of Wisconsin–Madison. The collection is particularly significant for preserving the sounds of Yiddish popular music, klezmer, theater songs, cantorial performances, and other Jewish musical traditions from the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

The collection contains more than 9,000 historical recordings, primarily on 78-rpm records, documenting Jewish musical life across the United States, Eastern Europe, Latin America, South Africa, and Israel. Many date from approximately 1895–1955.

Thank you to Sherry Mayrent, Christopher King, Tom Caw and Christina Crowder for sitting down with our Executive Director, Lynette Wood, to discuss this incredible work.

In celebration of our 20th anniversary, I’ll be sitting down with some of the people who have helped make Jalopy what it is today. This month's interview is with Lynette Wood, our Co-Founder and Executive Director. 

In this interview, Lynette tells the Jalopy origin story, shares some of the highlights and lowlights of the last twenty years, and reflects on watching the Jalopy's community grow and evolve alongside her:

HLT: When was the first inkling of the idea to start Jalopy?

LW: So, when we moved to Chicago, we had a really cute apartment- it had a backyard and we had control of the garage... We were meeting people, and we wound up with a couple people playing music...so art and music was always happening in this garage, and I would cook dinner for people; it was fun. And then we moved. We noticed after a few months that we're not doing that anymore, we're just meeting up at bars. Why? Well, we didn't have that dedicated space. Not that we didn't have enough space, it's just something about that space was- you went there after your workday to make art. And without that space, you weren't making art as much. So Geoff and I started a long conversation, and he had a lot of ideas around that. We talked and talked for a number of years, and thought about what it would look like?  I kept telling him he had to write a business plan. The business plan was basically made over hundreds of dinners over three years.

We actively started looking for places to open Jalopy, and we looked in Portland, Oregon, Portland, Maine, Portugal, in Lisbon.  Then Geoff went to a lutherie program, and the guy he was working with said "have you ever heard of Red Hook Brooklyn?" We flew out and rented a car and bought (the building for) Jalopy the same day.

HLT: Why Red Hook? 

LW: Well, what's… what's sort of the scariest, the hardest place to make art happen? Let's try New York. Peering in the windows of Sunny's Bar, because they were closed that night, that was a big determining factor. You could feel the aura coming off the building. We thought, "okay, I could be neighbors with these people."

HLT: What was the process of building the place out like?

LW: Geoff, he knows all the construction, he knows how to make all that stuff happen. I was really under the impression that I was gonna get the place started and help build them out and go back to work. But…we just started working. I'm not a builder, so I was a sander. I sanded everything in Jalopy, and Geoff designed and built it all. 

The health department came to give us our initial inspection before we opened, and Geoff and I were finishing sanding the bathroom door on sawhorses, so if you look back, you can see our first health department report says, ‘no bathroom door.’ Obviously, there's a bathroom door. You're just 20 minutes early for when it will be attached to the wall.

HLT: How did you decide you were going to stay on and run the place?

LW: It's hard for me to look back and remember what we were so busy with at first, because it took awhile to start doing five days a week or noon till midnight with shows. But recruiting teachers and getting things advertised- I guess it takes a lot to get all those foundations put in. Then all of a sudden, I found that I was loving the community part. And I still had every intention of going to find a political campaign to work on or doing some community organizing. I just slowly realized, this is community organizing. This is what I was destined to do, and what I've wanted to do and done in the past. 

Geoff always joked that he wanted to build a place where I would have to work with him, so that we could be together all the time- I thought that was sort of a silly idea. But it turned out to be true, and it was really fun that the two of us had this… bizarre life together. It really wasn't that long before you could see Jalopy in the context of a neighborhood that was so vibrant and cool, and the other people in Red Hook that were trying to make art and do business. That camaraderie was very inspiring, and we were all full of ideas of how we could do more for the neighborhood. Someone put up a picture of Geoff and I and it said, "saving the world, one beer at a time." 

HLT: I heard a rumor that the first actual event hosted at Jalopy was your wedding? 

LW: Geoff and I had been together for about 11 years when we got to New York, and, for a host of reasons, we figured it would be good to do something substantial to kick off the space. So, while we were trying to figure out how to open a business and build everything out, we decided that we should probably get married, too. Then we'd start whatever the rest of the history of the place would be. Luckily, we found a really nice friend in the neighborhood who knew how to do such things, because I really gave it almost no thought. Geoff designed a dress, and we found a dressmaker who found beautiful antique Japanese silk and made a dress for me. He designed the ring as well and found a jewelry maker that did that part of it. We got married on July 15th, and we had our first concert in August, and our classes started that September. So it was a nice boom, boom, boom.

HLT: You've put so much time and energy into facilitating this community that, revolves around music in many ways. You yourself are not a musician, at least not that I know of, though I wouldn't be surprised. Can you share a little about your connection with music? 

LW: I had a teacher, in 7th grade who taught poetry through Bob Dylan, and it was exactly at the time where I was coming of age and realizing how completely messed up the world was. I mean, my parents were good people, but they surely weren't activists in any way. The combination of hearing that music for the first time, and hearing ways to protest that spoke to me was totally foundational, in making me who I am. I'm not someone that wants to scream and yell in someone's face, but if something is unjust or wrong, I want to be clever about how I tell people to change their ways. That's where the folk music part comes for me. I just was always around musicians and always looking for music to speak to power in the right way. So yeah, that's sort of how that happened.

HLT: Was it always intended to be like a folk and traditional music venue?

LW: Yeah, absolutely. And we were so lucky, coming into Red Hook. There was a great, excellent bar called Lily's that was down in... to say no man's land is generous. Now it's across from IKEA, but at that time, you know, no one was there. But that's where we met everybody. Jan Bell was one of the first people that we ever met. Drove her home, and…been friends for 20 years. Mamie Minch was one of the first people that we met. I like to tell her that the Roulette Sisters are literally in the foundation, because we listened to them almost every day while we were sanding and building. So that was so key in getting in with like-minded people from the get-go… we were so lucky to come upon Lily's.

HLT: Obviously, this is a sort of an impossible question, but over 20 years, what are some of your favorite memories, shows, moments?

LW: I always think back to our first sold-out show, because we didn't really know what that was gonna be like, and it was with some people we met on the subway who turned out to be the Sweetback Sisters- still lifelong friends. We didn't have a beer and wine license at the time, so I was busy making cappuccinos for this show in December, and I didn't really realize how many people were there. All of a sudden, there were 125 people in the place and I'd made 100 cappuccinos. I thought- this is wrong. This is dangerous. We can't do this. So that's always pretty foundational.

And I like to remember, again, that community piece of it… when things got busy, we had a neighbor around the corner who seemed to know whenever it was going to be too busy, and he would run in and chill beer and start helping me serve people, so that was cool. We had these artists that were willing to tell their friends to come down to this place that nobody had ever heard of- the respect I had for them, for taking a chance on us. In most cases, they were people who are still foundational to our programming monthly, annually.

I will always remember Feral Foster looking at me askance, and not trusting me. That was… that was a good thing. Having (Roots n’) Ruckus come (to Jalopy) and having all these really fun young people start showing up, that was, again, that moment of, like, okay, more real community in these different ways. Watching the friendships made in classes- that always is really special. I don't have the roster anymore of who was signed up for the first sets of classes, but a good percentage of them still come. So those are highlights.

The first folk festival, I don't remember at all. The second one, I had to sit outside with a clipboard and a phone. And it was the first time I realized, I'm not gonna see any of this show, because we had such a waiting list that I just had to sit there and check people in, and when someone left, check somebody in. Then that became the story for 15 years of me never actually getting to see the Folk Fest, but the excitement of it was really cool. Just the cast of characters, feeling like summer camp every folk festival, because, like, who's coming back? Oh, who's gonna sleep on whose floor? What kind of shenanigans are gonna happen? 

Not to be too cheesy, but watching people grow up from, like, you and Nora (Brown), and Jackson (Lynch), my goodness. Watching their music grow up, too. That trajectory is so awesome. Then watching the place learn to survive without it being Geoff and I 24 hours a day. Having kids was great for stabilizing the organization, because we couldn't be there, and we had to figure out how to make it work.

So those are good memories. 

HLT: That's a lot of good memories.

LW: Yeah.

HLT: You know, you've done something that I admire so much, you and Geoff, you've definitely built something that is bigger than yourselves, and has impacted so many people positively. I think Eli said this at the gala last year, like, it's the third space for so many people, and has been throughout the years. Looking forward, what are ways that you want to see Jalopy grow, or change, or, you know, if money's no object, what are the things that you would do more of?

LW: Communal learning and friendship building. I think people are even more in need of it than a decade ago. It's great that we added one more classroom. When I think of where I want to see Jalopy go, I want these amazing artists that have spent their lives learning how to perform to be able to make a nice chunk of change, to help people connect with each other, and with an instrument, and with a passion that they want to build. That requires space. Whether that's partnering with a lot of other organizations across the city to offer more classes, or having more of our own space. That's at least half of the future for us. We've added so many different classes that we've never done before just in the last year. How many others are there? And how many other cool artists want to share with people? That's totally exciting to me. So that's one thing. Another would be more partnerships with cultural organizations; they're so fun. We giggle when we get to book them. It's so exciting.

I think that our pipeline for emerging musicians is really good. The record label does a lot for artists. Could we do more? You know, it's the same with the Community Through Music program. Could we be in 10 schools? And as gentrification happens across Brooklyn, maybe we need to be in Coney Island, or maybe we need to be in different parts of Brooklyn.

To do what we're doing, and have it resonate with the students who have the least access to music. So I guess that would be it.

HLT: In a similar vein, like, what are some of the surprises or challenges you’ve encountered running Jalopy?

LW: I call it a confirmation, like. Other than this robbery we just had, and one right after… (Hurricane) Sandy? Nothing's ever gone missing. Nothing's ever been stolen. I think that you actually can create a sense… of well-being that transfers to the people while they're in a place… There's something about really being communal that makes people at their best, and good to each other. It's sort of ridiculous to leave work, and say goodbye, and I love you, to all your coworkers, and a good share of customers!

HLT: And mean it.

LW: Yeah, I mean it! Like, what the… that is crazy! It's very cool. Trying to eke out a living for art in this world is not cool. It is not cool to not be able to pay people enough.
And as you professionalize, where can you find more money to do more things? You gotta take more from the artists who aren't making enough? That's stupid.

My responsibility as the ED is to go and find other ways for the place to make money, so that everybody can continue to go up. That's a struggle, and we're doing better at it, but that's like a societal failing.

I sometimes wonder- did I screw all these people over by making them stick around here? I want our artists to be, I don't know, on a beach in paradise with, like, crowds of fans around them. I sure wouldn't be as happy without all of you around, but… I hope I didn't damage anybody's future by making them part of a crazy collective.

HLT: I blame Eli Smith for ruining my life, not you per se.

LW: Good, good, good, he… he deserves that, then.

HLT: You touched on something that Feral did say was the biggest challenge of Jalopy, which is just you're working with people you love and people you're very close with adds an intensity to everything.

LW: It's a true statement, and I think that it also puts everybody at their best, because they're not just responsible to themselves and their performance at a job, they're responsible to each other. And that's awesome. And yes, totally stressful, too.

HLT:I think we have, we've made it through my list here. It’s nice to zoom out- you've accomplished exactly what you set out to do when it comes to making a space for art and community. Jalopy is the thing in my life that really has given me a sense of community and how to actively participate in a healthy way. So thank you for that. But I can let you go on with your Friday evening. Thank you for doing this. 

LW: That is fantastic. Yeah, and I think I'm gonna…go back to work that I shouldn't be prioritizing instead of the stuff I should.
