THE WATCHER IN THE ATTIC

By ETHAN SPIGLAND
NOVEMBER 21, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Stricken with ennui, Saburo Goda (Renji Ishibashi), a tenant in a tawdry boarding house in Tokyo’s Asakusa district, discovers he can spy on his neighbors by scurrying along the wooden beams in the attic above their rooms and peeping through holes in the floorboards. Shafts of hazy light give the shadowy attic the look of a projection booth, and each room below is a small theater where unsettling erotic fantasies play out. In one, Lady Minako (Junko Miyashita), a wealthy married woman who secretly rents a room in the house, plays sexual games with a submissive yet nimble circus Pierrot dressed in white with red polka dots.

Noboru Tanaka’s The Watcher in the Attic (1976) screens in a rare 35mm print as part of Japan Society’s “Nikkatu’s Golden Age of Eros,” which spotlights a run of softcore films in the studio’s “Roman Porno” series produced in the ‘70s and ‘80s. The Watcher in the Attic adapts the stories “The Stalker in the Attic” and “The Human Chair” by author Edogawa Rampo, Japan’s foremost literary master of the perverse. Rampo’s fascination with the grotesque and the absurd helped shape the interwar literary movement known as ero guro nansensu (“erotic grotesque nonsense”), and Tanaka gives these preoccupations a distinctly Taisho flavor. The boarding house is a riot of Japanese and European bric-a-brac, and in one elegant restaurant with stained-glass windows, Minako’s husband orders roasted frog for the two of them in an effort to revive his faltering libido.

Early on, Minako, played by Roman Porno goddess Miyashita, realizes Goda is watching her and looks straight back at him through the aperture. Her gaze punctures an illusion of invisibility that Goda shares with the cinema spectator. Being seen only heightens his desire, while knowing she is being watched spurs Minako to perform for him. Here, looking becomes a circuit of mutual fantasy. Tanaka frames bodies through doorways and tiny peepholes, and often cuts to close-ups of the voyeur’s eye. At one point, he zooms in on the jeweled eyes of an Art Nouveau snail painted on the ceiling of Minako’s room. From below, she directs the mise-en-scène, making Goda’s seemingly privileged vantage point serve her boundless pleasure.

When Goda watches Minako strangle the Pierrot with her vise-like legs during their sexual play, he sets out to prove himself her equal. He drips poison through the peephole and into the open mouth of the priest sleeping below, acting on the scene he once could only watch. Goda and Minako become witnesses to each other’s murders, and the spectator, confined to looking, is made complicit.

Outside the boarding house, patriotic parades reflect Japan’s growing militarism. Oblivious to the spectacle, Goda anvd Minako remain absorbed in their voyeuristic games. Late in the film, they have sex at last in the eponymous dark attic. Their lovemaking coincides with, and perhaps precipitates, the Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923. The architecture that structured their desire crashes down around them. Does the collapse of the distance between watcher and watched end their game of looking or bring it to its culmination? In the end, Tanaka leaves us with a servant pumping water amid ruins, the clear stream gradually turning deep crimson.

The Watcher in the Attic screens this evening, November 22, at Japan Society on 35mm as part of the series “Nikkatsu’s Golden Age of Eros.”
