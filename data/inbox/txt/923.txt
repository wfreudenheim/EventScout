STILL A BROTHER: INSIDE THE NEGRO MIDDLE CLASS

BY AARON CUTLER
OCTOBER 24, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

William Greaves is an amazing, undervalued filmmaker in the history of American film. More so than perhaps any other filmmaker, the late Harlem-born actor, writer, and director (whose centenary has been celebrated with film series in cities including Lisbon, London, and now New York) represents an ideal of the Black American intellectual, akin to innovators from diverse fields such as Langston Hughes, Booker T. Washington, and W.E.B. Du Bois—all of whom are invoked in various ways within Greaves’s polyphonic films. Regardless of whether the films are set at a pioneering arts festival in Dakar (The First World Festival of Negro Arts, 1966), the National Black Political Convention held in Gary, Indiana (Nationtime, 1972), or a social gathering for elderly Harlem Renaissance legends held at Duke Ellington’s brownstone (Once Upon a Time in Harlem, shot in 1972 and completed by William’s son, David Greaves, in 2026), they mix documentary interviews with reenactments to generate dynamic, oft-clashing dialogues about what it means to be Black. The very choice of which term those concerned should use to describe themselves—“African-American,” “Black,” “colored,” or “Negro,” with its hurtful historical connotations—often comes up as a point of contention.

Greaves’s films, with few exceptions—such as 1971’s independently produced film about filmmaking Symbiopsychotaxiplasm: Take One—were made as commissioned works to which he brought an authorial vision. Such was the case with the feature-length documentary Still a Brother: Inside the Negro Middle Class (1968), which was written by the playwright William B. Branch and commissioned by the American broadcast network NET (National Educational Television). The film first aired less than a month after Martin Luther King, Jr.’s death, and its use of the actor and Civil Rights activist Ossie Davis as a narrator inevitably recalls the earlier death of Malcolm X, whose eulogy Davis delivered. In the aftermath of these leaders’ traumatic losses—along with other violent instances to which the film refers, such as the Newark riots of 1967— Greaves mounts a state-of-the-moment exposé about a group struggling, day by day, to pave for itself the smoothest possible path through American society.

The group in question is the five million-member Black American middle-class, which the film calls “a veritable society within a society” and whose march toward respectability is positioned precariously between the refinement sought by the upper classes and the urge for survival that drives the lower ones. Greaves shows scenes of cotillions, summer vacations, dinner parties, and people going to work at corporate offices with the knowledge that what they have built could be quickly taken away by a society into which full assimilation is impossible, since, per the film’s narrator, “You can always see the Negro. He wears his badge of color.” Greaves also includes interviews about the problems facing this class with a variety of African-American men in public leadership positions, such as the Episcopal reverend Lorentho Wooden, the JET Magazine editor Robert Johnson, and the political activist Bayard Rustin, who collectively express hope for the middle-class’s security and stability while retaining varying degrees of skepticism.

The film’s title comes from a conversation with an Urban League leader named Horace Morris who recalls feeling a sense of recognition after seeing relatives wounded indiscriminately by police violence in Newark: “When it gets down to the nitty-gritty, right down to where it really matters, you’re still a Negro, and you’re still identified with every other Negro in America, be he in a ghetto or in a suburban neighborhood. You’re still a brother.”

Still a Brother: Inside the Black Middle Class screens this afternoon, October 24, and on October 27 and 28, at Film Forum as part of the series “William Greaves 100”. It will be preceded by Greaves’s medium-length film In the Company of Men.
