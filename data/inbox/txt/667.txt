Taryn Simon’s “Father Country I Do Love You”

Saul Ostrow

Taryn Simon, One little room on fire (detail), 2026. Archival inkjet print and two text panels in acrylic frames; print: 148.6 × 191.6 × 7 cm; text panels: 46.7 × 31.4 × 4 cm and 37.8 × 12.4 × 4 cm. © 2026 Taryn Simon.

Review

Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum, New York

Taryn Simon’s “Father Country I Do Love You” traces the life of a single unnamed federal agent across forty-two multipart chapters, consisting of photographic arrangements, composite images, videos, sculptures, and extensive explanatory texts. The chapters follow a chain of contingent associations and inadvertent events, as disparate as the sting operation that led to the arrest of the civil rights activist Marion Barry and the 1998 US embassy bombings in Nairobi. These associations make the agent less a sovereign subject than a conduit for events, information, and their aftereffects. Simon’s fractured structure refuses a single explanatory account; its focus is instead the uneven relation between an individual life and the bureaucratic, familial, political, and historical systems through which it acquires legibility.

The agent’s career spans a period in which the documentary apparatus changes radically: files, testimony, and analog photographs yield to databases, digital surveillance, networked archives, and AI-generated imagery. This is not a biography supplemented by a history of media but a double narrative in which each conditions the other. The agent moves through institutions as collector, handler, witness, and intermediary, while documentary technologies recast what can count as evidence, what can be retrieved, and what can be believed. Simon’s images of innocuous people, places, and things, long explanatory texts, objects, and video are not simply a treatment of one man’s archive; they are an analogy, staged at the scale of the rotunda, for what it means to know through an accumulation of records whose relations are never established in advance.

The agent is a middleman: someone who witnesses events, gathers fragments, transmits information, and leaves behind material whose meaning will be assembled elsewhere by others. The Guggenheim’s spiral makes this accumulation of information bodily. It turns the archive into a route, obliging the viewer to move through its chapters while never granting a position from which the whole can be mastered. This invites comparison with Tino Sehgal’s 2010 Guggenheim presentation of Kiss and This Progress, which used only the rotunda, voice, movement, and conversation as its media. Simon and Sehgal approach a related problem from opposite directions. Sehgal subtracts mediation; Simon multiplies it. Sehgal leaves us with an encounter between bodies, refusing the photograph, caption, catalog, and durable objects. The experience cannot be exported into another medium or checked against a record; the narrative and memory is what remains. Simon does the reverse, multiplying photographs, recordings, texts, sculptures, archival traces, screens, and spatial relations until mediation becomes impossible to overlook. Where Sehgal makes presence his medium, Simon makes mediation the condition through which presence is encountered.

The serial arrangement of the chapters through the rotunda also gives this work something of the structure of a film spatially dispersed. Episodes acquire meaning through their relation to what comes before and after. This contrast sharpens in comparison with the documentary filmmaker Ken Burns, whose mediation is similar to Simon’s. Both give the still photographs duration, but  by different means and to different ends. Burns’s slow pans and extended holds generally serve to sequence, stabilizing archival material within an explanatory narrative, smoothing historical discontinuity into continuity. Simon’s serial stills and composites do the opposite. Looking becomes comparing or puzzling; the photograph is no longer simply what it records, because other images have become part of what each photograph is.

Where Burns often seeks to produce a psychological condition, Simon produces the condition of contemporary information: abundance without sequence, proximity without continuity, fragments whose meaning shifts according to adjacency. Her story is panoramic; its experience is discontinuous. The work does not merely represent a world in which information has become excessive, unstable, and managed, but whose proliferation makes that condition difficult to step outside of.

Simon’s third register is Walter Benjamin. His claim was not merely that mechanical reproduction changes what counts as an original; but more importantly technologies reorganize the sensorium itself—attention, memory, distraction, proximity, shock, and the relation between the individual and world. Photography and film generate habits of seeing. Simon’s installation does not illustrate this proposition so much as work through it. History becomes legible not as a sequence of events but through the juxtaposition of separated images, objects, voices, and documents, each returning the others to another register.

In Simon’s installation, language mediates everything, and this is not always to its credit. The images are not self-explanatory, which returns the viewer to Benjamin’s insistence that photographs require captions to become legible. Simon’s signage, however, does not meet its site’s demand: it is ill-considered, drafted with little regard for the public that has to navigate it. Likewise what passes here for multimedia is often juxtaposition—one medium set beside another rather than made to work through it. The experience, for long stretches, is a stubbornly linear one, in which the viewer waits to gain access to the explanatory texts. So one looks at the work installed and then waits their turn to read a wall text in twelve-point type, to register the backstory of a given installation, only to move on to the next station until one gives up and strolls.

The exception to the text dilemma, and perhaps the most engaging single part of the show, is a site-specific channel running along the embankment of the ramp, down which marbles descend in a pattern that appears random, terminating in a Skee-Ball-like well where they accumulate. Their sound precedes them. As one continues up the ramp, one discovers that they originate in a large cabinet serving two purposes: a hand crank permits visitors to release the marbles one by one, while the cabinet also houses a video of a man, perhaps Simon’s father who we are told suffered from Alzheimer’s, presents 35 mm slides to the camera, narrating a rambling demented monologue. This piece comes closer to what the rest of the installation only gestures toward. What the signage and wall text attempt to do, the cabinet performs; where language elsewhere supplies an image’s meaning, here the mechanism is an analogy. The viewer, the site, and the exhibition’s structure enter the system the installation describes. Participation is no longer something added to the work. The viewer is already inside the machinery through which the work becomes legible. They are part of its systems.

Saul Ostrow is a critic and curator who has organized more than eighty exhibitions across the United States and Europe. He co-founded Critical Practices Inc. (CPI) and is Art Editor at Lodge for BOMB Magazine. His writings have appeared internationally in magazines, journals, exhibition catalogs, and anthologies.
