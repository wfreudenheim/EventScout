EVERY A-SIDE HAS A B-SIDE
WITH RALPH LEMON, SOL CABRINI, TAJA CHEEK, JONATHAN GONZÁLEZ, NILE HARRIS, YUNIYA EDI KWON & T. LAX

November 13, 2026
Doors at 7 p.m., event at 8 p.m.
Roulette
509 Atlantic Ave.
Brooklyn, NY 11217
Free with RSVP

“Live performance art is some kind of canary in the coal mine,” wrote Pope.L, lamenting the institutionalization of “a form that lives and dies on liveness.” This trajectory—and the tension between performance and property, objects and bodies—is central to On Value (2015), a book edited by Triple Canopy and Ralph Lemon. Reflecting on the efforts of institutions to collect, codify, and reproduce performance, On Value considers the particular experience—as old as the commercialization and circulation of Black art in the United States—of being valued as a source of entertainment or art, transcendence or truth, while being discounted as a person.

For this year’s symposium, and in advance of the publication of a second expanded edition in early 2027, we invited the artists, writers, and performers Sol Cabrini, Taja Cheek (L’Rain), Jonathan González, Nile Harris, yuniya edi kwon, and T. Lax to channel the book’s concerns into a variety of performances and readings. Lemon will introduce the event.

GHOSTED, TOO
WITH MORGAN BASSICHIS

November 14, 2026
Doors at 7:30 p.m., event at 8 p.m.
Roulette
509 Atlantic Ave.
Brooklyn, NY 11217
Free with RSVP

Morgan Bassichis has spent several years reanimating the legacies of queer and Jewish icons through neurotic stand-up comedy, incantatory songs, and historical excavations. In 2017, Bassichis began adapting Larry Mitchell’s cult book The Faggots & Their Friends Between Revolutions (1977) for performance and facilitating its reissue. Over the past few years, Bassichis turned to the late comedian and performance artist Frank Maya in their acclaimed off-Broadway solo show Can I Be Frank?, which won two Obie Awards. Next year, Bassichis will take on the Amy Winehouse songbook.

For the symposium’s second night at Roulette, Bassichis will deliver a talk on their compulsion to commune with the dead. “Adoration has a way of keeping the deceased at least a little bit alive,” Bassichis writes. “I will sometimes refer to them as ghosts, but a bit reluctantly, because that is a term I have only ever heard from the living.” Considering ghosts as manifestations of “unfinished business,” Bassichis will offer provisional reflections on performance as a mode of necromancy, or “visitationship.”
