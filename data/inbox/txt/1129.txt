ZORN II

AMALRIC <–> ZORN
November 13 – 25
This series focuses on the collaboration between two figures who are vitally important within their respective art forms, and close to our hearts here at Anthology: filmmaker and actor Mathieu Amalric and composer, conductor, and musician John Zorn.

For those who have been engaged with cinema and music over the past fifty years, neither of these artists require an introduction. Amalric has graced the screen since the mid-1980s, in films by everyone from Otar Iosseliani, Arnaud Desplechin, Raúl Ruiz, Alain Resnais, and Kiyoshi Kurosawa, to Julian Schnabel, David Cronenberg, and Guy Maddin; while the quietly astonishing films he has directed himself – including THE BLUE ROOM, BARBARA, and his recent masterpiece, HOLD ME TIGHT – have proven him to be not only a collaborator of these artists but their peer. John Zorn, for his part, has had a truly immeasurable impact on NYC culture, thanks above all to his surpassingly dynamic, genre-defying music, but also to his extraordinary visual art (underappreciated until a revelatory 2025 exhibition at the Drawing Center) and his transformative work as a producer, organizer, and creative catalyst.

As acclaimed as Amalric and Zorn’s work has been, their ongoing collaboration hasn’t been properly celebrated in the U.S. Upon meeting in 2008, the two formed an immediate and sustained bond, which has (so far) resulted in three separate documentaries. These films, directed by Amalric between 2010-22, are at once fond and in-depth portraits of a particular artist; utterly transfixing investigations into the process of composing, conducting, and performing; and rapturous meditations on the nature of music and collaboration (between Zorn and his various creative partners, especially – in ZORN III – the singer and conductor Barbara Hannigan).

Anthology has long been devoted to the work of both Amalric (who we hosted for a retrospective in 2015) and Zorn (who has been dedicated to Anthology throughout its existence, and who we’re proud to count as our Composer-in-Residence) – so it’s high time we organized screenings of the films they’ve made together. Alongside this enthralling and invaluable trilogy, we’ll be screening a selection of Amalric’s other music- or performance-themed films, including his experimental biopic BARBARA (2017), three additional films made with Barbara Hannigan, and MAÎTRES ANCIENS – COMÉDIE (2022), his filmic document of a theater piece adapted from a novel by the great Austrian writer Thomas Bernhard.

Mathieu Amalric will be here for selected screenings, and will be joined by John Zorn for the screening of ZORN I + II on Sat, Nov 14 at 5:45!

This series is presented with the generous support of Villa Albertine and Unifrance & LaScam.

Mathieu Amalric
ZORN I (2010-2016)
2017, 54 min, DCP
“The tense, but smiling face of maverick composer legend John Zorn listening to music might just restore your faith in human beauty. Every muscle moves in harmony with sounds and chords, it’s like seeing a soul wandering off into another realm. Mathieu Amalric has been filming this zestful musician for more than 12 years now. They first met when the actor and filmmaker narrated Zorn’s vocal album Shir Hashirim live in Paris. Those familiar with Amalric’s haptic cinema will immediately understand what drew him to Zorn and vice versa. They connect through their constant and obsessive drive for feelings and sensations, their tireless energy devoted to the art, their hunger for life. In the style of a home movie, Amalric accompanies Zorn at concerts, city walks, inside the sound studio or at practice.” –Patrick Holzapfel, VIENNALE

Mathieu Amalric
ZORN II (2016-2018)
2018, 59 min, DCP
“In the second part of his ongoing portrait of the prolific musician, Amalric shows the artist as a social man. Like a sponge, Zorn takes in whatever surrounds him: people (mostly colleagues from the New York Underground scene), dreams, technology, cinema (screening films by Maya Deren or Harry Smith during his performances), places and the emotions evoked by sounds. […] Amalric chooses a more structured approach than in the first part. Text inserts reveal details about the composer’s life and his philosophy. The camera gets more in tune with Zorn’s eruptive movements, as he goes onstage at sold-out concert halls as if he were just discovering the instruments there during an evening stroll. He plays each note as if it was the first or last, and Amalric tries to film him accordingly.” –Patrick Holzapfel, VIENNALE

Total running time: ca. 120 min.
Sat, Nov 14 at 5:45, Sun, Nov 15 at 5:45, Mon, Nov 16 at 6:30, Sat, Nov 21 at 5:00, and Tues, Nov 24 at 7:00. Mathieu Amalric & John Zorn in person on Sat, Nov 14!

ZORN III

Mathieu Amalric
ZORN III (2018-2022)
2022, 82 min, DCP. With John Zorn and Barbara Hannigan.
“Why can’t Mathieu Amalric stop filming John Zorn? It’s because consciously or unconsciously, he understands that he can only get closer to this man if he imitates his restlessness. It’s this Sisyphean tirelessness, the fact that we must imagine Zorn (whose surname translates into German as anger) as a happy man, which is at the core of his art. He makes music in order to be alive. This time, however, Zorn is not the only protagonist, as the Canadian soprano Barbara Hannigan enters the wild world of the composer. Much more straightforward than its previous iterations, the film captures Hannigan’s rehearsals for a performance of Zorn’s song cycle ‘Jumalattaret’ together with pianist Stephen Gosling. The work pushes her vocal capacities to the limit, with the sudden shifts in register, extended high notes and bird-like chirping demanding everything from her.” –Patrick Holzapfel, VIENNALE
Sat, Nov 14 at 8:45, Sun, Nov 15 at 8:45, Mon, Nov 16 at 9:15, Sun, Nov 22 at 5:30, and Wed, Nov 25 at 6:45.

Mathieu Amalric
BARBARA
2017, 98 min, DCP. In French with English subtitles. With Jeanne Balibar, Mathieu Amalric, Vincent Peirani, Aurore Clement, and Grégoire Colin.
Amalric’s seventh feature-length film as a director is both a heartfelt tribute to the legendary chanteuse Barbara (1930-97), and a vehicle for another peerlessly talented French luminary, the actress and singer Jeanne Balibar. Amalric’s films are remarkable for their intuitive freedom, and their emphasis on emotional textures and states of feeling rather than on straightforward narrative construction or easily grasped meanings. BARBARA is a case in point: eschewing anything like a coherent, birth-to-death account of the events of her life, the film instead orchestrates fragments of the singer’s life and performances into a free and quasi-abstract meditation on Barbara’s identity and cultural presence. And by casting Balibar not as Barbara per se but more self-referentially as an actress making a film-within-the-film about the singer, his film becomes an even broader investigation into the concept of biographical filmmaking, representation, and identity. An experimental biopic in the tradition of Stanley Kwan’s CENTER STAGE, Peter Watkins’s EDVARD MUNCH, and Paul Leduc’s FRIDA, it features a typically riveting, charismatic performance from Balibar.
Fri, Nov 13 at 6:30, Sat, Nov 21 at 7:45, and Mon, Nov 23 at 9:15.

HOLD ME TIGHT

Mathieu Amalric
HOLD ME TIGHT / SERRE MOI FORT
2021, 97 min, DCP. In French with English subtitles. With Vicky Krieps, Arieh Worthalter, Anne-Sophie Bowen-Chatet, and Sacha Ardilly.
“Propelled by a fierce, tour-de-force performance by Vicky Krieps, who is fast becoming one of the most sought-after actors on the international scene, HOLD ME TIGHT is an ingenious memory piece exploring the grief of separation and the paradoxical comforts and claustrophobia of the domestic home. As Clarisse flees her family at daybreak and hits the open road, leaving her husband and two children behind, the film shuttles between time and space, becoming a mental landscape whose contours are gradually revealed like fleeting moments of reminiscence – those that ultimately make up a life. With its beguiling, puzzle-piece structure that builds to a shattering denouement, HOLD ME TIGHT powerfully keeps its audience in a state of hypnotic reverie as we share in the protagonist’s profound sense of disorientation, the sort of disembodied surrealism that uncertainty can unleash. Yet Krieps gives a surprisingly earth-bound, visceral performance as a woman negotiating a new path for herself, all while trying to stay close to those she loves and attempting to make sense of it all.” –Andréa Picard, TIFF
Fri, Nov 13 at 9:00 and Mon, Nov 23 at 6:45.

Mathieu Amalric
MUSIC IS MUSIC
2017, 20 min, DCP
“In a poetic composition of song, narration, and images, this film follows Canadian soprano and orchestra conductor Barbara Hannigan, accompanied by the Ludwig Orchestra, as she records her album ‘Crazy Girl Crazy’. Why did she choose these three pieces, in essence so different, but which intimately resonate with her: Berio’s ‘Sequenza III’, Berg’s ‘Lulu Suite’, and Gershwin’s ‘Girl Crazy’?” –INTERNATIONAL FESTIVAL OF FILMS ON ART

Mathieu Amalric
BEFORE IT’S TOO LATE / AVANT QU’IL NE SOIT TROP TARD
2023, 65 min, DCP
“After 47 years together, the Emerson String Quartet records their last album, ‘Infinite Voyage’, in three days. For this farewell, they invited soprano Barbara Hannigan, and a film was born. Their precise work, the moments of enthusiasm and fear, the research and the jokes, the age limit of these men and Hannigan’s flamboyance, the admiring friendship full of irony and rigor, the profession and yet the discovery, humor as a cog towards concentration, the individual and the group…all this composes a portrait of the usually invisible production of music. Before everyone resumes their path.” –DOCLISBOA

Mathieu Amalric
MAÎTRES ANCIENS – COMÉDIE
2022, 80 min, DCP. In French with English subtitles.
“Like Reger – the character [Austrian novelist and playwright] Thomas Bernhard placed on a museum bench facing a painting by Tintoretto, an ‘Old Master’ if ever there was one – an actor finds himself alone and thwarted after a notorious virus forces the indefinite closure of theaters. He wanders through an empty auditorium, paces the now-useless backstage areas, and bursts out into a street haunted by masked passersby. Having persuaded a firefighter to keep watch over him and his explosives, he pours out – to the guard and the surrounding silence alike – his loathing for, among other things, Heideggerians, a lack of punctuality, people who admire rather than respect, and the State’s appropriation of children. At the intersection of cinema and theater, despair and the will to survive, 1980s Austria and 2020s France, Bernhard’s text takes on a new resonance: ‘And as for culture in this country, your stomach exists only to turn.’” –Marie Hermann, FIDMarseille

PROGRAM 1: NATHANIEL DORSKY
CARACOLE (FOR IZCALI) 2023, 17.5 min, 16mm, silent
“In the last few years I have been making a series of cinematic portraits of young filmmaking friends acting as collaborators in portraiture. There is always a surprise as to what the Bolex reveals of a person that is not revealed with the naked eye. This happened in a very strong way with Jacob while shooting PAVANE. But I was not prepared for the surprise that took place when I looked through the camera at Linda. I suddenly saw her as never before and asked without hesitation if she were Aztec. She responded enthusiastically that indeed she was, in fact, her middle name is Izcali, after the important Aztec festival month of rebirth.” –Nathaniel Dorsky

NAOS 2022, 12 min, 16mm, silent
“A Naos is the inner room of an ancient Greek temple in which the image of the deity celebrated by that temple is depicted (usually in the form of a statue of that deity). Jacob, a young filmmaker, offered to help me with some shooting and became the center of the film itself.” –Nathaniel Dorsky

DREAMS REVEAL A WEIGHTLESS WORLD 2016/24, 20 min, 16mm, silent
“OSSUARY was made up of the Kodachrome outs and trims from more than a decade of shooting and making films in Kodachrome. This new film, DREAMS REVEAL A WEIGHTLESS WORLD, is made from the Kodachrome outs from editing OSSUARY… It is the outs of the outs, so to speak.” –Nathaniel Dorsky

PROGRAM 2: JEROME HILER
CARELESS PASSAGE 2024, 20 min, 16mm, silent
“More than ever, origins have been on my mind. In the case of CARELESS PASSAGE, the origins of sight. Particularly, how, after millions of years, our ancestors developed that bubble-like globe that allowed them to see the world. Subsequently, I found that, before this liquid-filled sphere developed, a form of sight existed whereby a small ‘pinhole’ on the surface of an organism allowed a bit of light in to reflect on an interior surface. I had the delightful realization that ‘cinema’ preceded eyesight itself. (I knew it all along). My film, as usual, is a walk around town but haunted by surrogate shapes of ‘bubbles,’ ‘eyes’ and soupy things in emergence. Squares, those uniquely human shapes enter as well and try to find their place in a slippery world.” –Jerome Hiler

WORDS OF MERCURY 2011, 25 min, 16mm, silent
“WORDS OF MERCURY is my loving farewell to color reversal 16mm film. The Bolex camera, which has served me so well for over fifty years, became a container for a process of distillation. I wanted to extract as much as I could from my remaining rolls of Ektachrome 7285. Film emulsion is so rich with latent colors and images, it was a simple decision to make a film of superimpositions which were all shot in the camera. […] The film takes a journey from darkness and a bare world through the seasonal spreading of seeds to a place almost choked and repugnant with color – a place that invites death. The final couplet from Shakespeare’s ‘Love’s Labour’s Lost’ speaking of the place of death, supplies me with my title. I seem to make the same film again and again in different ways.” –Jerome Hiler

NEW SHORES 1971-87/2014, 35 min, 16mm, silent
“Like [IN THE STONE HOUSE], NEW SHORES consists of earlier footage edited in recent years. It could be seen as a sequel to IN THE STONE HOUSE especially since it begins with a cross-country journey to the West Coast, where I settled, and concludes with a visit, in 1987, to the ‘stone house’ in rural New Jersey. Even though there is some sort of time line that can be imagined, the film stands on its own. It is simply a series of episodes that touch upon facets of living in a new area with new weather, new people, new identities and stubborn old fears.” –Jerome Hiler

Total running time: ca. 85 min.
Tues, Nov 17 at 7:00.

EPILOGUE

PROGRAM 3: NATHANIEL DORSKY’S ‘ARBORETUM CYCLE’
“For the past several years California experienced an extreme drought. But this past winter good fortune brought a bountiful amount of storms and liquid refreshment. The spring that followed took on magical and celebratory qualities of energy, joy, fullness, and rebirth. In walking distance from my apartment is San Francisco’s Arboretum located in Golden Gate Park. I decided that I would make a film now on a single subject and that subject would be the light – not the objects, but the sacredness of the light itself in this splendid garden. What I did not know is that the great beauty of this magnificent spring would bring forth not one, but seven films, each one immediately following the previous. I began to photograph on the second week of February and finished the editing of the seventh film during the last days of December.
These seven films spontaneously manifested as the stages of life: early childhood, youth, maturity, old age, and death. ELOHIM was photographed in early spring, the week of the lunar new year, the very spirit of creation. ABATON was photographed a few weeks later in the full ripeness of spring, the very purity and intoxication of passion. CODA was photographed in late spring, in the aftermath of this purity, the first shades of mortality and knowledge. ODE, photographed in early summer, is a soft textured song of the fallen, the dissonant reds of death, seeds, and rebirth. SEPTEMBER is indeed, Indian summer, the halcyon swan song of earthly blessings. MONODY, shot in the fading autumnal glory is an energized declaration of the end. And EPILOGUE, photographed in early December, rests in quietude, the garden’s energy now descending into the dark, damp earth.” –Nathaniel Dorsky, 2017

GO YE AFAR

POSTCOLONIAL MOTION: TAXIS AND TRAVELOGUES
November 12
While not as long-standing as the romance with trains, cinema has a love affair with taxis. A cab ride is often more than just a means of getting from one place to another. The taxi is a cinematic apparatus in motion, a confessional, an urban microcosm, a transition, a temporary intimacy, an archive of daily life, a nightmare of forced conversation, an eavesdropper’s dream. Yet beneath such metaphors are material threats: the gig economy, prior and enduring precarities and exploitation of labor, movements of migration conditioned by colonization.

POSTCOLONIAL MOTION: TAXIS AND TRAVELOGUES draws together three films combining oral testimonies and the kinetics of transport. Frank Sweeney, Karimah Ashadu, and Timoteus Anggawan Kusno each consider the movements of people, goods and images, tracing how workers warp the barriers of historical categories and economic constraints as they cross borders.

Guest-programmed by Yasmina Price, who wrote the introduction and individual film descriptions.

Frank Sweeney
GO YE AFAR
2026, 24 min, DCP
Irish research-based artist Frank Sweeney’s latest work, GO YE AFAR (2026), stages a historical examination of economy, religion, national identity, and state power through the nocturnal taxi journey of a Nigerian driver in Dublin and Calabar. Making a link between the IMF’s role in imposing a debt structure on Ireland in the 2010s and the economic dependency still inflicted on the African continent, Sweeney makes a taxi journey an illuminating lens onto the dubious legacies of Catholic missionaries and European philanthropy, global privatization and the erosion of the commons.

Karimah Ashadu
BROWN GOODS
2020, 12 min, DCP
BROWN GOODS (2020), by British-born Nigerian artist and director Karimah Ashadu, both shares and reconfigures a similar terrain. The film scans the intermingled economies and geographies of Africa and Europe, conjuring Nigeria from its viewpoint in Germany. The setting is specifically Hamburg, a hub of international trade and multinational corporations. Ashadu’s agile camerawork delves through docks, weaving amongst ship containers and stacks of tires as Emeka – the self-sufficient central figure, excluded from formal work due to his humanitarian status – voices a sharp analysis of manufacturing and industrial monopolies, the politics of resources and global capital.

Timoteus Anggawan Kusno
OBSCURA
2026, 14 min, DCP
Indonesian filmmaker and visual artist Timoteus Anggawan Kusno’s OBSCURA (2026) echoes the unsettling, spectral dreaminess and mediatized archival memory of GO YE AFAR. Operating between Indonesia and the Netherlands, the film critically reworks the form of the ethnographic travelogue and colonial imagery through a story of theft (another kind of informal job). Gallery workers liberate a painting and carry it out in a truck, performing how images physically travel, are taken apart, literally unframed and reframed.

Total running time: ca. 55 min.
Thurs, Nov 12 at 7:00. The screening will be followed by a conversation with filmmaker and artist Frank Sweeney!

DEVO: RESTORATIONS AND REVELATIONS
COLLECTED FILM AND VIDEO WORKS 1976-84
PRESENTED BY FILM RESTORATIONIST PETER CONHEIM
November 17
Formed in the wreckage of the Kent State shootings in 1973, where co-founders Gerald V. Casale and Mark Mothersbaugh were students during the massacre, and still blowing the horn five decades later, Akron’s DEVO would take their subversive pop-art to staggering mainstream success. All the while, they considered themselves to be filmmakers as much as they were a “band.”

From their first film onward (1976’s THE BEGINNING WAS THE END: THE TRUTH ABOUT DE-EVOLUTION), they fused their love of Dadaist image-making with their constantly shapeshifting music, confounding their record label by plowing their meager promo budgets into companion films for songs. This program unveils painstaking new restorations and reconstructions of much of this pivotal work (including SATISFACTION, FREEDOM OF CHOICE, WHIP IT, and much more) alongside a host of rarely (and never)-seen live footage, encounters with hapless interviewers, and other spud ephemera, curated specially for Anthology Film Archives by film archivist and musician Peter Conheim (Mono Pause, ex-Negativland, Wet Gate, et al).

Total running time: ca. 80 min + discussion and Q&A.
Tues, Nov 17 at 7:30.

SINS OF THE FLESHAPOIDS

ACROSS THE MOVI-VERSE
November 10 & 11
To celebrate the publication – by the Film Desk – of Across the Movi-verse: Writing on Film, 2011–2021, a new collection by the one and only J. Hoberman, Anthology has invited Hoberman to guest-curate two evenings worth of programs, featuring films discussed in the pages of the new volume. Gathering together pieces written by Hoberman since his departure from the Village Voice in 2010, and originally appearing in Film Comment, Artforum, The Nation, Harper’s, Tablet, and elsewhere, Across the Movi-verse is a characteristically invaluable treasure trove of essays, articles, and reviews by one of the most thoughtful, penetrating, wide-ranging, and culturally astute critical minds in contemporary film culture. The book features Hoberman’s reflections on 21st-century cinema, society, and politics, as well as retrospective pieces on some of the artists, films, and fellow critics he’s engaged most deeply with through the years, such as Jack Smith, Jonas Mekas, the Kuchar brothers, and Manny Farber. For more info about Across the Movi-verse: Writing on Film, 2011–2021 visit the Film Desk Books website.

THE BATTLE OF TUNTENHAUS

GERMAN PUNK
November 4 – 11
On these shores we tend to think of the punk movement as one that, in its heyday, was predominantly an American and British phenomenon. But punk music, punk culture, and its ethos spread quickly and widely in the late 1970s and 80s, to (among many other places) Australia, Japan, Brazil, and to the countries of the Eastern bloc in Europe. This series focuses specifically on the impact and development of punk culture in Germany – both East and West – in the 1970s-90s. The series gathers a wide variety of films – all of them shot between 1979-99 and most of them very rarely screened in the U.S. – that document not only the bands that proliferated throughout Germany at the time, but the social and economic factors that influenced their emergence, and the response of German society at large. Taking place in West Berlin, East Berlin, the industrial Ruhr region, and elsewhere – both before and after the fall of the Wall – these films represent a fascinating and vibrant snapshot of an era, and feature invaluable contemporaneous glimpses of bands and musicians including Einstürzende Neubauten, Die Tödliche Doris, Die Kanzlers, Das Freie Orchester, and Nina Hagen.
 
The series has been guest-programmed by Sukhdev Sandhu, Sara Stevenson, and Zach Feldman.

Co-presented with the German Film Office, an initiative of the Goethe-Institut and German Films, and the Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung—New York Office, as part of a multi-city initiative of the Goethe-Institut marking the 50th anniversary of the punk movement.

Juliet Bashore
THE BATTLE OF TUNTENHAUS, PART 1 1991, 24 min, DCP. In German with English subtitles.

EINSTURZENDE NEUBAUTEN: LIEBESLIEDER
1993, 99 min, DCP. In English and German with English subtitles.
An essential text for anyone with a love for the avant-garde. The explosive soundscapes of industrial music titans Einstürzende Neubauten first shook Berlin – then the whole world – to its core in the 1980s. In LIEBESLIEDER, co-directors Klaus Maeck and Johanna Schenkel wield a fractured feast of killer Neubauten performances from Euro television: ones that highlight the group’s cinematic presence, wild handmade instruments, and totally unique style. For those who are at all curious about industrial or noise music in general, this sweaty documentary is unmissable. NARROW ROOMS: DRUNKEN NOODLES
November 12
In an era of gay big-screen romances, Cable-TV Christmas movies, and prestige Oscar-bait, there’s no shortage of affirming content for homocathected cineastes. Recent years have seen an establishment of a “gay film canon” that pops up on streaming services during Pride month. But there are hundreds of stranger, darker, and more exhilarating films that often fall through the cracks. “Narrow Rooms” is a series that celebrates these weird gay films, historically ignored or derided but shared like viruses amongst lovers of edgy, unvarnished queer cinema. Taking its name from James Purdy’s twisted gay S&M Southern Gothic novel, “Narrow Rooms” is a celebration of film faggotry, gay villains, horny homos, and heterodoxy. After 12 years co-curating the acclaimed artist-selected Queer/Art/Film series and programming for mainstream LGBT film festivals, filmmaker and writer Adam Baran picks his own favorite flicks for adventurous audiences at Anthology Film Archives, an institution that has celebrated edgy, shocking, groundbreaking gay cinema for over 50 years.

“Narrow Rooms” is curated and presented by Adam Baran.

DRUNKEN NOODLES
2025, 83 min, DCP. In English and Spanish with English subtitles.
When Adnan (Laith Khalifeh) arrives in New York to work at a Lower East Side gallery for the summer, he’s newly single and ready to mingle. He cruises strangers in the park at night and begins a flirtation with a delivery driver. At work, he’s helping to exhibit the recently discovered work of Sal Salandra, a real-world gay artist and elder who crafts tableaus of gay orgies and sex club scenes in vibrant needlepoint. These artworks – and Sal himself, played by Ezriel Kornel – become the connective thread for a time-jumping narrative that unfolds in four parts. Made for little money, with a small crew and total creative control, the latest by Lucio Castro (END OF THE CENTURY) is a delightful, sexually-charged short-story collection with whimsical elements of dreamy magical realism. Released during the full flower of NYC PRIDE season, DRUNKEN NOODLES is well worth watching – or rewatching – before making your Best-of-2026 lists.

• FACES: MOVING-IMAGE PORTRAITURE
November 20 – 30
More often than not, when films are referred to as “portraits” what is meant are fictional or non-fictional narrative accounts of a particular character or subject. This series instead focuses on films that function more literally as portraits in the art-historical sense – that follow in the tradition of the painted, drawn, sculpted, or photographed portrait, but that introduce the elements of motion and time, as well as montage. Exploring the tradition of portraiture in the cinema, this series surveys the variety of approaches filmmakers have taken to depicting the human face – and by extension, the mysterious relationship between the face and the depths of thought, emotion, and personality that human physiognomy variously suggests, reveals, or conceals. Including everything from Andy Warhol’s SCREEN TESTS (perhaps the most famous and ambitious collection of filmic portraits) and Gregory Markopoulos’s majestic GALAXIE, to Yoko Ono’s FILM NO. 5 (SMILE) and FLUXFILM NO. 16, to the work of other artists who have demonstrated a sustained engagement with the form (like Gérard Courant, Friedl vom Gröller, and Joan Logue), the series also features short portrait films by Stan Brakhage, Narcisa Hirsch, Tehching Hsieh, Kurt Kren, Maria Lassnig, Gunvor Nelson, Edward Owens, Ernst Schmidt Jr., and many others.
