OHIO TEENS REMAKE THE CROW

BY PATRICK DAHL
NOVEMBER 17, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

The dandy psycho is an irresistible pose for maladjusted theater kids whose will to power and raw-nerve sensitivity struggle to coexist within the hormonal soup of adolescence. Those first tastes of real emotional torment, the humiliating swing between impotence and invincibility, and the hubristic conviction that universal human experience is paradoxically novel to one’s own young life all subsidize a great deal of popular culture. If Brandon Lee hadn’t died on the set of The Crow (1994), promotional images of him in leather pants and whiteface hunched below street lights would probably still have inspired Halloween costumes for millions of highly sensitive young men. Recalling Michael Keaton’s Batman and anticipating Heath Ledger’s Joker, Lee’s Eric Draven is catnip for brooding teens: a Poe-quoting, slender-yet-shredded rock star returned from the dead to avenge his fiancee’s rape and murder. Throughout the film, he spends equal time torturing bad guys and sobbing in the dark while hugging his knees. The entanglement of the film’s goth angel iconography and Lee’s death have largely obscured the richness of director Alex Proyas’s grunge-industrial riff on Tim Burton’s Gotham while prolonging the life of this unlikeliest of intellectual properties.

After 14-year old David Ullman of Rittman, Ohio, saw The Crow “quite a few times” he sought out the source material, James O’Barr’s graphic novel, and subsequently enlisted pal Matt Jackson to “re-adapt” the book as a tribute to the Proyas/Lee film. Ullman starred as Draven while Jackson manned the camera. Filmed on family camcorders, the effort spanned their entire high school careers, resulting in both James O’Barr’s The Crow (1998)—gothic source attribution was all the rage in the 1990s after Bram Stoker’s Dracula (1992) and Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein (1994)—and a behind-the-scenes documentary, Inertia: Re-Making The Crow (2001). Both are admirable, naked documents of adolescent devotion.

If Proyas wisely jettisoned much of O’Barr’s cringe poetry, lines like “I am morphine for a wooden leg” were understandably intoxicating to pubescent suburbanites trapped in a lifestage defined by aching reach exceeding clumsy grasp. Turning empty piggy banks into aesthetic virtue, the duo shot in low/no lighting conditions and desaturated their footage to reflect the monochromatic comic, producing an impressively high-contrast tonal range with chaotic germs of video noise swimming through it. Their framing is particularly striking, with several shots possessing an immediate atmospheric beauty that shortcircuits our warm condescension toward Ullman and Jackson’s youth. During a reflective interview in Inertia, Jackson innocently describes watching “real movies” to learn about blocking. When in doubt, they returned to their inspiration like a hymn and recreated O’Barr’s panels as best they could on basement sets.

If Inertia has an antagonist, it’s the flakiness of the classmates Ullman and Jackson conscripted to round out the cast. Their inability to wrangle enough dudes to play Draven’s victims locked the production in a perpetual crawl and at one point made them both quit the enterprise entirely. Buoyed by the lovely belief that they “would have a movie” if they just kept going, they resumed filming and assembled “the picture” at first in a makeshift bedroom editing suite and eventually during overnight sessions at a local TV studio. Despite the tension with unpaid buddies who’d rather steal nips from Dad’s vodka than learn their lines, the project is a testament to the more palatable aspects of young suburban maledom. While Ullman struggles to get into character for a scene of self-mutilation, Jackson whispers to his camera, “Dave’s in the corner cutting himself and trying to cry.” We can forgive ourselves for laughing at the absurdity of the statement itself, but the moment evinces a familiar, uncomprehending concern for a friend who simultaneously inspires and frightens him. Later, the commingled fear and desire of the adult world animates a discussion of whether or not to adapt the graphic novel’s scene of sexual assault. Looking back just a few years after production, one participant remembers thinking “We’re 14, we can’t film rape!” while Jackson admits to wrestling with the idea of making something he doesn’t want his family to see. But as the production aged, so did its creators, who ultimately found creative methods to honor the story without stepping too far out of their coccooned innocence.

“Ohio Teens Remake The Crow” screens tonight, November 17, at Spectacle.
