DAVID LYNCH'S DELETED SCENES

BY DEAN HURLEY
NOVEMBER 16, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

With a brand new transfer of more than an hour of deleted and extended scenes, Criterion’s new Wild at Heart 4K release provides another unique opportunity to peer into Lynch’s filmmaking process. Lynch was a fierce advocate for the final cut of his films, and he was also incredibly protective of the audience’s experience of that cut. Some home video releases of his work purposely left out chapter stops so viewers couldn’t skip to individual scenes. The similarly protective Stanley Kubrick is rumored to have gone so far as to destroy his own unused footage. So it’s striking that Lynch, later into his career, actually made a point to present and celebrate his outtakes. He ultimately released compilations of deleted scenes running fifty to ninety minutes for the disc releases of Blue Velvet, Wild at Heart, Twin Peaks: Fire Walk With Me, and INLAND EMPIRE.

This attitude began to take shape after the turn of the millennium, with his focus on early internet-based video experimentation. His own website, davidlynch.com, shifted his attention toward short-form experiments along the lines of his early films made as an art school student, and eventually some of this digital material would connect to form his final feature, INLAND EMPIRE (2006). I began working with David during this period in 2005. I was hired as an audio engineer and started work on the sound and music for what would become INLAND EMPIRE, followed by mixing these deleted scene assemblies throughout the next several years. The very first collection of deleted scenes to be unearthed and re-examined was the material from Wild at Heart in 2008.

Wild at Heart’s sprawling road movie format uniquely lent itself to overshooting. Adapted from Barry Gifford’s 1989 novel, the film covers a lot of asphalt and a lot of characters. Adapting a novel to the screen typically is an exercise in omission and concession, but Lynch set out to shoot far more of the book than anyone could reasonably expect to fit into a single feature while even wedging in new elements such as the Wizard of Oz themes absent from Gifford’s novel. According to editor Duwayne Dunham, the first assembly was a staggering four and a half hours, and it “didn’t work.” But Lynch insisted on premiering the film at Cannes in May of 1990, so a dramatic restructuring needed to happen in a short amount of time in order to make the festival deadline. Scenes were reordered and combined, and entire chunks were eliminated. The result is a taut, punctuated cross-cutting that didn’t exist in the original shooting script. It was a ham-fisted yet boldly dramatic approach, and it ultimately came to define the film’s stylistic voice.

Unlike the deleted scenes from Blue Velvet and Fire Walk With Me, the Wild at Heart scenes are direct workprint lifts from 1990. The picture edits visible in the transfer are the exact same tape splices Dunham originally made before the scenes were ultimately removed. The Blue Velvet and Fire Walk With Me deleted scenes were reconstructed from scans of the original camera negative (OCN). Every take and piece of coverage was transferred, allowing Lynch to edit the scenes entirely from scratch, which he did in 2010 and 2013, respectively. But the Wild at Heart scenes are a unique time-capsule that show the original assemblies as they were put together with the original editor during post-production.

Scott Ryan’s recent oral history The Road to David Lynch’s Wild at Heart (2026) reveals that many of these rearrangements and trims continued right up through the film’s final dub at Skywalker Ranch. An array of stylistic cross-cuts, extreme close-up inserts, and dissolves became a toolset for stitching together its final arrangement. Sound and musical effects were improvised on the dub stage, punctuating and emphasizing these seams with a sense of strong intentionality. The result is a heavily stylized mosaic of a story that whiplashes its audience back and forth down the full length of its yellow brick road.

The material left on the cutting room floor amounts to nearly another feature worth of material. Some early connective pieces were dropped from the front of the assembly, such as transitional moments where characters travel from point A to point B. But there is also a wealth of material that introduces entirely new characters, including ones played by Tracy Walter and Max Perlich. Other scenes significantly deepen our understanding of the primary players. Crispin Glover’s Dell, a marginal character in the feature, gets more attention, both in his backstory and in a missed encounter at a filling station where Sailor and Lula stop for directions. Harry Dean Stanton’s Johnnie Farragut has much more screen time in his pursuit of Sailor and Lula, including three separate moments running into Reggie and Dropshadow before his demise. We also see an extended version of Farragut’s torture scene, apparently intended as a much longer set piece with a graphic climax that sent early test audiences “rushing” out of the theater in protest. That climax is still absent even from the deleted scenes presentation. One can only surmise that those test audiences conveyed something to Lynch that he took to heart in understanding how much was too much for this particular story.

There is such a wealth of additional footage across Wild at Heart, Blue Velvet, and Fire Walk With Me that it’s easy to see how Lynch could have shaped these stories in different ways. It offers a wider view of each film’s story and reveals the latitude of material required to have a high level of control over the pacing and focus of the final versions. It’s a revealing look into the unchiseled stories and a reminder that the trim can define, shape, and emphasize what’s left, and ultimately has a huge hand in a film’s artistic voice.

There was a fearlessness to the editorial approach on Wild at Heart. All of Lynch’s efforts shared this attitude: he sought out experimentation like an oracle and left himself room to be able to find a film. As Dunham told Ryan, “There are directors who shoot the movie. I think Steven Spielberg is the poster child for that. David Lynch, George Lucas, Francis Ford Coppola, and Marty Scorsese shoot around the movie, and they find it in the cutting room. There’s a big difference.” Lynch’s decision to allow viewers access to his omitted material pulls back the curtain on the wild style of discovery at the heart of his own craft. It’s not surprising that most of the deleted material from Wild at Heart adheres closely to scenes from Gifford’s novel, and its removal actually works to shape Lynch’s version further into his own voice.

Wild at Heart went on to win the Palme d’Or at the 43rd Cannes Film Festival. As the inevitable years stack between Lynch’s audience and his death in 2025, elements like these deleted scenes help connect the dots of his unique craft and output.

Dean Hurley is a film sound designer, composer, and re-recording mixer. He operated Lynch’s Asymmetrical Studio for a decade and a half, where he collaborated on the sound and music for many of Lynch’s film projects, commercial work, and musical albums. Among these were INLAND EMPIRE, Twin Peaks: The Return, as well as finishing and mixing deleted scene assemblies of Blue Velvet, Wild At Heart, and Fire Walk With Me.

Wild at Heart, including new 5.1 mixes by Hurley for the feature and deleted scenes, is now out on 4K UHD and Blu-ray from Criterion.
