Interview with Hanne Lippard

Daniel Muzyczuk

Hanne Lippard, Look for Words, 2024. Seven-channel digital-audio sound installation, reflective flooring, lighting system, 21:46 minutes; dimensions variable. Installation at Hamburger Bahnhof - Nationalgalerie der Gegenwart, 2024. © Courtesy Hanne Lippard. Photo by Jacopo La Forgia.

November 24, 2026

www.e-flux.com

Feature

Hanne Lippard’s work uses rhythm and repetition to heighten viewers’ understanding of how everyday language—and in particular speech—mediates their experience of the world. Across vocal performances, texts, and LPs, overheard phrases become the basis for looping, stuttering texts, while disembodied voices in her sound installations mimic the incorporeal authority of satnav systems and smart speakers. As recent solo exhibitions at Kunsthall Trondheim, Munich’s Haus der Kunst, and Hamburger Bahnhof Berlin attest, the artist’s voice is unmistakably her own—yet it could just as easily belong to an automated service, or even an institution. In this conversation, Lippard and Daniel Muzyczuk, director of the Muzeum Sztuki in Łódź, discuss how the artist’s work moves between performance and installation, and how legal language can become material for poetry.

Daniel Muzyczuk: Your practice has shifted over time from graphic design to visual art, and increasingly towards music. I would like to begin with this migratory form. Does the work change because of the site, occasion, and medium, or do you feel that something constant moves through all these transformations?

Hanne Lippard: I think that the constant is myself and my voice as a reference point. The forms change, of course, and maybe that comes partly from graphic design—from this idea that there is always a form to adhere to, or a frame given by the occasion. When I am invited to do a performance, there is already an idea of what that performance might be. But the performance itself is usually very minimal. It is rarely staged in any elaborate way.

For me there is also a very specific stability in the performance situation. When I perform, I need to be in a fixed position. I do not really like moving around. I need the score and the microphone in front of me. It makes me stable, mentally and physically. In one sense, then, the form is given and almost rigid. But the writing is not rigid in the same way. There is something about words moving through me while I perform. I can practice alone in a room, but certain realizations only happen in the suspension of the live moment, with people listening.

While in performance I often prefer the singularity of the voice, the one-to-one experience, in an installation, I like the multiplicity: a chorus, a dialogue, or a more spatial aspect. It allows the voice to become distributed in a way that is not possible when it is just my body and my voice in front of an audience.

DM: Let me move to the script. My understanding of Look for words, your 2024 work that moves between live performance and multichannel sound installation, exploring how repetition and wordplay destabilize language and the speaking subject, and perhaps of your work more broadly, is that voice is not only expression. It produces a subject through rhythm and repetition. I am thinking of phrases such as “I feel so much and I want to be part of everything,” or “I myself am a staircase”—formulations in which the subject seems to appear through language. Do you think repetition changes the meaning of language, or does it change the person speaking?

HL: It definitely changes meaning. Depending on how long you repeat something, meaning can go into nonsense. The word becomes sound before it becomes meaning again. Repetition can do both things: it can strengthen a word and it can weaken it. Socially speaking, words are repeated so much that their original meaning or potency can be lost. At the same time, repetition can make a word more audible and make us hear it again. This ambiguity, the ability to morph and influence the meaning of a word through sound and repetition, is what is so fascinating with the spoken word.

It also creates rhythm. It makes language musical. Whether it changes the person speaking is more complicated, because there are two different positions: the person who speaks and the person who listens. But I think it can influence both. As a speaker, you become more aware of delivery. It becomes less about logic and more about sound, rhythm, and conviction. Repetition can be a way of convincing yourself, or trying to convince others.

DM: Through your mimicry of customer service, wellness centers, and other impersonal voices, you assume roles that go beyond the individual. They are depersonalized, but they are also connected to contemporary social forms. How do you think about persona? What interests you in these registers of voice?

HL: What interests me is the strange counterpoint between something very impersonal or corporate and something very personal or confessional. We are expected to act in a confessional way now: to use our own identity, our own story, our own experience. This is connected to therapy culture, to marketing, to social media, to the idea of the self as a product. The language becomes both very personal and not personal at all.

I often write in the first person, but it is never simply me. I leave it open for the reader or listener. People often assume that if there is an “I” it must be autobiographical. But that “I” can be plural, or fictional, or just a device in itself.

DM: So it is a device for distancing yourself from the persona?

HL: Yes, but more than that, it is a device for digesting language. I constantly absorb terms and voices I come across. It is a way of distancing myself from the persistent murmur of the world, but not only that. It is me and not me. It is certainly me speaking now in this interview, although this interview also has a persona. Any form of speech has a kind of act in it. This again becomes a question of form and format.

That makes the work more complex, because these voices often assume intimacy. A wellness voice, for example, may be disembodied, but it speaks near intimate experiences or accompanies them. It may not speak about intimate things directly, but it is present while intimate things happen. So it leaks into the folds between private and public.

DM: That is exactly what interests me. Persona is not only a distancing device. It can also bring something closer. It can feel strangely intimate at the same time.

HL: Yes, and I am aware of that intimacy. My body is included in the work. The voice is not far from the voice speaking now. It is very close, almost one-to-one. Whether I am visibly in front of people or whether the voice comes through a speaker, the voice creates a physical experience.

When there is nothing to see, people become more aware of their own bodies. In a performance, especially a very quiet one, the audience becomes conscious of every small sound: someone moving in their clothes, swallowing, digesting, shifting slightly. The silence makes the body loud. That is one of the things I find interesting about performing in front of people: the physical awareness of the audience becomes part of the work.

DM: I want to return to the legal and institutional material in your work. In The Contract (2024), a performance text based on the bind created through the language in an agreement, you look for a poetic sensibility in legal language—a language that contains waiting, dependency, emotional management. There are lines such as “the work itself will not depend upon the happiness of the artist” and “the artist will have to remain accountable for her own emotions.” This touches on what we have been discussing, because institutional language becomes strangely intimate. What interests you in contracts and legal language as poetic material?

HL: With The Contract, it came to my awareness that the contract for the production of the work almost looked like a play. It listed actors. It begins by naming the artist, Hanne Lippard, but then inside the text I am referred to as “the Artist,” and the museum is “the Museum.” For me, it read almost like a staged play, or like an initial statement of the positions.

That is an interesting area because art is often imagined as coming from a spontaneous and emotional world, or a place without frames, a place of unruliness. Then these formats try to contain that unruliness: this must be delivered by this moment, this must happen in this way. It reflects different aspects of my own practice, especially when something goes wrong, or when the emotional part of the work becomes visible.

There is also the question of how much is demanded from the artist, and how those demands are not always returned by the institution. The institution can have many demands, while the artist's own needs are not necessarily met. This is especially true for performance. Museums often do not have the same standards for performance that music venues have. There are fewer established conditions, fewer spaces to retract to or to wait in.

The contract I used as a starting point came from the National Museum in Warsaw. This is not a critique of that institution in itself; Poland is a very bureaucratic country, and the contract was also translated from its original language into English. But I was amused by the language: this is this, this is not that, the work is not dependent on the artist’s happiness. In a fictional addition I made to the original contract, the artist is asked to contain her emotions, or show them only outside the building—unless they are part of the work itself, in which case they are allowed. The piece is partly about delivery and responsibility, but it is also farcical, almost parodic.

DM: It also plays with the tradition of scores and instructions, which returns to the hard core of the neo-avant-garde. But there is another dimension in it, which is also present in Look for words and Talk Shop (2024): the relation between language and embodiment. A contract is abstract, but it disciplines bodies, moods, behaviors, movements, expectations. In the contract there is also the sentence that the artist “becomes the text during the performance.” Do you think the contract allows you to embody invisible infrastructure, to bring it to the foreground?

HL: In the contract, the museum and the artist are placed on an equal level, at least in language. But of course the museum has infrastructure, staff, power, procedures. The artist is an individual practice. That equality is therefore strange.

My work often concerns the individual operating within a larger body, whether that larger body is capitalism, a company, an institution, or language itself. There is a dialog between these bodies. How does one manage individual experience inside something larger?

DM: If the subject is placed on the same level as an institution, does that feel empowering or overwhelming? Legal language confirms the capacity to act, to operate, to activate. But the same comparison might also become crushing.

HL: I think it is first of all overwhelming. In general, everyone is overwhelmed by information and language today. My work is an attempt to structure that, or to navigate it—to find some logic, or non-logic, within the overload. The empowerment comes through humor. Humor can be empowering because it makes the overwhelm lighter. By addressing the absurdity, you can survive it a little better.

Daniel Muzyczuk is an art historian and the director of the Muzeum Sztuki in Łódź, Poland. His most recent book is Twilight of the Magicians (Spector Books).
