Bangkok Art Biennale 2026, “Angels and Mara”

Patrick Langley

Sun Yuan and Peng Yu, Angel, 2008. Silica gel, fiberglass, stainless steel, human hair, 180 × 220 cm. Image courtesy of Bangkok Art Biennale.

November 17, 2026

October 29, 2026–February 28, 2027

www.e-flux.com

Review

Various locations, Bangkok

An angel lies face down on the floor of Bangkok’s National Museum as if it has crash-landed from Heaven. Its fleshy, stunted wings resemble those found on supermarket chickens: unfit for flying. Sun Yuan and Peng Yu’s sculpture Angel (2008) portrays the titular celestial being as decrepit, gray-haired and—most sacrilegiously—deceased. A depiction of mortal frailty convincing enough to invite the viewer’s pity, though not suspend their disbelief, the work signals that the fifth edition of the Bangkok Art Biennale (BAB), featuring seventy-two artists and collectives across eleven venues ranging from Buddhist temples to sprawling shopping malls and public spaces, will seek to turn the spiritual world on its head—and that the result might not be subtle.

The metaphysical turn feels timely. From the rise of mysticism during the Black Death to the spiritualism craze of World War I and, more recently, the surge in popularity of astrology and tarot (not to mention conspiracy theories) during the Covid-19 pandemic, desperate times have led people to seek divine explanations for, and deliverance from, worldly chaos. A month before the opening of this year’s BAB (curated by biennale co-founder and ongoing artistic director Apinan Poshyananda, alongside co-curators Adulaya Hoontrakul, Rémy Jarry, Melanie Pocock, and Kulapat Yantrasast), catastrophic floods hit Bangkok. Stranded locals navigated submerged streets in boats and kayaks while authorities declared the city a disaster zone. The floods had cleared by the time BAB opened, yet the biblical disaster set a fittingly drastic backdrop for a show that frames Krung Thep—Bangkok’s shortened ceremonial name, typically translated as “City of Angels”—as a battleground of Manichean conflict. What role can contemporary art play, this exhibition asks, in situating a disordered present in wider ethical and mythical contexts?

The angels of the title refer to the divine messengers of the Abrahamic religions, while Māra is taken from Buddhism: a deva who personifies temptation, delusion, and death. “Angels and Mara” thus combines two strikingly different moral systems, not to mention classes of supernatural beings, to establish a moral contrast—and it quickly gets confusing. The goal of Buddhism is liberation from the endless cycle of rebirth, which to Christians is sacrilege; the goal of Christian eschatology, meanwhile, is union with an omnipotent creator in which no Buddhist believes. To further complicate the picture, angels can fall from grace—the Devil used to be one, of course. Indeed, the short curatorial text printed at the entrance to the venues questions whether fixed moral categories are sustainable in the age of disinformation and deepfakes. “There is no longer a universal law of human compassion and empathy,” it states. But was there ever?

In an overly familiar curatorial move that echoes the theme of BAB’s third edition, 2022’s “CHAOS : CALM,” the show thus invokes a binary in order to dissolve it.(1) And by plucking ideas from various spiritual traditions based on superficial resemblance, it risks flattening important differences—a common pitfall for exhibitions that engage spiritual themes without taking seriously the complexity of the belief systems they invoke. “In Hebrew,” the curatorial text reads, “Mara means bitterness or strength.” Whether or not that is accurate, the fact that a Hebrew word is spelled similarly to the Sanskrit and Pali name of a Buddhist deity is a linguistic coincidence.(2) To elevate it to anything more would require a deeper engagement with scholarship.

Thankfully, a number of the selected artists take on the themes of the exhibition with a nuance lacking in the framing of the show itself, with works that often speak directly to the contexts in which they are shown. Sin Wai Kin’s sunset-hued perfume advert Essence (2024), displayed on a giant LED billboard in a public square surrounded by sleek offices and shopping malls, satirizes the fantasies of transcendence at the heart of consumer culture. At temple complex Wat Pho, by contrast, Leiko Ikemura’s bronze sculpture Memento Mori (2013/26) depicts a reclining bronze figure with hollow eye sockets whose lower half transforms into a flower, a chimerical counterpart to the forty-seven-meter-long Reclining Buddha displayed in the adjacent hall. Pairing a central tenet of Buddhism—everything that exists will one day perish—with a titular nod to western Classical and Renaissance art, the work achieves what the exhibition as a whole doesn’t manage: making felt the human predicament underpinning both religions without grasping after an equivalence between them.

A highlight is Sakarin Krue-On’s two-channel video installation Tha Tien (2018). This pastiche of black-and-white, silent-era movies depicts a conflict between the quarrelsome guardian giants of Wat Arun and Wat Pho—both also serve as exhibition venues—and superheroes played by the artist and armed with little more than hands clasped in prayer. This deft, hilarious piece wears its serious message lightly: nonviolence practiced by ordinary people has moral if not always political power. Elsewhere, video works by Pathompon Tesprateep and Sudaporn Teja use lullabies and folk songs to moving effect, positioning the artists and their subjects as conduits for cultural, familial, and historical forces larger than themselves.

Some artists push this idea further by adopting quasi-shamanic guises. Apulu Pati Tyrell’s hypnotic video Fāgogo (2016), displayed in the galleries tucked away amid the sneaker and physiotherapy shops of “integrated mixed-use lifestyle district” One Bangkok, blends startling, dreamlike visuals with Samoan ceremonial dress to link traditional storytelling traditions to contemporary queer identity. In Amanda Coogan’s performance at BACC, The Wren, The Wren (2025), the artist ascends the corkscrew ramp that connects the upper floors of the museum with purgatorial slowness: part Sisyphus, part everywoman. Also at BACC, Chomphunut Phuttha tattoos didactic Thai proverbs about how women should behave on volunteers’ bodies in Tattoo sketch for Womara (2026), reversing the text to subvert their power. The artist’s wry, often funny demeanor turns a durational performance into a collective event that implicates viewers in a ritual transformation.

The most forceful works dispute the curatorial text’s claim that “our perception of reality is trapped within the blurred borders of right, wrong and in between.” At BACC, Manit Sriwanichpoom’s provocatively titled video The Banality of Evil (2026) splices together news reports and social media posts, including those by members of the IDF, to document war crimes in Gaza. This relentless work underscores that, when it comes to genocide, the distinctions between good and evil are not only plain to see but important to articulate—the question is whether anything will be done to stop it.

Similarly, Htein Lin’s grueling two-channel video When I was a Lousy Millionaire (2022), which documents the torturous conditions of his six-year detention for pro-democracy activism in Myanmar, leaves little doubt as to distinctions of right and wrong. 000235 (1998–2004), a harrowing collection of monoprints made using syringes, toothpaste, old uniforms, and other materials available to Lin in detention, are equally moving. These sobering works return us, like crash-landing angels, back to the human world. Hell is not a metaphysical realm in the afterlife, Lin suggests, so much as a condition manufactured by people on Earth and inflicted upon the innocent by the powerful.
