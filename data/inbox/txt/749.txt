Picture This: When the Ocean Blooms
By Brittney Borowiec

Consider the emerald-green mats of algae that blanket large parts of the North Atlantic every spring. These seasonal blooms power the oceanic food web by injecting valuable nutrients into the system. Some blooms are so large that they can be seen from space, and some can poison the water.

As simple creatures made up of only a single cell, algae lack eyes, hearts, and brains. It’s not commonly known but algae are microbes, not plants; still, they use photosynthesis to trap energy from sunlight within the chemical bonds of sugar, the fundamental fuel of metabolism. But what algae, more properly called phytoplankton, lack in complexity as individuals, they more than make up for as a community during a bloom.

The Dreams of Octopus and Cuttlefish
By Peter Godfrey-Smith

Dreaming seems a mental extravagance, far from the brain’s essential business of perceiving the world, remembering events, and controlling behavior. Tracking the environment and learning its patterns—those are the sorts of things we’d expect to be common in the minds of other animals, not the wild excursions of dreaming.

Dreams are also hard to study even in ourselves, quickly forgotten and likely confabulated in memory and report. But the evidence for dreaming in nonhuman animals is better than we had any right to expect.

Cephalopods, especially octopuses and cuttlefish, have a special place in the history of these discoveries. This is partly because they are such expressive animals; an unusual amount of what is going on inside them can be glimpsed from the outside. The continual changes in color and pattern on the skin of these animals are controlled by the brain, through tiny muscles, and these colors and patterns can change in less than a second. In cephalopods, the skin is a window in. These are expressive animals in other ways as well, with their numerous and flexible arms. From this expressiveness, we’ve learned a lot.

Picture This: Abyss Abundant
By Dan Samorodonitsky

To a eukaryotic imagination, the bottom of the ocean is a hadal zone, something hellish, abyss-like, barren. A lowly fish notwithstanding, the deeper one goes, the less there seems to be. Vessels that have gone to Challenger Deep—the bottom of the Trench and the deepest place in all of Earth’s seas, a site about 35,000 feet below the surface of the Pacific—have seen little in the way of complex life. That makes sense—most life is dependent on energy and carbon from photosynthesis, and light doesn’t penetrate well enough to support it below about 600 feet. The remaining 34,400 feet lie in relative or complete darkness.

Those divers can be admonished if not forgiven for focusing on things like “complexity” or “cell membranes” or “spines.” No matter how deep the submersible goes, no matter how empty the waters seem, there will always be something there. As far as the ocean is concerned, the deepest depths can contain more varied, diverse bacterial life than even the more pleasant, habitable areas of waters near the surface.

Against the Ocean Wall
By Lauren Markham

In the small, coastal city of Saco, Maine, not too far from where my brother lives, the Army Corps of Engineers is currently busy building a wall of rock to keep out an invader: the sea. Actually, what they are building is what’s called a spur jetty—that is, a wall jutting out from an existing breakwater that, due to rising seas and more intense storms, is no longer up to its task. At the cost of $45 million, the Saco wall will be constructed by piling massive rocks atop one another some 20 feet above the usual level of the sea. But what’s usual, these days, anyway?

The Saco coastline is, quite literally, disappearing into the sea. As a result of just two January 2024 storms, the coast of Saco, along with neighboring Kennebunkport and Biddeford, lost 28 percent of its shoreline—most of which was never recovered or rebuilt. So intense were these storms that the water lifted higher than it had ever been recorded, sweeping houses, boats, cars, bridges, pieces of lighthouse, and critical ecosystems away with it. Two hours north, where my mom lives, the water lifted a neighbor’s boathouse off its foundation and deposited it a quarter mile down the coast, on someone else’s lawn. Mainers lost treasured homes, businesses, piers, and economies. Hence, the wall.

It’s a favored human response to encroachment, real or perceived. In the U.S., the call to “build the wall” immediately brings to mind the southern border. How common it now is to gesture to the barricade, stoking fears and convincing the population that it’s under attack from the outside. Of course we’ve long used walls to lock people inside, too: behind the iron curtain, for instance, or on the infected side of the Vaucluse mountains in France during the time of the plague.
