POEM OF THE SEA

BY Sonya Vseliubska
OCTOBER 18, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Yuliya Solntseva is too formidable an artist to be confined to Oleksandr Dovzhenko’s shadow, yet The Poem of the Sea (1958) makes separating them impossible. A revered icon of Ukrainian cinema, Dovzhenko once described himself as “a victim of barbaric working conditions, a victim of the wretchedness and pettiness of a bureaucratic, dead film committee.” Still, a visit to Kakhovka gave him his final creative impulse, as he was captivated by the beauty of its land and culture, ultimately devoting his last five years to a film about a world soon to disappear beneath the reservoir. Scholars have suggested that Solntseva’s ties to the NKVD shielded her husband from repression, though not from the depression its threats fostered. He died of a heart attack just before filming began and Solntseva became what the writer Oles Honchar called a “tigress guarding Dovzhenko’s interests,” making her directorial debut with a flamboyant farewell steeped in personal loss.

Poem of the Sea follows General Fedorchenko (Boris Livanov) on a belated return to his homeland before its houses, orchards and ancestral graves sink beneath the reservoir. As villagers take leave of their old land and contemplate the new village rising on higher ground, Fedorchenko’s intrusive war flashbacks and voiced-over philosophical musings on mortality turn the festivities toward bitter, pensive nostalgia.

Dovzhenko was enthralled by the utopian city of Nova Kakhovka, yet his fascination sat uneasily alongside his spiritual connection to the Ukrainian earth and culture the dam would submerge—an ambivalence Solntseva shapes into an ode to the project and an elegy for what it consumes. The very notion of collectivism subduing nature by force is melodramatic at its core, and Solntseva amplifies her husband’s taste for epic images, ritual gestures and lyrical swerves between realism and fantasy. Her immaculate vistas of green hills and wide valleys under a pink sunset, set to a sweeping orchestral score, transpose Dovzhenko’s awestruck Kakhovka diaries into images, a lyricism the film breaks each time it descends to the ground.

There, the characters deliver their lines in chrestomathic Bolshevik jargon steeped in dutiful optimism. Though she never worked on stage, Solntseva arrives at something close to Brechtian estrangement, the declamatory performances exposing the artifice until it tips into accidental sarcasm, as when one hero confesses, “When I have a hard time, I remember Lenin.” At home, at work and even in their dreams, people insist that only a couple more years must pass before the good times arrive. The refrain distils communism’s imperative to endure, with happiness and success perpetually deferred to a vague future that Dovzhenko would never live to see. The villagers’ toast to a bright future now plays as a requiem after Russian forces destroyed the Kakhovka dam in June 2023, drowning yet another ancestral world. And Poem of the Sea now becomes a lament its images had been composing all along.

Poem of the Sea screens tonight, October 18, at Metrograph on 35mm as part of the series “Oleksandr Dovzhenko: Earth and Empire.” Curator Anri Vartan will introduce the film.
