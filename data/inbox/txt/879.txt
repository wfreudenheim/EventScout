OCTOBER COUNTRY

BY STEPHANIE MONOHAN
OCTOBER 21, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Filmmakers Donal Mosher and Michael Palmieri documented the everyday life of Donal’s working-class family in the deindustrialized Mohawk Valley in New York over the course of one year. Their efforts resulted in the feature-length documentary October Country (2009), a sad and empathetic portrait of multigenerational hardship characterized by abuse, PTSD, and poverty. Yet the directors avoid exploiting a defining event or revelation the family has to reckon with as a gateway into the Moshers’ story, instead demonstrating how such generational trauma collapses the past and the present, trapping those affected by it in a pattern of inertia that is difficult to escape.

It is Dotte, the family matriarch, who analyzes the family dynamic most poignantly throughout the film. While giving (often unheeded) advice to her loved ones, she provides commentary on their various struggles, psychologizing everyone for the filmmakers and viewers. Dotte is especially upset by the repetition of teen pregnancy and domestic violence experienced by her daughter Donna and granddaughter Danael. When the film opens, Danael is about to lose custody of her 2-year-old daughter Ruby after separating from Ruby’s abusive father. Eventually, all of the children and grandchildren—and even other non-familiar troubled youth—wind up in Dotte’s care. Dotte’s husband Don, a Vietnam veteran and ex-cop is the strong, silent type, tight-lipped about his upsetting memories of wartime and thus also unable to exorcise the demons that have plagued him since his youth. His self-imposed loneliness is on display frequently, with scenes of him avoiding the family to watch old war movies on T.V. or working on seemingly random projects in the garage included throughout the film. Meanwhile, lurking on the periphery is Don’s estranged sister Denise, a practicing Wiccan who dresses the part, visiting the local cemeteries at night to make communication with spirits and frustrating her brother simply with her existence.

The visual style conjured by Mosher and Palmieri feels like a blend of their backgrounds in still photography and music videos, respectively. October Country began as Mosher’s own photo project, where he captured his family and their economically-depressed suburban surroundings. The duo builds on Mosher’s rust-belt gothic aesthetic in the documentary, incorporating more stylized slow-motion sequences and emphasizing the saturated colors in their digital photography to create a more lyrical portrait of the Mosher family than a more straightforward vérité treatment would allow. This visual approach, alongside the film’s melancholic ambient score, imbues the footage on display with an eerie beauty that brings the haunted dimension of the Mosher family to the fore while also differentiating the film from stereotypical depictions of poor and working-class life popularized by 2000s reality and docudrama television, like Teen Mom and The Simple Life.

The pervasiveness of violence in both the Moshers’ home and the wider Mohawk Valley community resurfaces throughout the film. From the frequency and familiarity in which they are discussed, child abuse and domestic violence are practically facts of life, with every generation of Mosher women encountering one or both at some point. In their world, violence is not a horror hidden behind the closed doors of an imaginary suburban ideal, it permeates the entire atmosphere of the Mohawk Valley. In one scene, Danael describes how the only substantive economic thread the region has to hold onto is the Remington arms factory, which in turn puts everyone in a position where they’re pressured to support the United States’s wars in the Middle East in order to keep the need for weapons manufacturing going. Thus, inside and outside the home, violence is a perverse component of social reproduction.

The title October Country refers to the film’s seasonal bookends, but it also captures something intrinsic about what living in the Mohawk Valley is like for the Mosher family. While we see them over the course of four seasons, the chilly awareness of slow decline and death never lifts. If autumn is the time of year when people reflect and look forward to a fresh start, the Moshers are not so illusioned. They know a fresh start is not something that will just occur naturally. Every mistake and disappointment they encounter is compounded by the fact that they’ve already seen all of it before. For the Moshers, the veil between the living and the dead, the past and the present, is always thin.

October Country screens this evening, October 21, and on October 24, at Spectacle.

PARIS WAS A WOMAN

BY MEGHAN RACKLIN
OCTOBER 21, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Opening with one war and ending with another, Greta Schiller’s documentary Paris Was a Woman (1996) is aware of the fleeting nature of the milieu it depicts. She documents the evanescent artistic community of queer women who populated Paris’s Left Bank in the 1920s and 1930s, including Gertrude Stein and Alice B. Toklas, Djuna Barnes, Natalie Barney, and Colette, among others.

Schiller’s film, now restored in 4K for its 20th anniversary, has the charming, lived-in feeling of a BBC documentary, aided by actress Juliet Stevenson’s elegant voiceover. Schiller pairs Stevenson’s narration with expert interviews and archival gems, including street scenes and newsreels, home-video footage of figures like Barney, Stein, and Toklas, and clips from Josephine Baker performances and Germaine Dulac’s L’invitation au voyage (1927). The result is perhaps not as formally radical as Stein’s literary experiments, but in its collage-like effect, the film reconstructs the scene as a group portrait, focused not on any singular genius but rather a community of women.

Paris Was A Woman’s success rests on this ensemble cast, whom Schiller brings vividly to life. Some of the film’s most charming figures are Sylvia Beach and Adrienne Monnier, life partners whose twinned bookstores, Shakespeare & Co. and La Maison des Amis des Livres, sat across the street from one another and jointly served as the neighborhood’s heart and cultural center. An archival interview with Beach, cheerful and poised, is one of the film’s chief pleasures. Other key interview footage features Janet Flanner, The New Yorker’s longtime Paris correspondent, who emerges as a flinty, forceful presence on the scene, and the dashing photographer Gisèle Freund, whose color photographs furnish the film with some of its most compelling images.

In her eagerness to depict an idyllic vanished world, Schiller occasionally minimizes the uglier aspects of the cultural milieu of interwar Paris. She exoticizes Baker while at the same time using her status in Paris to gloss over the racial homogeneity of her subjects and their world. And toward the end of the film, as the onset of World War II splinters the scene, she mentions only briefly that Barney and her partner, the artist Romaine Brooks, were fascist sympathizers, a view she characterizes, considerably downplaying the matter, as “misguided.” Schiller also does not mention Stein’s collaboration with the Vichy regime.

But while Schiller sometimes reduces the complexity of her subjects, she remains attuned to the complexity and richness of their social world. Throughout the film, she plots her women’s lives on a map, a charming way of demonstrating how close they were, in more than simple geography. These women were lovers, friends, and artistic companions—a web of relationships that leads Schiller to a moving reflection on their reconfiguration of the figure of the muse. They were each other’s muses, she suggests, transforming the traditional woman’s role from a passive to an active and creative one.

Paris Was a Woman screens this evening, October 21, at Metrograph.
