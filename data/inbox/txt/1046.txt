FORM, SHADOW, MOTION

BY GLADYS LOU
NOVEMBER 4, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

A young man holds a rainbow-colored rabbit lantern aboard a ferry rocking across Victoria Harbour. A glint of sunlight catches his shoulder, outlining the contours of his face, as the silhouette of the skyline gently sways in the window behind him. This shot from Cici Wu’s film is taken from one of four contemplative portraits of Hong Kong in a screening organized by The Drawing Center that features artist films and videos by Wu, Chan Hau Chun, Tang Kwok-hin, and Simon Liu. Floating between documentary observation and surrealist association, the films follow a missing person, a village’s inherited customs, and the city’s ever-changing political and urban landscapes across an extended timelapse. Without explicit depiction of protest or violence, they ruminate on the feeling of loss and uncertainty that has lingered in Hong Kong since the 1997 Handover to China, as well as the erosion of civil liberties following the imposition of China’s National Security Law in Hong Kong on June 30, 2020. Abstract images of public spaces and fleeting figures flow by like hazy memories, gathering over the city like fog.

In Unfinished Return of Yu Man Hon (2019), Wu revisits the case of Yu Man-hon, an autistic boy who went missing on August 24, 2000, after crossing the Hong Kong-Mainland China border. The film opens onto a sky-blue expanse, where amorphous white forms resembling clouds or out-of-focus kites drift slowly across the frame. The image then cuts to the back of a young man’s head, followed by an audio clip from Edward Yang’s Yi Yi (2000), where the child Yangyang explains his desire to photograph people from behind, “I want to tell people things they don’t know. Show them things they cannot see.” Wu made a deliberate connection as Yu, now grown up, is played by Jonathan Chang, the actor for Yangyang.

Like Yang’s film, Wu’s work reflects on the multiplicity of truth and what remains fundamentally unknowable beyond the limits of our perception. In this imagined homecoming, Yu reappears as an apparition, wandering through Hong Kong’s ferry terminals, playgrounds, bus terminals, markets, and MTR stations. Carrying a paper rabbit lantern, with a large cow lantern trailing behind him, he walks through the city in search of his lost past. Footage of children flying kites and playing on the swings momentarily brings back the beauty and innocence of childhood, only to be interrupted by Yu’s missing-person poster, identity photographs, and handwritten accounts, pulling the audience out of the grainy, almost-too-perfect dreamscape. In the voiceover, interviewees recount what they know of the boy, but their testimonies never establish what happened to him. Yu forever lives at the border between Hong Kong and the mainland, never arriving at a destination.

Chan Hau Chun’s The Memory of a Cicada is Longer than its Life (2024) takes on a more elegiac and political dimension. The film opens with abstract, blurred images of pedestrians and pigeons strolling through an urban park, accompanied by a soundscape of cicadas’ high-pitched buzz, swelling in rolling crescendos. The metallic whir eventually gives way to a harmonica that rises into a piercing wail, punctuated by heavy breathing, conjuring a body expending its remaining life force in sudden bursts of energy. The sound mixes with the ambient noise of traffic and the surrounding streets to form a disorientating chorus.

Handwritten text appears on screen alongside a repeated, muffled voiceover, introducing a woman who once stood in the park but is now gone. Made in 2024, the film alludes to Hong Kong’s 2019 pro-democracy movement and the subsequent arrests and prosecutions of over ten thousand protesters, activists, and student leaders. Chan draws an analogy with two life forms known for their intensity and brevity: the tung blossom (油桐花) reaches full bloom within days before breaking from the tree and falling to the earth, and the cicada sings through the summer heat over its lifespan of a few weeks. The cicada’s song can be understood as a cry for independence in the knowledge of its inevitable fate, much like those who have sacrificed their blood and sweat in the fight for freedom. In the final sequence, a woman submerged in water raises her arms in a posture reminiscent of the Goddess of Democracy, the iconic figure of the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests. As she is consumed by the tides and vanishes beneath the surface, the tung blossom she holds remains afloat. In Hakka literature and Taiwanese poetry, the flower is remembered for the way it “lifts its head,” holding its dignity until the very end.

“Form, Shadow, Motion: Selections from the Burger Collection, Hong Kong” screens this evening, November 4, at Anthology Film Archives.
