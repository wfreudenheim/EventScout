BRIDES OF DRACULA

BY BRIAN O'CONNELL
OCTOBER 31, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

“I am the great exponent of explicitness in horror,” the elderly Terence Fisher remarked in a 1975 interview with Cinefantastique. His early successes with the low-budget British studio Hammer Films, The Curse of Frankenstein (1957) and Dracula (1958), thrilled international audiences with their visceral displays of violence and sexuality, which were made all the more carnal because they were shot in Technicolor. Time has not dulled these films’ capacity to shock, but their impact today has less to do with the mere fact of their explicitness than with the sensitivity of Fisher’s direction. His cinema exists at a delicate intersection between delirious content and perfect clarity of form, training a lucid eye on perverse, irrational spectacles. The Brides of Dracula (1960), a loose sequel that surpasses its predecessor in beauty and intensity, offers one of the richest demonstrations of Fisher’s gifts.

The film is rife with erotic menace from its outset. A young schoolteacher (Yvonne Monlaur), waylaid in her journey through one of Hammer’s typical Mittel-European landscapes, takes refuge for the night in the castle of an old noblewoman (the great Martitia Hunt). Despite her host’s hospitality, it’s not long before she suspects all is not well—especially when she discovers the Baroness’s handsome son (David Peel) chained by the foot in his bedroom.

To say more would spoil half the fun. Suffice to say that seductions, stakings, and Van Helsing (Peter Cushing, piercing as ever) all arrive in due course. It’s the visual impressions, however, that grip the most. The dreamlike atmosphere of Bernard Robinson’s sets, expressionistically illuminated by lavender gels and red lampshades, is offset by the bracingly frontal presentation of the bodies and faces in frame. Fisher’s gaze shares something of Van Helsing’s forensic coolness. In contrast to the rhetoric of ambiguity and suggestion popularized in the genre by Val Lewton and Jacques Tourneur, his camera stares down every fanged grin and ambulatory corpse without wavering, casting the uncanny in a hard, plain light. Supernatural evil is treated almost empirically as a palpable and immediate presence in the world. Acts of salvation are equally vivid, as in the beautifully theatrical flourish of Van Helsing compassionately casting a scarlet curtain over a newly redeemed vampire. Far from the campiness that has long dogged Hammer’s reputation, Fisher stages these familiar rituals as solemnly as any filmmaker ever has. His great facility for dramatic and gestural compression, which he worked hard to preserve from the interference of producers by cutting in camera, infuses each moment with poignant, unsettling weight.

A complex moral vision animates this Gothic aesthetic. Good cannot triumph without first encountering evil intimately. Even Van Helsing receives a cruel kiss from damned lips, for whose obscene pleasure he repents with an act of self-punishment no less awful or sublime. Purity and passion are intrinsically entwined in Fisher’s twilit world. The Brides of Dracula accordingly ends with a mystic transfiguration of space summative of his cinema: a massive windmill turned makeshift cross, ablaze with undying infernal flame.

The Brides of Dracula screens this evening, October 31, at Metrograph on 35mm as part of the series “Terence Fisher: Hammer’s Master Smith.”
