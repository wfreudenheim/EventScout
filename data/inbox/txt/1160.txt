50 Years of Free Expression with
Franklin Furnace

Nov 18th, 2026

Printed Matter is pleased to welcome Franklin Furnace founder and book editor Martha Wilson to celebrate Back to the Present: 50 Years of Free Expression, published by Princeton Architectural Press.

Marking the 50th anniversary of an arts organization that emerged from the same Downtown ethos as Printed Matter, this visually rich volume captures five decades of boundary-breaking art and the ongoing fight to defend creative freedom. The book traces Franklin Furnace’s history from its founding in downtown Manhattan in 1976, through the culture wars of the 1980s and ’90s, to today’s debates around censorship, free speech, and artistic freedom.

Join us for an evening of readings, cake, Martha Wilson Trivia, and more. Wilson will discuss Franklin Furnace’s history and her own experiences during the early culture wars, reflecting on the artists who challenged institutional norms and defended the power of art as a tool for social change. Throughout the evening, audience members will have the chance to win prizes for answering trivia questions correctly—or cleverly!

Tipper Gore’s Advice for the 90s

Martha Wilson

M. Wilson
New York, NY, 1993

$3

Martha Wilson’s 1993 poster sees her dressed as the wife of Vice President Al Gore, with wig, dress, choker, and heels. Clutching at her purse and smiling wholesomely, she delivers a plea to her fellow “creative artists” to allow the government to apply her warning labels (which were/are used in the record industry to warn parents of offensive material) to “risky” artworks.

Eric Copeland on Demon Portraits with Dem Passwords

Book Launch
Thursday, November 19, 6–8PM

Join us for the New York launch of Demon Portraits, a letter-by-letter, cut-and-paste work written over Aldous Huxley’s Brave New World. Working from four identical paperback copies, Eric Copeland cut and pasted letters over the existing narrative, using correction fluid to obscure others. The process required more than 11,000 hours over three and a half years, with Huxley’s text guiding and restricting every word. Moving between poetry and prose, the resulting work reflects Copeland’s practice as a visual artist and a member of bands including Black Dice.
