Dear readers,

“From high above — from the viewpoint of an omniscient narrator, such as existed in the 19th century, or one of those camera-equipped drones that exist in the 21st, which certain male students love to fly among the maples and conifers of Westish College, past the grassy practice fields and big stone bowl of the football stadium, far out over the rippling blue-gray water of Lake Michigan — you could see, in the early September dusk, amid the dark rectangles of roofs and the deep-green treetops, the bustle of preparations for the Parties.”

So begins a new piece of fiction by Chad Harbach, published in n+1’s Fall 2026 issue and excerpted from his long-awaited novel, The Brightness, which is out in just under a month. Readers may remember Westish College as the luxuriantly described setting of Harbach’s beloved and bestselling debut, The Art of Fielding—a setting Harbach returns to in The Brightness, with a cast of new and familiar characters who, as Tom Perrotta put it, “feel as real as your own roommates.” 

In “The Parties”—excerpted from the novel and newly out from behind the paywall in Issue 54—it’s September at Westish. The baseball players we met in The Art of Fielding are just settling into the school year, as are other students, including Irma, Dahlia, and Pammy, a trio of young feminists. 

> The entire student body seemed to be out, thronging the street and flowing in and out of the biggest tumbledown houses. Voices buzzed like hordes of insects behind the fences that girdled backyards. Students milled on weedy front lawns, sipping from giant cups, the sparse grass already churned to mud by footsteps and spilled beer.
>
> Several raucous games of cornhole were in progress. A beanbag the color of Dahlia’s lipstick sailed through the air, its lumpiness shifting and contorting in flight, and skidded up the gentle slope of a wooden ramp. A group of girls skipped down the middle of the street, elbows linked as in a musical, long strands of colorful beads swaying across their dresses. They looked so young to Irma — freshmen, she guessed, who’d met last week at orientation and glommed together with sleepaway-camp intensity. The boys they skipped past wore gaudy party shirts splashed with birds and pineapples, or T-shirts, or no shirts at all, their chests hairless and tan. A football thrown from a rooftop clipped a power line, spun like a helicopter seed, and dropped into the crowd.
>
> There were speakers everywhere, pointed out upper-story windows, throbbing on porches, wedged in the crooks of trees, and the sum of so much blasted music composed a surprisingly gentle mélange above which burbled the happy shouts and shrieks of classmates greeting each other after a summer apart. So this, Irma thought, was the Parties.
>
> Dahlia turned up the walk of a ramshackle house whose massive double doors stood open, admitting a steady stream of partygoers. She slid aside to let the boys behind them enter and plunked down on the porch swing. Smiling, she undid the clasp on her purse and, unflappably, as though at home alone, peered into her compact mirror as she reworked her magnificent lipstick.
>
> Far out over the lake, unharried by human light, hung a white half-moon with a pumiced convex edge. A surge of nervousness washed over Irma.
>
> “Do I look OK?” she asked.
>
> “You look great,” said Dahlia without glancing up.
>
> From an Altoids tin in her bag, Dahlia fished out an oblong white pill and snapped its middle seam. She held out her palm, offering Irma one of the chalky semi-ovals. Irma shook her head. Dahlia shrugged and tossed both halves in her mouth. She wore a look of steady purpose Irma had never seen. “Ready?” she asked.
