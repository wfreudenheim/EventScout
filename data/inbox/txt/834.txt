BROADCAST COMES ASHORE

OCEANIC ELEGIES AND THE DEATH MARCH OF PROGRESS.

Pioneer Works

Oct 16

 

READ IN APP

 

This fall, we’ve considered the ocean from many thrilling vantages—as the site of octopus dreams and psychoanalytic inquiry, the miraculous setting of whale births and manta-ray encounters, and the cradle of life itself. The sea’s boundlessness has inspired us, and we’ve journeyed toward its unexplored depths and endangered edges. But as our OCEANS series comes to a close, our attention is fixed on endings of every scale.

Environmental historian Matthew Shutzer examines the dawn of climate science in oceanography—a study of planetary destruction born of the same seas that inspired John Steinbeck to write, “Man is the only animal whose interest and whose drive are outside himself.” Meanwhile, the remarkable poet Ishion Hutchinson ruminates on where that drive has brought us, and what it’s taken away. His poem is an elegy to worlds lost to that “vicious leveller Progress”—an ode to fatal geographies and the difficult joys of homecoming.

Dive In

Like Tomato Soup
By Matthew Shutzer

The American chemists at Bikini Atoll treated their laboratory like a toilet. Broken beakers and dirty flasks decorated the field office. Inside, a general dampness pervaded, with evidence of sand and seawater, plates of unfinished food, and sweat-soaked clothing draped against tables and chairs. Coral souvenirs, emptied from the scientists’ khaki pockets, littered the space. This was certainly not up to the standards of a military operation, but that was partially the point. Bikini was late to the total war of the 1940s. The American and Japanese armies had fought their Pacific campaigns on different atolls, and though encircled by military supply lines, Bikini experienced none of the exploded ordinance or brutal killing of Kwajalein or Enewetak. Miraculously, it had been preserved for thermonuclear experimentation.

This at least was the impression held by Roger Revelle, the imposing six foot four American oceanographer the U.S. Navy dispatched to Bikini in 1946 to oversee initial lab teams for Operation Crossroads, the first nuclear test after the Second World War. Revelle and his team were charged with making geophysical and hydrographic surveys for the 23 nuclear weapons that would come to be tested on Bikini and its ecosystem of living coral islets between 1946 and 1958. He would often write and lecture about himself as a modern-day Ulysses, personifying the world spirit of ancient mariners seeking truth and homeland from saltwater. It was from his studies of the sea and the irradiated lagoon at Bikini that, remarkably, Revelle would develop one of the first scientific theories of how fossil fuel emissions impacted the planetary carbon cycle, published in a foundational co-authored paper on greenhouse warming in 1957. Later at Harvard in the 1960s, Revelle taught a young Al Gore, becoming a mentor to the future senator and inspiring Gore’s 1992 senatorial tome, Earth in the Balance: Ecology and the Human Spirit.

The Mariner’s Progress (Revisited)
By Ishion Hutchinson

Paramin. Lalibela. Delft. Other names are blurring through the poem—Boston, Santa Cruz—places where I have stayed long and to which I have returned many times over two or more decades. There are the names of places which are home because they are the places of my childhood: Stoney Hill, Port Antonio. I have seen things I thought would outlast me vanish—for instance, the great bamboo passage leading into the town of Port Antonio from the east, taken not by the sea or by a hurricane, but by the vicious leveller Progress.

Gone like Emmaus, a place lost to time. Perhaps that is why, from that geographic loss, the poem is an elegy, or it has elegiac shading, even though, ultimately, it is an attempt to celebrate with oceanic clarity and indistinctness the difficult joy of homecoming. To once again, before it is gone, scale the devil’s altitude of Paramin, in Trinidad, and see the bawled-out blue of the Caribbean Sea.
