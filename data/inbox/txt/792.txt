THE FREELANCE YEARS: A CONVERSATION WITH J. HOBERMAN

BY MARK ASCH
OCTOBER 14, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

“After My De-Voice,” the introduction of J. Hoberman’s new collection of film criticism Across the Movi-Verse (2026), takes us back to the first week of 2012, when the venerable alternative weekly The Village Voice, well into its terminal decline, laid off their senior film critic (and union steward). A regular contributor since 1977, and full-time staffer since the 1980s, Hoberman was thrust into the brave new world of freelance, balancing the necessity of making a living with the abiding interests he had been free to pursue, with greater or lesser interference, for 30-odd years.

Hoberman had a beat at the Voice—he began covering the downtown avant-garde film scene, and returned, as much as the release cycle would allow, to related, crypto-autobiographical subjects that included Cold War politics, postwar Pop, subculture and counterculture, mass communication, and Jewish-American life and politics—and a style, based on a time-consuming, research-intensive historical approach which is wildly cost-inefficient for a writer who doesn’t have health insurance through work.

His new book, published by The Film Desk, begins with several pieces, many originally published in Film Comment in the middle of the Obama years, that revisit the beginnings of his foundations as a critic and the beginnings of his time at the Voice. It also collects book reviews, retrospectives, obituaries, and a very tightly curated selection of essays about new releases, taking the reader through Donald Trump’s first election, the Covid-19 pandemic (via a piece for the Jewish magazine Tablet on plague imagery, xenophobia, and 1922’s Nosferatu), and the emergence of second-screen content.

I Zoomed with Hoberman last week to talk about his career and what he’s watching these days, two topics that, as every freelance film critic knows, are actually the same.

Mark Asch: The introduction, which is the most recent piece of writing in the book, is about the difference between being staff and freelance. I remember you saying once, in an interview or talk, something like, “Some weeks it’s a struggle to have an opinion.” Not everyone is vocationally reactive, like Pauline Kael. Is that feeling something you remember?

J. Hoberman: I do remember saying something like that. What I was getting at was that, even though I had a tremendous amount of freedom at the Voice, the available films are not always equally interesting. And, I had an obligation as the senior critic or lead critic to cover things which I might not really have wanted to.

I think I also said that after I lost my job, the best thing about it was that I would never have to look at another Spider-Man film again.

MA: That’s quite sad coming from you, because you’re a contemporary of Peter Parker, right? You’re both from Queens. You’re about the same age.

JH: There’s no doubt that Marvel comics were a remarkable development in the mid-60s when they appeared. I was, as a Queens kid, totally with the idea of a superhero from Forest Hills, and did enjoy the attitude [of Spider-Man], but I was more taken with [Steve] Ditko’s other comic, Doctor Strange. I got more interested in underground comics once they appeared, but I followed [Jack] Kirby.

MA: In the piece in the book about Manny Farber, from 2010, you wrote, “The 750-word weekly film review is a form of journalism. Gathered together, short pieces written over a period of months and years become a chronicle of an individual sensibility.” In 2012, you also characterize Siegfried Kracauer as “a nonacademic intellectual” who had to do a lot of piecework, and say that, despite being friends with [Walter] Benjamin and [Theodor] Adorno, that his work was “essentially journalistic.” These pieces were written immediately before and after the end of your time at the Voice, and they echo what you say in the intro about you seeing your work as a form of journalism. Was that true consistently for the Voice decades?

JH: It’s not like I had a job anywhere, prior to the Voice. I had written some things for, you know, National Screw, Monster Times, Crawdaddy, the New York Ace, the Berkeley Barb. A fairly motley bunch of publications. But the Voice offered me a regular spot, which I turned into a beat.

[Arts editor] Richard Goldstein had done a film supplement and had wanted an overview of avant-garde film. So I did that. He liked me. Jonas [Mekas] had left in ‘74, so he had been gone for like three years, but [Goldstein] understood historically that the Voice had covered avant-garde film. He suggested that I do that.

Initially, I was able to generate my own assignments. The Whitney had film shows, MoMA had Cineprobes, there was the precursor to Anthology, the Film Makers’ Cinematheque, the Millennium, a bunch of venues where I could cover things. So I would suggest that, assign myself this, maybe every other week, and they gave me more space because I never missed a deadline. I delivered clean copy. I was a very reliable writer; I was not a temperament. So, I was able to expand my purview. And there were things which neither [Andrew] Sarris nor Tom Allen had the slightest bit of interest in covering—sometimes to my utter amazement. I was so excited when Celine and Julie Go Boating [1974] got a commercial opening and these guys didn't care.

MA: Something in your Eraserhead [1977] and Killer of Sheep [1978] piece that gives me a real pang of recognition is about the expectation that you're the young person who is ostensibly on the cutting edge, but you're in the wrong scene to actually flag something new as major. [As Hoberman wrote in one of the first-person Film Comment pieces republished in the book, he led his inaugural Village Voice column with Jon Jost’s Angel City, with a few words on Eraserhead as the second feature; he likewise writes of his regrets in using his entire word count on a Miklós Janscó retrospective the week that the Whitney played Killer of Sheep.] Did it feel like things moved slower, that there was more time to consider work across multiple weeks or issues, to observe a phenomenon as it emerged? Or was it the nature of that early, marginal beat that things would come and go very fast?

JH: I adjusted to the rhythm of a weekly. The thing that was the biggest breakthrough for me was when I did an entire feature on Super-8 punk films; that unfolded over a couple of months, looking at these films, talking to filmmakers.

I had another thing like that with [Hans-Jurgen] Syberberg’s Hitler, because I had seen a very early screening of that, on hard chairs at the Goethe House. I was hyper-prepared when it was about to open on a larger market. Jeanne Dielman [1975] is another example. I’d actually written about it two or three times before, but when it got a run, I knew that I could do something with that.

MA: I’ll get out of the ‘70s in a second, but it is kind of amazing to me that your first two Voice columns as a regular contributor were Eraserhead and News from Home [1976]. 

JH: Yeah! The thing is, Eraserhead was totally unexpected. I had never heard of David Lynch. I just went to the Cinema Village on a Friday or Saturday night. It was almost totally empty, there were like 10 people in the audience. But News From Home I had heard about, at least. Jackie Raynal showed me that in her apartment. That was something that I was psyched to do. In my allegiance to the preexisting avant-garde I put this Brakhage film first, The Governor [1977], which is not uninteresting… I didn’t lead with News From Home, which, journalistically, would have been much more important.

MA: News from Home, the 52nd greatest film of all time, a film on both our Sight & Sound ballots. What was Akerman’s stature at that point?

JH: She’d been in New York and people knew her. I knew her cinematographer, Babette Mangolte. There was this colossal word of mouth coming from some circles in Europe about Jeanne Dielman, starting in ‘75. I was completely knocked out by News from Home; if I had been more experienced, I would have known how to position it. It’s a New York film, it should have been a huge deal in the Village Voice. But Stan Brakhage was somebody I covered a lot in those early days and was still the most complicated and in some ways radical figure in American avant-garde film…

MA: In book reviews republished in Across the Movi-Verse, you talk about Farber’s and Kracauer’s and Buñuel’s and Godard’s teaching and speaking and editing and distribution gigs. There’s an awareness that even geniuses still have to work, and how the need for money shapes the output and ambition of even visionary artists.

It's interesting to think about a critic, on a journalistic beat, who can still put together a substantial body of work despite the contingencies of having to write for money. So, I guess I should ask you about how you put this book together—it has thematic chapters and a fairly satisfying flow, despite being a collection of journalistic piecework. You are still a reasonably prolific freelancer, but you were pretty selective about what ended up in the book, right?

JH: I tinkered with it quite a bit. It comes out of being very protective of these pieces. I was told by my original publisher, Temple University Press, which put out two anthologies [of my criticism], that these anthologies just didn’t work anymore. But I had done all this stuff in the decade after leaving the Voice that I wanted to put between covers somehow. Just having [them] out there in cyberspace didn’t seem real to me. I suggested it to Jake Perlin [of The Film Desk] and I was glad that they went for it.

But if you ask how I did it [i.e., created a body of work as a freelancer], basically, I, again, lucked into this sinecure which, although it was brief, was substantial. When Dave Kehr left [The New York] Times, they, almost with an air of desperation, asked me, would I like his job [as the weekly DVD columnist]. Are you kidding? I mean, this was, in a way, the easiest job I ever had, and it was like $1,000 a column. That was the bridge for me between the Voice and Social Security.

You know, I don’t think I have to elaborate on the misery of being—it’s one thing to be a freelance writer when you’re 25 or 30. It’s another thing when you’re in your 50s, late 50s, to be doing that, you know? I was able to sell pieces, I was able, for a while, to sell things to Harper’s, and so on; the money was good, but it’s not like you can live on that.

MA: It’s funny, if you said, it’s one thing to freelance when you’re 25, it’s another thing to freelance when you’re in your 50s to a young person who’s freelancing, they’d say, yeah: it’s worse when you’re younger, because you know you have to do it for 40 more years instead of 10.

JH: [Laughs] If that’s what it is, although, you know—

MA: Yeah, I know, there’s always law school. But it also does change. It’s gotten worse for all of us since 2012. Has it gotten worse for you, in the time you’ve been freelancing?

JH: Sure, I think it’s bad for everybody. I used to enjoy teaching what I considered practical film criticism. I taught it three times, and some seminars at other places, but I taught it intermittently at NYU. The last time I taught it was 2008, and that’s when I realized: I was teaching this as if, and I was hoping that, my students would be able to go out and get jobs. That’s what I thought it was, the first two times I taught it. By 2008, there were some people in the class who were able to make careers, but the field had severely contracted by then, so I didn’t want to teach it anymore. I didn't want to teach it as a hypothetical thing, I wanted it to be practical.

MA: In terms of the curation of this book, it’s necessarily a portrait of the times from about 2012 up to about midway through the Biden administration. Were you thinking about putting in pieces that you felt would have the most retrospective historical value, or that you were proudest of, or that made a portrait of your interests?

JH: All of those, but the heart of the book, the things that I really wanted in there before anything else, were a series of pieces that I did for Gavin Smith at Film Comment. There were maybe half a dozen of those in there. And some longer pieces that I did for ArtForum, mainly for Don McMahon. And, as I say in the book, these were things that weren’t pegged. I just got interested in something, and these editors were generous enough to give me space, and also with a minimum of interference. So I was living off my capital, in a way, having been a quasi-famous film critic.

Then there were other things that I did that I was pleased with and that I wanted to include. Although, I’m not sure what the staying value of 12 Years a Slave [2013] is now. Or No. I don’t know whether those films seem as important now as they did then, but maybe that will change. I really liked that piece about The Godfather, which I had written for Harper’s and they turned it down. I was very grateful to Tablet for running it—I enjoyed writing for them, but then the political gulf was too wide for me to bridge.

MA: So, I did have a question about that. You wrote for Tablet, you did good work for them. You haven’t written anything new for them in the last three years. I was curious how intentional that was.

JH: I had always had deep disagreements with what I perceived as their editorial stance. But if I wanted to write about Diane Arbus, I could do that; if I wanted to write about Todd Haynes, I could do that. I did do some things that were critical. There was a documentary, The Settlers [2017], which I wrote about, and I didn’t get any pushback. I think they liked having me there, some other voice. But then after October 7, they wanted me to write something, be involved in something about Carlos the Jackal, and I wanted to see how things developed. Their politics just got more and more extreme.

Listen, I never grew up believing in “America, right or wrong,” and I certainly did not believe in Israel, let alone Benjamin Netanyahu, right or wrong. I just could not be associated with that. Too much of a Voice writer, in the end.

MA: Based on the stuff that's being published on there now, you got out at the right time. 

JH: I dread thinking what they're going to accuse NAZA of—being created by AI?

MA: Something I found myself thinking a lot about when reading this book is what constitutes the “marginal.” In your piece about Eraserhead and Killer of Sheep you write that “new ideas in motion pictures typically arrive from the so-called margins.” This is a theme of the book as it relates to your professional history and many of your abiding interests, so it does lead me to ask about your current cultural consumption. Some of the more recent pieces in the book are about what you call “vernacular video,” after Peter Snowdon. You’re clearly interested in work that metabolizes an instant archive being created on TikTok and Instagram Reels and YouTube—is that something you feel like you have to work backwards into when you’re dealing with work that engages with it? Does it feel natural to engage with that stuff?

JH: I teach one seminar a year at Columbia and for the past six or seven years, the subject has been documentary activism. There’s also a book I’m working on for Fireflies on this Chinese documentary, Disorder, which came out in 2009. Documentary is much more what I think about, these days. I really do think there's a documentary avant-garde. In China, there definitely was, although it ended in 2011—right when I lost my job at the Voice, Xi came to power and just terminated this whole unofficial culture. It’s really kind of shocking.

The two things that are in the book, Shooting and Storming of the U.S. Capitol [2021] by Jayden X, and the French documentary Monopoly of Violence [2020], had a certain sense of immediacy that I wanted. That’s where a lot of my thinking is, in terms of what’s happening or what’s interesting to me in film right now. This is something the New York Film Festival has been good on for the past few years, in the Currents section: avant-garde documentaries. Obviously I don’t like everything that I see there, but I like the tendency. These are things which accept the world that we’re dealing with now.

Once in a while, there’s something that I like. Coyote vs. Acme, or I don’t know if one could consider the latest Alex Cox film to be a Hollywood movie. There’s a few big-budget things, Sinners [2025], but basically I don’t have much interest in that. The same thing is true to a large degree of foreign art films. There were some that I saw in the New York Film Festival that I like, but they're not as interesting to me as documentaries that are trying to map out cyberspace or whatever, this new arena. I wrote about the digital turn years ago, although the book came out in 2011, and I still think there was something that profoundly changed in the nature of motion pictures. I guess these vernacular videos are “marginal,” although I have a student who told me he was doing his PhD thesis on vernacular video. So, I guess it’s not that marginal at this point.

Probably I’m crankier in a way. I had this conversation last night with my wife — I amassed this wonderful DVD collection, thanks to the New York Times. And so I put on something like Ride the Pink Horse [1947], and I get so much more out of this ancient Hollywood movie than anything that they produce now. And I’m not saying I think that it’s better, it’s just where my head is at.

In that Eraserhead/Killer of Sheep piece from Film Comment, you ask, rhetorically, “Who’s to say that comparably original works are not now being produced at the margins? But in order to find them one would have to hack through the jungle of student films, amateur movies, fan sites, and, as proposed by Howard Hampton in this journal's July/August issue, YouTube—or worse.” That “or worse” we now know as Twitch streams, Discord servers, online fandoms. For myself and my colleagues, I think we’d like to believe that it’s possible to write about contemporary mass communication without our attention being totally captured by it, but I’m not sure it’s true. 

JH: Look, we're all products of our particular moment in time. Manny Farber said this amazing thing to Jonathan Rosenbaum: he said, in essence, that he was like a young person during the Depression, and he never got over it.

I could never keep up with stuff. I still get The Nation—although I find I like it much less now that it's a monthly, I liked it much better when it was every other week—and somebody’s writing about some independent filmmaker from the West coast who made a communist crime movie. I’m reading the thing and I’m saying, “Why don’t I know about this?” I don’t know about it because I’m not functioning on the edge of these things. I’m much more of a termite, following my own particular path.

That was both the curse and the blessing of being a weekly critic. I was obligated to know about everything. And I prided myself on it. That’s how I felt I could keep my job.

MA: To continue thinking about the new or marginal, in your quickly written response to the first time Trump was elected in 2016, you wrote that “the candidate most adroit in deploying new communications technology has pretty much always prevailed… Donald Trump, a long established television personality, was defined by Twitter. The next new media will perhaps be some form of virtual reality.” I think, 10 years on, we can say, virtual reality, AI slop, close enough, we count that. The major development that immediately follows the historical period covered in this book is generative AI. I’m curious if that’s something you would ever write about, or if there's anything you’ve seen that has used it that you feel is at all valuable.

JH: I had a back-and-forth with Radu Jude in Film Comment which was largely about AI. I found Dracula [2025] fascinating and horrible. It really was the most disturbing film I had seen in a while, but I think this guy is great. This even goes back further into the digital turn and CGI: how can you believe what you’ve seen? The truth claim that cinema once had, and photography, no matter how complicated—it’s been completely overthrown. That is of interest to me.

ASH: I guess this is a good time to mention that both you and your wife, Sarah Fensom, are film critics. Sarah is also in this film and worked on it with you as a producer. She plays the person doing aerobics next to the pool.

CS: Nothing is possible without Sarah because, on the practical level, we're two Italian-Americans who are talking constantly, from the moment we wake up to the moment we go to bed. Once Morbid Weekend became a real possibility, we were just talking about it all the time. She is like the co-author in a very significant way.

So, with her doing aerobics… The people in the film, they're all shot in their spaces. That's where they live, and I really like that because I hate art direction and production design. I wanted the actual world in the film to be as interesting on screen as it is to me. Why fake it? Sarah is actually the only one who is not in a space where she lives but the part is tailored to her because she's obsessed with vintage aerobics videos. We were talking and I asked what's something that would be funny or interesting for you to do?

ASH: Yes, we see her doing aerobics by a pool at night as the pool is cleaned by some kind of automatic pool-cleaning device. You say you are not interested in art direction or production design, but one thing that's clear in this film is that you are interested in wardrobe and makeup.

CS: Yes, very.

ASH: One of the main characters in the film, who is played by Rosie Wilt, who is really excellent in it, I guess is a professional clown like Anne Archer was in Short Cuts?

CS: She is not a professional clown. She is actually a computer engineer...

SF: A software engineer.

CS: She’s a friend we knew from NY who designs and wears these incredible looks for Halloween or her birthday or what not. Her makeup and her costume were her own. I got the idea for her to do the clown and the other costumes she's in because Sarah and I went to this Rob Zombie, Alice Cooper, and Ministry concert. I love Rob Zombie unapologetically. We had a great time and I never do this, but I looked at Rob Zombie's Instagram after the show and there was this person posting in the comments about how she tailors her looks to honor Sheri Moon, the actor Zombie is married to. I was like, "What is this?" So I went to this person's profile, and they're a cosplayer for metal and horror stuff. I thought it was really interesting, and I liked how elaborate it was. So I thought, "What if Rosie, who's really good at doing these looks, what if that's her form of mourning for her dead boyfriend?"

ASH: She performs various rituals as these characters, or just hangs around her apartment in the outfits.

CS: I wanted to limit it to three things, because I believe in a Ramones form of verse-chorus-bridge. Rosie and I limited her mourning scenes and outfits to three figures: a clown, Kurt Cobain, and Jackie Kennedy.

ASH: That's an odd group, a clown, Kurt Cobain, and Jacqueline Kennedy. Wilt's performance is sometimes just her sitting back in a chair, or playing videogames. The chair shot could serve as the primary image of the film. Then there's that very brief moment where she's playing the videogame and the character in the game starts laughing, like it's laughing at her as a clown. That was very haunted. The film does have a contemplative and funeral sense. We see a funeral in the film and you returned to that graveyard a couple of times. 

CS: As far as that videogame moment, that is, again, just working with someone in their house and in their interests. I asked Rosie what are some things that you could do while you're mourning, and she had an old Sega Genesis. I remembered that game Altered Beast where a guy says "Rise from your grave." It's a happy accident. I said play the game and then that moment becomes a transition. As for the funeral stuff, I am a taphophile. I love cemeteries.
