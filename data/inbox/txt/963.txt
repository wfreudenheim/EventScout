SAN SEBASTIAN 2026

BY VADIM RIZOV
OCTOBER 28, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

My San Sebastián Film Festival began with a homecoming of sorts: Dust, Tsai Ming-liang’s twelfth installment in his “Walker” series, arriving fresh from its Venice premiere. As with every installment beginning with 2014’s Journey to the West, Dust stars Tsai’s career-long muse Lee Kang-sheng as a monk walking, as slowly as possible, through various cities—in this case, San Sebastián. I was able to glean from audience laughter that Tsai’s introduction was laced with self-deprecating jokes about how, although Dust might seem long, it was still shorter than an afternoon siesta.

He certainly read the room right, but novelty of local investment aside, the results confirmed a dark suspicion I’ve been nursing for a while: one of the world’s great filmmakers is, if not getting worse precisely, definitely getting lazy. Lee’s presence is a locus for crowds; their gawking at him is grist for Tsai’s camera, rearranging the composition of the frame without the filmmaker having to do anything. (One of the series’ inadvertent indexical functions is to track the growing prevalence of smartphone culture; 12 years ago, people were more inclined to just gaze than snap.) The monk is an object example of the increasingly explicit Buddhism underpinning Tsai’s work, a living example of a sustained contemplation of the world around us and an externalization of the filmmaker’s own hyper-attentive methods, in which durationally attentive rigor yields beautiful results.

But these shots are relatively short, prone to random deployment of handheld, and betray the gaze of a hasty visitor. I read up on Basque Spain for a while before I arrived in the region, and I guess Tsai did too: he goes to a hardcore show (the genre is deep in the area’s DNA) and films it in a lazily handheld fashion that would be rightly rejected by any professional content-creation company. To be fair, Tsai travels far outside fest central; I assumed the movie would act as a kind of preview of sculptures and buildings I would see over the days to come, but they remained unknown to me by the time I left. All these sculptures, of which San Sebastián is full, are carefully listed at the end; exported in textual form, this might form a solid afternoon’s itinerary, but it’s not much of a movie. Having been told that Tsai receives $100,000 to $300,000 for each film in this series, which comes with the presumably desirable bonus of paid travel to new locales, I’m not surprised that he keeps at it. But though Tsai has certainly earned the money and his legacy is secured many times over, he needn’t phone it in so flagrantly.

A more satisfactory form of local representation came from Chilean-born filmmaker Thomas Woodroffe, currently enrolled at the Zine Eskola graduate film program which operates out of the Tabakalera, the multi-functional arts space that serves as one of the festival’s venues. Woodroffe is clearly hooked up in his home country: his debut feature Erratics (pictured at top) lists, in its credits, just about every single working Chilean filmmaker I know of. (Close enough geographically, the best credit is actually for a great Argentine filmmaker performing in an unexpected capacity: “Catering: Martín Rejtman.”)

Erratics concerns the legacy of French filmmaker Lucien Castelnau, a fictional French equivalent to Robert Flaherty played by Denis Lavant; as the film begins, his magnum opus has been re-discovered, and Castelnau skulks around the restoration house, unseen by the people discussing his work. One strong point in Erratics’s favor: the fictional counterpart of Nanook of the North here is the most convincingly faked archival footage since Peter Jackson’s Forgotten Silver (1995). Insofar as the film has a narrative throughline (it’s mostly a vibe-piece), it’s about juxtaposing the rediscovery of Castelnau’s anthropological document with footage of present-day Patagonia, both segments vaguely circling questions of the colonial gaze. A highlight is a set visit to Felipe Gálvez’s The Settlers (2023)—a narrative film explicitly about colonialist genocide in Chile, and thus resonant with the larger concerns—that’s one of the better such demystifying scenes, observing onset boredom and cold crew members shivering and waiting as one actor’s falling-down is repeatedly worked through. Filmmaking is tedious! On a conceptual rather than visceral level, I have to also applaud the particularly winnowing-down of the ending, which presents the full faked silent film in true silence, leading to much audience shuffling toward the doors as the faux-anthropology ground on. There’s a little too much inadvertent downtime beforehand, but if I were a film programmer, I’d be scouting Woodroffe’s future work.

The festival’s engagement with Latin America as a whole included the timely gesture of showing a bumper decrying legislation that threatens INCAA, Argentina’s government-administered funding body, by removing three specific sources of funding. The trailer doesn’t mince words: if said legislation passes, film production in Argentina will cease. Whether this is hyperbole is beyond my paygrade to evaluate, but I have no trouble believing that this is the latest salvo intending something to that effect from Javier Milei’s administration, which already attempted to defund the institution fully, first with a 2024 omnibus bill that failed and later by allowing all work contracts to expire. I fully support the gesture (undertaken at the request of the Argentine Film Academy) to draw attention to the situation; with all filmmakers’ approval, the bumper was shown before four different Argentine films. I saw three of those, during which the spot drew a nice hand every time: Locarno Golden Leopard-winner The Illusion of an Everlasting Summer, which sets some kind of single-film record for being shot over 25 years, and Berlinale carry-over The River Train, a sprightly quasi-comedy slightly under Jacques Rivette’s spell that definitely isn’t another slab off the slow cinema block.

The premiere to boast the bumper beforehand was María Alché and Benjamín Naishtat’s documentary Pedro in the Land. Naishtat is currently representing Argentina worldwide, having arrived straight from the TIFF premiere of his fifth narrative feature, Glaxo; the day after the festival ended, Argentina chose The Virgin of Quarry Lake (which he co-wrote) as the country’s submission for this year’s Academy Awards. In this context, Pedro in the Land represents a curious way to blow off steam from the presumably higher-budgeted rigors of Glaxo, a ’50s-’80s period piece with lots of production design recreating Argentina in Brazil.

By contrast, the 61-minute Pedro begins with a stylistically anomalous five-minute prologue, a symphony-of-a-city montage of Mexico City backed by voiceover readings of letters exchanged between Peronian resistance leader John William Cooke (Mariano Llinás) and his wife Alicia Eguren (Lucía Aduriz) as they were separately shuffled from prison to prison. The remainder consists of an extended interview with their son, Pedro Catella, literally cutting around him as he talks at great length, splitting the difference between Jean Eustache’s Number Zero and a slower-paced Michael Bay as the sunlight goes down and their subject has to move towards the window. Only slightly leavened by cutaways to archival stills, the result is an unremittingly deep-dive into intersectional political history, hopscotching among the finer points of Argentina’s post-Peronian history, Bolivia and Cuba. Anticipating my slightly bewildered response, the film ends with a stern reproof from its historical advisor, Miguel Mazzeo, that lesser-known political movements are often dismissed as insignificant “because they bring into play modes of understanding that are not colonized, distinct from those of populism or the dogmatic Left.” Fair enough! I wouldn’t say the results are for me, but I’ve described them precisely because, both politically and aesthetically, they’re certainly for somebody.

A retrospective spoke even more to the present moment than the festival’s new programming, albeit in a nasty way. Felipe Cabrerizo, a longtime sidebar retrospective curator for the festival, assembled a 20-film series of works written and/or directed by José Giovanni, the French writer who broke into the film industry by adapting his novels into two staples, Jacques Becker’s The Hole (1960) and Claude Sautet’s Classe tour risques (1960), before transitioning into his own directing career, during which he made many commercially successful films starring iconic French men: Lino Ventura, Alain Delon and Jean-Paul Belmondo. But Giovanni’s rap sheet before film included extorting hidden Jews during World War II and collaboration; when the full details were finally revealed he basically brazened it out. The crimes are grody, and although Giovanni was officially rehabilitated, I think it’s fair to say that he never was morally. A retrospective of his work in France is almost certainly impossible.

In liberal Spain, however: not a problem—and, in an unsavory sense, to treat him as a reclaimable master is exactly on global political schedule. Though not listed as such on the festival’s site, quite a few of these titles screened on 35mm (the giveaway: they’re programmed in Principe auditorium 6). Once I picked up on this, and that they would almost never find their way to me (let alone their nation of origin), some rapid schedule rearranging occurred. Not all of these fascinate; Giovanni’s earlier efforts give the sense of a director working primarily to protect his own script, and he never became precisely elegant with his camera movements. I was oddly taken with Where Did Tom Go? (1972), whose opening man-and-dog mountain romp is great fun. Regrettably, said German Shepherd’s screen time is curtailed as the movie proceeds, but their opening clamber is the first of several scenes showcasing Swiss-born Giovanni’s career-long fixation on extended scenes of mountain-climbing; I would trade a fair number of more respectable and better-made films for this film’s extended shots of Tom (mononymous star Rufus) clambering like a little chamois down Swiss slopes. The plot finds Tom abandoning his job at a UN-like institution which negotiates a little too politely for hostage release and trains himself into a political assassin for liberation; the film’s conclusion, expressed with a startling lack of dissimulation, is that political violence in the name of progress is by no means inconceivable or necessarily undesirable, and Giovanni’s clarity of purpose definitely blasts through some “but what about civilized norms” handwringing.

Even better was my series closer, Giovanni’s penultimate theatrical feature My friend the traitor (1988), a sort of apologia for himself. With handsome production values (courtesy of the now very-MeToo’d producer Alain Sarde), Giovanni presents the fairly hopeless story of collaborator Georges (Thierry Frémont), who attempts to rehabilitate himself by identifying and informing on his onetime Nazi conspirators. The French state, however, is hypocritical, eager to wash its hands of any complicity, and unfussy about how it does so, so Georges’ final fate is inevitably bad. The title’s unsavory twist is that it refers less to Georges—who, while volatile, is definitely presented as having his reasons and no meaningfully worse than any of his fellow French compatriots—and more to Adrien Rove (André Dussollier), the French military officer who offers him rehabilitation but ultimately proves chooses to advance his career at the expense of honoring his promises. The film’s “it’s not all my fault” posturing is, at a minimum, unnerving if not outright offensive (even Giovanni’s longtime collaborator Lino Ventura publicly balked). But it’s also not that far off from the diagnosis rendered by Giovanni’s one-time collaborator Jean-Pierre Melville in Léon Morin, Priest (1961) or Claude Chabrol’s Story of Women (1988), two films highlighting how a state which never purged itself of collaborators was happy to victimize, sometimes to death, women instead. That Giovanni’s motive was less righteous and more overtly self-pitying doesn’t invalidate his ultimate point.
