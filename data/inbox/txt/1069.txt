RAISE THE RED LANTERN

BY KATHERINE CONNELL
NOVEMBER 5, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

“To me, history and the present are the same,” Zhang Yimou told Film Quarterly in 1999. Belonging to the Fifth Generation of Chinese filmmakers, who had directly experienced the Cultural Revolution and whose training coincided with the PRC’s “reform and opening up period,” Zhang’s emergent work from the 1980s through the 1990s was controversial—and sometimes banned—in China while revered by international festivals, arthouse aficionados, and mainstream Anglo-American tastemakers.

Much like Zhang’s preceding first two films, Raise the Red Lantern (1991) limits its primary action to a single setting. Compared to the tall windswept fields in Red Sorghum (1987) or the unspooling Technicolor dye mill in Ju Dou (1990), there’s little vitality to this environment: a residential complex housing a wife and three concubines to a powerful, affluent man in 1920s China. On the roof, there’s a small room where two women of previous generations were said to have “hanged themselves.” If not literally haunted, the spectre of surveillance is evoked through Zhang’s approach to shooting this architectural enclosure of interlocking stone courtyards and empty alleyways. His mise-en-scène produces a maze of uniformity imposed through high angle shots, a relentless obfuscation of sky, and cascading doorframes that trap characters or sever them from each other. The women who animate this claustrophobic network learn to move with extreme caution. Their Master (Ma Jingwu) is nearly faceless, captured from afar or turned away. Each evening, a fictional ritual of lighting red lanterns announces in whose quarters he’ll spend the night.

Contrasting this emphasis on visual distance, the film’s opening scene is one of considerable intimacy. In a tight close-up, protagonist Songlian (Gong Li) addresses an unseen, recently widowed mother. “Let me be a concubine,” she declares, as her eyes brim with tears. “Isn’t that a woman’s fate?” Interpreted literally, it's a bleak outlook adopted by the film. Unlike the fieldworker solidarity that ultimately defines Red Sorghum, the taut patriarchal matrix of Raise the Red Lantern sees its women characters resort to brutal individualism to harness proximate power and desirability. Being chosen has perks: a rattling foot massage that echoes throughout the compound, selecting the lunch menu, and the superiority of favoritism. Within this strategic ecosystem, Songlian stumbles to find her stride. She misrecognizes allegiances, overplays her hand, refuses to flatter the Master, and abuses her maid Yan’er (Kong Lin).

Though Gong Li is Zhang’s most mythologized thespian collaborator, appearing in eight of his films to date, Raise the Red Lantern is something of an ensemble work, and really a two-hander with He Saifei, who plays Third Mistress Meishan, a former opera singer hiding an affair. Meishan’s introduction as a rival is something of a red herring, and though friendship with Songlian is a possibility that is dangled then horrifically dropped, her character is part of a recurrent interest across Zhang’s work in uncovering unexpected personalities beneath socially scripted roles that people are forced to perform. It’s somewhat surprising, then, that his later work has moved toward politically palatable, hyper-budgeted spectacles, including the Olympics. In the end, Songlian’s refusal to conform to her circumstances is both subversive and damning. Although the film doesn’t present a way out of its hegemonic world, it isn’t asking viewers to accept theirs.

Raise the Red Lantern runs November 6-19 at Film Forum.
