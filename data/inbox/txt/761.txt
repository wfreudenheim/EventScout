A LITTLE MACHINE THAT'S CREATING A MAGIC TRICK: CHRIS SHIELDS ON MORBID WEEKEND

BY A. S. HAMRAH
OCTOBER 10, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Chris Shields is a filmmaker and writer based in Los Angeles who often contributes to Screen Slate, Film Comment, Sight & Sound, Reverse Shot, and Film Quarterly. He also writes essays for Vinegar Syndrome and Cinématographe releases. He is a film programmer and a frequent moderator of Q&As at the American Cinematheque in Los Angeles and other venues.

With his new film, Morbid Weekend, Shields makes his narrative feature directorial debut. Set just outside LA, Morbid Weekend is a horror movie of a different sort. Using a range of experimental techniques, it obliquely tells the story of a pizza delivery driver (Greg Diaz) who dies in a car accident while working. He is memorialized and buried but still haunts the living, including his girlfriend (Rosie Wilt). Texting pizza delivery updates and dropping off pies, he watches them, unseen. His melancholic, ghostly stalking replicates the viewing experience of theater, the cinema, and surveillance. Or of just eating in front of the TV.

Shields and I spoke by phone on the eve of Morbid Weekend's New York debut. Our conversation has been edited for length and clarity. 

A. S. Hamrah: Your new movie, which is your first feature-length film, though it's a short feature at 69 minutes, is called Morbid Weekend. When I heard the title, it sounded to me like some kind of cross between a Jean Rollin film and The Ghost in the Invisible Bikini. But then I saw it and it's about the ghost of a dead pizza delivery guy who observes people in Southern California going on with their lives at night: photographing their pets, doing aerobics by the pool, practicing with their band in their garage. It's kind of a contemplative look, from the point of view of a dead man, into other people's lives. Do you have some experience with pizza delivery that made you want to make this? 

Chris Shields: I worked pizza delivery for several years. I grew up in Central Florida, where I was a pizza delivery guy for a bunch of different places. To me, it's a very emblematic job of a certain kind of scumbag, and if you were punk at that time, you probably delivered pizza. It sucks as a job, but at the same time it's a very contemplative occupation because you're just driving, usually at night. You're listening to music, and there is something cinematic about the experience.

Over the years, I've basically had every low-wage job that somebody could have, but pizza delivery is one that has stayed with me as having something cinematic and poetic about it. You could just drive through rural Florida and be listening to the harshest music, smoking cigarettes, and you're bringing someone's pizza to them. There was a kind of freedom and beauty there, so I was thinking it could be the site of something beautiful. Like what you said about Jean Rollin. Rollin deals in the dumbest subject matter possible—zombies and vampires—but his films are so beautiful. I'm a huge Grapes of Death and Living Dead Girl and Night of the Hunted fan. Those just live in the basement of my brain, so that was something that was there.

ASH: Just as Jean Rollin's films are often quite tied to Paris, Morbid Weekend is tied to the region in which you shot it, which is Southern California, but it's a part of Southern California that wasn't so recognizable to me as someone who doesn't live there. Where are we geographically in your film?

CS: It's the San Gabriel Valley, which is like a suburb outside of what we recognize as Los Angeles. I had some friends who lived there, and my wife Sarah and I would drive out there, and it reminded me of Florida, where I grew up. I love really empty spaces. I feel that my mind is more free in those places. The area was new to me because we had moved to Los Angeles relatively recently from New York City, and so it was novel and kind of spooky, a little haunted, but pastoral. I thought it would give me a certain freedom to work with that landscape

ASH: What towns are in the San Gabriel Valley? How far is this from where you live in Hollywood

CS: Rosemead is one of them. I'm asking my wife, Sarah. Where else?

Sarah Fensom: We also shot in Pasadena, which I don't think is considered San Gabriel Valley.

CS: But was close to that area. It's very much a suburb in its own way. We were, I'd say, 15 or 20 minutes on the highway outside LA. It feels disconnected in a sense.

ASH: I guess this is a good time to mention that both you and your wife, Sarah Fensom, are film critics. Sarah is also in this film and worked on it with you as a producer. She plays the person doing aerobics next to the pool.

CS: Nothing is possible without Sarah because, on the practical level, we're two Italian-Americans who are talking constantly, from the moment we wake up to the moment we go to bed. Once Morbid Weekend became a real possibility, we were just talking about it all the time. She is like the co-author in a very significant way.

So, with her doing aerobics… The people in the film, they're all shot in their spaces. That's where they live, and I really like that because I hate art direction and production design. I wanted the actual world in the film to be as interesting on screen as it is to me. Why fake it? Sarah is actually the only one who is not in a space where she lives but the part is tailored to her because she's obsessed with vintage aerobics videos. We were talking and I asked what's something that would be funny or interesting for you to do?

ASH: Yes, we see her doing aerobics by a pool at night as the pool is cleaned by some kind of automatic pool-cleaning device. You say you are not interested in art direction or production design, but one thing that's clear in this film is that you are interested in wardrobe and makeup.

CS: Yes, very.

ASH: One of the main characters in the film, who is played by Rosie Wilt, who is really excellent in it, I guess is a professional clown like Anne Archer was in Short Cuts?

CS: She is not a professional clown. She is actually a computer engineer...

SF: A software engineer.

CS: She’s a friend we knew from NY who designs and wears these incredible looks for Halloween or her birthday or what not. Her makeup and her costume were her own. I got the idea for her to do the clown and the other costumes she's in because Sarah and I went to this Rob Zombie, Alice Cooper, and Ministry concert. I love Rob Zombie unapologetically. We had a great time and I never do this, but I looked at Rob Zombie's Instagram after the show and there was this person posting in the comments about how she tailors her looks to honor Sheri Moon, the actor Zombie is married to. I was like, "What is this?" So I went to this person's profile, and they're a cosplayer for metal and horror stuff. I thought it was really interesting, and I liked how elaborate it was. So I thought, "What if Rosie, who's really good at doing these looks, what if that's her form of mourning for her dead boyfriend?"

ASH: She performs various rituals as these characters, or just hangs around her apartment in the outfits.

CS: I wanted to limit it to three things, because I believe in a Ramones form of verse-chorus-bridge. Rosie and I limited her mourning scenes and outfits to three figures: a clown, Kurt Cobain, and Jackie Kennedy.

ASH: That's an odd group, a clown, Kurt Cobain, and Jacqueline Kennedy. Wilt's performance is sometimes just her sitting back in a chair, or playing videogames. The chair shot could serve as the primary image of the film. Then there's that very brief moment where she's playing the videogame and the character in the game starts laughing, like it's laughing at her as a clown. That was very haunted. The film does have a contemplative and funeral sense. We see a funeral in the film and you returned to that graveyard a couple of times. 

CS: As far as that videogame moment, that is, again, just working with someone in their house and in their interests. I asked Rosie what are some things that you could do while you're mourning, and she had an old Sega Genesis. I remembered that game Altered Beast where a guy says "Rise from your grave." It's a happy accident. I said play the game and then that moment becomes a transition. As for the funeral stuff, I am a taphophile. I love cemeteries.

BRIEF ENCOUNTER

BY ELIZABETH HARVATH
OCTOBER 10, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

David Lean’s Brief Encounter (1945) is a remembrance of things past, a whirlwind affair told in retrospect. Early in the film, we see Laura (Celia Johnson) sink into an armchair as Rachmaninoff plays on the radio. Across the parlour, a million miles away, her husband Fred (Cyril Raymond) works on a crossword. Laura, in agony, wishes to confess everything that has transpired over the past few weeks: the trains to Milford, the bit of grit in her eye, and the stranger, Alec Harvey (Trevor Howard), who gently coaxed it free, and how a small intimate gesture was enough to sow the seed of a grand infatuation. It wasn’t anyone’s fault; it was merely love at first sight. Alec and Laura, two married people, keep accidentally colliding, and their seemingly innocent meetings—lunches, films, conversations—become conscious promises to meet again. Within weeks, the two of them find themselves in the throes of an emotional affair.

Brief Encounter is built around this tension, between outward composure and inward tumult. Lean’s decision to dramatize Laura’s innermost feelings through voice-over turns the film into a kind of diary. She reveals everything to us, even as she remains unable to tell those closest to her. The voice-over underscores her isolation, capturing the painful distance between what Laura feels and what she can say. Her narration is juxtaposed with Johnson’s restrained performance, in which the smallest shifts in expression reveal a deep interior life.

Lean uses his camera sparingly. This makes its few movements all the more striking. Consider the moment Laura truly falls for Alec. In a moment of idle conversation, Alec speaks and Lean’s lens slowly pushes in on Laura. Her eyes drop to his lips, then flick quickly back to his eyes, and the Rachmaninoff swells. Nothing has been said aloud, yet everything is different. Lean invites us to consider an unsettling question: how much of love is chosen, and how much is merely timing?

Fitting for this accidental romance, Lean stages nearly everything in what are, essentially, non-places: spaces of transit where no one lingers and nothing is permanent, and where every conversation has a timetable. True intimacy is never really on hand, and the real world never stops interjecting—tinny station announcements ring out like the decrees of a watchful God, and other people are all too ready to voice their suspicions, constantly reminding the lovers that, to the world at large, their bond is something shameful. Laura and Alec don’t consider themselves forged in sin; to deny their feelings would be to deny nature itself. How could something as pure as true love be wrong? The film lets them ask the question but never lets them answer it.

Brief Encounter screens this afternoon, October 10, and on October 23, at Metrograph on 35mm as part of the series “Trains: Local Routes.”
