SPRING IN A SMALL TOWN

BY BERNARDO RONDEAU
NOVEMBER 11, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

A cinematic landmark that still languishes in relative obscurity, Fei Mu’s Spring in a Small Town (1948) not only anticipates the modernist aesthetics and narrative strategies of mid-century art-house cinema, it was made under remarkably adversarial circumstances and nearly lost altogether. Fluent in several European languages and a one-time accountant for a mining firm, Mei directed more than 10 feature films during his short life, most of them made against a backdrop of war and unrest in China. Spring in a Small Town, his final feature, was released just months before the People’s Republic of China was inaugurated and Mei fled to Hong Kong. By then, he had not only worked with the ill-fated superstar Ruan Lingyu, but also with Lan Ping (also known as Jiang Qing), the future wife of Mao Zedong. He also managed to make China’s first color film with smuggled negatives. Yet he received recognition only decades after his sudden death in 1951, at age 44, from a heart attack. After a print of Spring in a Small Town was struck in Hong Kong in the 1980s, the film was newly appreciated by a generation of filmmakers born long after its original release, among them Zhang Yimou, Jia Zhangke, and Tian Zhuangzhuang. The latter even remade the film to great acclaim as Springtime in a Small Town in 2002.

Even before its release and brisk suppression, Spring in a Small Town endured a difficult production. Budgetary constraints meant that two-thirds of the script had to be cut before shooting began, and Mei had a cast of just five actors and three sets to work with. Perhaps due to these limitations, Mei alchemizes a visually distilled film language. The camera roams and searches, every time finding a uniquely elegant frame to settle on; dissolves conjure gauzy doppelgängers mid-scene; and Mei’s blocking of actors is endlessly intriguing.

The film’s “small town” is a ruined façade and piles of rubble, while the world beyond its gates is an endless expanse of sky and wild grass. In a tumbledown mansion, the ailing Dai Liyan (You Chi) lives with his lovelorn wife, Zhou Yuwen (Wei Wei); his teenage sister, Xiu (Zhang Hongmei); and their sole remaining servant, (Lao Huang). Mei makes much of the bombed-out walls and brickpiles surrounding the property. More than a decade of war has ravaged both the landscape and the spirits of its inhabitants. Yuwen provides the film’s sole voice-over, though she doesn’t as much narrate the proceedings as offer a frank interior monologue that contrasts with her steadfast exterior. Caught in a dismal loop: she wanders aimlessly outside or withdraws to her chambers to embroider.

The unexpected arrival of Zhang Zhichen (Li Wei), an old colleague of Liyan and, secretly, Yuwen’s former lover, appears to break the household’s gloom spiral. Unlike Liyan, who wears a traditional changshan, Zhichen sports a suit and trench coat. While Liyan has been largely bed-ridden, Zhichen has traveled the country. The former is sick, the latter is a doctor. Resourceful in the face of his limitations, Mei crafts an atmospheric, nearly dreamlike drama that unfolds across a blurred timescape as this trio takes stock of their circumstances. Songs are sung, drinks downed, and a trip downriver offers a spell of plein-air revelry. But at its heart, Spring in a Small Town is a shadowy chamber drama that foretells the hallucinatory interiority of Flowers of Shanghai (1998), the waking dreams of Alain Resnais and so many other ethereal portraits of alienation. No less an authority on lyrical ennui than Wong Kar-wai has praised Mei, saying, “I wouldn’t presume to call myself a ‘poet of cinema’; there is only one such poet in Chinese cinema—Fei Mu.”

Spring in a Small Town screens this evening, November 11, at Asia Society as part of the series “Films to See Before You Die.”
