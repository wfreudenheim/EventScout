TESTAMENT

BY BERNARDO RONDEAU
NOVEMBER 7, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

The apocalypse hits small town America in Lynne Littman’s debut feature. Based on a short story by Carol Amen, Testament (1983) chronicles the protracted aftermath of an unexpected and unexplained nuclear holocaust as seen through the eyes of Carol Weatherly (Jane Alexander in an Oscar-nominated performance), a married mother of three. Eschewing spectacle for intimacy, the family’s daily struggles in a gradually disintegrating world are the stuff of nightmares yet Littman handles it all with disarming sobriety.

Testament’s atomic cataclysm occurs almost entirely offscreen. The viewer, like the Weatherlys and their fellow residents in the Northern California town of Hamelin (as in Pied Piper of…), are left with their imaginations to speculate how exactly the disaster unfolded. Littman offers only a pair of images to represent the bombings. The first is a blinding flash of light that consumes the screen just after the cartoon that Carol and her children are watching is interrupted by a frantic news bulletin. The second is verbal. Henry Abhart (played by veteran character actor Leon Ames), an elderly ham radio enthusiast who serves as the town’s sole point of contact with the world beyond their streets, reports, “A fellow in Twain Harte thinks they hit Yosemite. The sky is black with splinters, and trees and rocks coming down like rain. Must have been a mistake. There's nothing strategic there.” The world’s end, in error.

The film is adapted from Carol Amen’s short story, “The Last Testament,” published in Ms Magazine in August 1981, eight months after Ronald Reagan’s presidential inauguration. By November 1983, when Littman’s film was released, Reagan had announced the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), claiming to shield the US from nuclear ballistic missiles. Littman’s vision of an Armageddon afterlife arrived in an era of interminable doomsday anxiety for a generation raised on “Duck and Cover” drills.

Carol’s sparse diary entries log the horrors, interpreted with progressively more affecting Super-8 home movies of sunsoaked memories from the Weatherlys. Testament’s depiction of human attrition is immensely powerful as days blur together and survival becomes less tenable. Electricity is never restored and death becomes a full-time resident. A Paramount Pictures release that landed on television screens just a year after its theatrical roll out, Testament launched the 4th season of American Playhouse the Monday after Thanksgiving. With winter on the horizon, the film likely served as a potent prologue to a shut-in season. The bomb may be less of a concern in our current age of rolling disasters, but Testament’s glass-darkly view of gradual collapse is all too familiar.

Testament screens this afternoon, November 7, at the Museum of the Moving Image on 35mm as part of the series “Rewind.” Director Lynne Littman will be in attendance for a Q&A.
