Deeply informed by the concept of ma—the negative space in between, or “the deepest now”—Keiji Haino’s music vacillates between excess and tranquility, throwing each into stark relief. He describes his approach as nazoranai (“not repeating”), distinguishing it from forms of improvisation that return to familiar motifs; he has said that his aim is to make music so estranging that it falls outside the gaze of God. In Haino’s music, there is a playfulness with rhythm and a patience for sustained irresolution, as well as a penchant for extremes. Bluesy syncopations stutter through the sludgiest, caterwauling licks, suddenly opening onto pockets of deep serenity. The lightest touch produces shock waves, a scream blisters the lungs; moments later, Haino patiently works his way along a single string, yielding delicate slides and vibratos, his voice reduced to breath and murmur. Haino’s formative influences are never far from view: on the one hand, Antonin Artaud’s Theatre of Cruelty, which sought to bypass psychological realism in favor of a primal, convulsive spectacle capable of shocking an audience into a different state of awareness. On the other, The Doors, whose trancelike organ, outsized personas, and yowling confrontation with spiritual darkness offered a stark counterpoint to the utopianism of the hippie era.

In 1970, Haino joined Lost Aaraaff, a percussion-led outfit for which he provided hair-raising, ethereal vocals and, later, an array of instruments he acquired autodidactically, including guitar and saxophone. Touring with Lost Aaraaff, Haino matured quickly, absorbing the influence of such luminaries as Takashi Mizutani, Kaoru Abe, and Masayuki Takayanagi. That same decade, Haino founded his rock band Fushitsusha, and in 1981 released his first solo album, Watashi Dake?, on Takafumi Sato’s now-legendary underground label Pinakotheca. By the end of the decade, Haino had found an important home in Hideo Ikeezumi’s P.S.F. Records, the label and record-shop nexus that became one of the principal conduits for Japan’s underground music. P.S.F. issued Fushitsusha’s first album, a sprawling double-live set, in 1989, followed by Haino’s Nijiumu in 1990 and a stream of recordings that situated his work alongside a remarkable constellation of Japanese psychedelia, free improvisation, folk, and noise.

Alongside Fushitsusha, Haino pursued projects including Vajra with Kan Mikami and Toshiaki Ishizuka, and later Aihiyo, whose radically transformed versions of pop and rock songs extended his practice into an unlikely repertoire of covers. At the same time his work increasingly circulated abroad, through John Zorn’s Avant and Tzadik labels and collaborations with Loren Connors, Alan Licht, Jim O’Rourke, and others. His body of work now spans more than two hundred records in various formations, on which he has explored an extraordinary range of instruments, from guitar and percussion to lute, CDJs, cymbals, and the medieval hand-cranked hurdy-gurdy. At seventy-four, Haino remains as active and uncompromising as ever, performing regularly around the world and continuing to pursue new forms; this year, the Venice Biennale named him the recipient of its Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement, recognizing more than half a century spent radically expanding the possibilities of sound and performance.

In 1991, Keiji Haino sought out Loren Connors, an elusive figure in New York’s underground who, like Haino, had forged a singular guitar vernacular. Returning to the city nearly a decade after his July 1982 appearance at Studio Henry with Fred Frith and others, Haino performed that December at CBGB with John Zorn. During the same trip, he visited Connors and Suzanne Langille at their Brooklyn Heights apartment, where the pair performed a private concert for him—remarkably captured on VHS by Linsey Herman.

Connors, whose recordings then included nine volumes of Unaccompanied Acoustic Guitar Variations and a handful of song-based albums with Kath Bloom, was beginning to explore noise and distortion rooted in electric blues through releases on his St. Joan label. His open, blues-inflected improvisations stood in contrast to Haino’s ear-splitting walls of sound, chordal and grimy. Yet their early collaborations, documented on Live at Downtown Music Gallery (1995) and Vol. 2 (1997), reveal a striking affinity. These recordings capture the pair in a rare mode of restraint, their instrumental voices interweaving seamlessly as they trade showers of tones and leisurely anti-melodies that hang, warping, in the air.

Their collaboration continued into the aughts and beyond, as Connors’s playing evolved toward a mellow, painterly style indebted to Irish airs. Over three decades of friendship, Haino and Connors have continued to find common ground in the fluency they've cultivated within a deeply personal idiom.

*

Fushitsusha is a Japanese psychedelic rock band whose blistering, no-holds-barred take on the idiom dismantles rock’s archetypes to arrive at a mind-splitting distillation of what can be accomplished with guitar, bass, and drums. Formed in 1978 by guitarist and singer Keiji Haino with Tamio Shiraishi on synthesizer, the group soon assumed its signature power-trio form, now comprising Haino, bassist Shingo Naruke, and drummer Noriaki Yoshida.

Inspired by early blues, krautrock, and psychedelia, Fushitsusha’s experimental free rock has been called the loudest in the world. Their staggering passage through fried guitar turbulence, dripping feedback, and harrowing fragments of contemplative melodic beauty remains peerless. Clad in the same black monochrome that adorns their enigmatic album covers, Naruke and Yoshida underpin Haino’s incendiary playing, alternately hushed and howled singing, and convulsive onstage theatrics informed by butoh and Antonin Artaud. Fushitsusha gropes through a sonic void, grasping for coherence only to push it to the brink of broken catharsis—with true abandon.

 

Blank Forms is a nonprofit organization supporting emerging and historically significant artists who produce work across disciplines, often rooted in traditions of experimental and creative music. We aim to establish new frameworks to preserve, nurture, and present these artists’ work and to build platforms for practices underrepresented in art’s commercial, institutional, and historical fields. Blank Forms collaborates with artists on commissions, exhibitions, publications as well as archival and estate projects within contemporary cultural ecosystems and in perpetuity. In presenting and documenting this work, Blank Forms seeks to foster an artistic community founded upon engaged and equitable conversations across continents, media, and generations.
