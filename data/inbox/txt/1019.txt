GRANDMA'S BOY

BY JUSTIN LALIBERTY
NOVEMBER 2, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

It’s impossible to separate Grandma’s Boy (2006) from its production company. Happy Madison Productions, founded by Adam Sandler following a string of box office success in the ‘90s, quickly became known for its ribald brand comedy, often written, directed by and/or starring Sandler’s fellow Saturday Night Live alums and comedian friends. The company’s first film was the raunchy, R-rated Deuce Bigalow: Male Gigolo (1999) starring Rob Schneider. Its massive worldwide financial success it achieved despite dismal reviews signaled that Sandler and Co. were impervious to bad press and knew what audiences wanted. With the exception of a couple of Sandler-starring swings—Little Nicky (2000) and Eight Crazy Nights (2002—Happy Madison Productions continued to release comedies into multiplexes going into (and beyond) the mid-aughts. In 2006, Grandma’s Boy released months apart from two other Happy Madison productions: the Sandler-starring comic fantasy Click and the ensemble baseball comedy The Benchwarmers. They earned 36x and 10x Grandma’s Boy’s global box office cume, respectively.

The marketing campaign for Grandma’s Boy, which included included a gorgeously hand-illustrated poster by Phil Roberts, positioned the film film as a throwback to the party comedies of the ‘70s, like National Lampoon’s Animal House (1978). This brand of frat-bro-targeted humor was enjoying a comeback in the early ‘00s thanks to Todd Philips’s successes with Road Trip (2000) and Old School (2003), except that Grandma’s Boy was hardly cut from the same cloth. Although it featured an extended party sequence and contained ample amounts of drug use and gratuitous nudity, Grandma’s Boy predicted the tech industry malaise that began popping up in American cinema a decade later, although usually in the form of a drama like Aaron Sorkin’s The Social Network (2010). Grandma’s Boy’s script, co-written by co-stars Allen Covert and Nick Swardson, unlike many of the more dramatic films that followed in its footsteps, sees the humanity, evil, and innate humor that defines the lives of those working in the tech world.

Covert’s lead character, the titular Grandma’s Boy, is a slacker game designer working on his own video game outside of work hours while spending his 9-to-5 testing a game developed by a nebbish yet deceitful self-proclaimed prodigy named JP (Joel David Moore). Amidst its parties, bong rips and casual sexual encounters (some with senior citizens), Grandma’s Boy makes time for its adversaries to face off in a textbook battle of good versus evil. As the villain, JP is a sympathetic caricature of tech industry ego. While his mania will translate into hilarious material for many, it will also be deemed cool by those who see the character as a mirror; one can easily imagine Elon Musk seeing the portrayal of JP and thinking that he’s a wronged hero.

In Luca Guadagnino’s Artificial (2026), Ike Barinholtz plays Musk in a performance that’s ultimately the comedic relief of the film. He renders Musk as all bluster, entering and leaving rooms in a hurry, talking almost solely about himself whenever given the chance. Grandma’s Boy isn’t far removed from what Guadagnino is doing now, and while the former is clearly a work of fiction, it exemplifies the fact that comedies often provide the most astute observations about ourselves and our enemies. Yes, even comedy films that feature kung-fu fighting monkeys and references to Charlie Chaplin getting a handjob.

Grandma’s Boy screens tonight, November 2, at Nitehawk Williamsburg on 35mm as part of the series “Video Store Gems” presented by Night Owl Video.
