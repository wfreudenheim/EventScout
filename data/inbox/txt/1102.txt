The Dreamed Adventure

Main Slate by Amy Taubin

There were over a dozen movies that matter as cultural objects and as prompts for how we might negotiate our lives in ever more perilous times in Main Slate at the 64th New York Film Festival. Add to this that many of the strongest of these films were directed or co-directed by women, or centered women on the screen. These films were not programmed simply to give “otherness” a chance. They are exciting movies and there were more than enough of them to fill anyone’s best of the year list.

NAZA, by investigative film journalists Yuval Abraham and Rachel Szor, is in a class by itself. It is restrained in form and content, filmed at night on Tel Aviv rooftops and consisting of 24 interviews with members of the Israeli military and intelligence service. The subjects are anonymous, their faces deeply shadowed, their voices often partly disguised. NAZA, the title of the film, is derived from a Hebrew term for collateral damage. Ten dead “NAZA” for each real or suspected Hamas member killed is “legal,” but depending on circumstances, 50 times that many are allowed. I’m sure that many in the NYFF audience had read much of this information in The Guardian (which produced the film and will stream it for free when its theatrical run is over) or other journalistic outlets. But it is more chilling to hear these testimonies. Most of them are spoken in matter-of-fact tones, but some are edged with confusion, as when one soldier mentions his obligatory educational trip to the sites of German concentration camps and wonders whether the non-response of Germans to the Holocaust during World War II is paralleled in the refusal of the majority of Israeli citizens to acknowledge the genocide in Gaza just a few miles away. (Freud might have added the term “NAZA”—just one letter apart from “Nazi”—to his analysis of words that contain opposite meanings in his essays on “The Uncanny.”) In the background of all the dark rooftop interviews are the windows of brightly illuminated apartments where Tel Aviv residents eat dinner, chat, and watch their TV screens. At one point, an image of Claude Lanzmann appears on a TV. His epic Shoah (1985) is being shown for Holocaust Remembrance Day. At the very least, NAZA is that film’s more than worthy and crushing addendum. The filmmakers took their interview strategy from Shoah, but at the very last moment their cameras enter the complete wreckage that is Gaza to find a Palestinian man desperately searching the rubble of his home for his children and crying out “my love,” “my love.” If “NAZA” is a term as dehumanizing as “insect” or “vermin,” no one can question who is dehumanized by this slaughter.

After the NAZA press screening, I ran into Julia Loktev, who said that Abraham and Szor were now in the same position as the Russian journalists in her film My Undesirable Friends: Part II – Exile. None of them can go home unless those who rule their countries are deposed. Loktev captures the tragedy of exile for the journalists—most of them women—whom we met in Part 1 of what is now a 10-hour epic. Part 2 is both more intimate, but also has the scale of a great Russian novel. Perhaps it is the urgency of the political moment that demands documentary treatment, but NYFF rose to the occasion with not just these two but three more revelatory non-fiction flicks. William and David Greaves’s Once Upon a Time in Harlem—a celebratory, historically essential Harlem Renaissance time capsule—looks back at the 1920s through a festive gathering in 1972 of elderly visual artists, musicians, writers and organizers who participated in the collective creative energy of that moment. Primarily filmed in 16mm more than 50 years ago, the vérité images and sounds finally have been edited into a buoyant, visually splendid 100 minutes. Iranian filmmaker Mani Haghighi’s marvelously oddball I Deserve a Lover Whose Every Rise Sets Fiery Dooms Raging Across the Skies asks how we choose what is worthy of preservation when the entire world is being ransacked and destroyed. And Ava DuVernay’s 14th, which examines the eponymous amendment to the U.S. Constitution that redefined citizenship and was ratified in the aftermath of the Civil War, is crammed full of brilliant historians and lawyers, led by 14th Amendment scholar Sherrilyn Ifill. Because the 14th’s birthright citizenship guarantee is under attack by Trump and associates, the film needs more eyes on it than the NYFF provides. How infuriating that Netflix is waiting until after the midterms to release it.

The Debut

Spotlight by Stephanie Monohan

Only in its sixth year, the Spotlight slate at the New York Film Festival is meant to represent “an eclectic survey of the year’s most eagerly anticipated films” and “special presentations,” often featuring concert films, documentaries about well-known artists or cultural figures, and narrative works that have generated buzz while on the festival circuit. The downside of such curation is that some titles are so eagerly anticipated that they are not screened for the press at all, so unfortunately I cannot provide you with my impression of Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s You Can See Everything, whose premiere immediately became the most coveted ticket of the festival. All in all, this year’s Spotlight section showcased a fine mélange of films that could potentially serve as festival entry points for a broader moviegoing audience. There was a lot I liked, but nothing I truly loved.

The official centerpiece of Spotlight this year was the world premiere of Takashi Yamazaki’s Godzilla Minus Zero, a particularly pop selection for the gala film, even among a slate typically considered more mainstream. Intense wind and rain kept many critics away from the morning press screening, but those who braved the nor’easter were treated to a largely satisfying sequel to the acclaimed Godzilla Minus One (2023). This installment takes place in 1949, as the characters from the first film have begun to settle into a state of tenuous normalcy in the wake of World War II, the United States’ atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the (assumed) defeat of Godzilla. However, when they learn that Godzilla has begun to regenerate and a new monster is looming, the traumatized found family is drawn back into crisis once again. The period setting is one of the “Minus” series’s great strengths, providing a rich space for the kaiju action and genre elements to intermingle with the classic Godzilla themes of trauma, cycles of violence, and atomic terror. A brief, intense scene that encapsulates all of these ideas also provides the answer to those wondering why the new Godzilla movie would make its world premiere at the New York Film Festival (of all places) in a grimly humorous way. I would argue that this film could have used some more Godzilla, but ultimately, if you enjoyed Minus One, you’ll find a lot to love in this one as well.

Werner Herzog’s latest feature, Bucking Fastard (his first fiction film in seven years), opens with a card reading, “Dedicated to David Lynch.” While not nearing the enigmatic artistic heights of the beloved auteur, Bucking Fastard’s delightful absurdity does have a Lynchian heart. Inspired by Freda and Greta Chaplin, real-life British twins who became famous in the 1980s for willingly living in complete unison, Herzog creates Jean and Joan Holbrooke, sisters who dress the same, say the same things at the same time, and even share the same lover. Sisters Kate and Rooney Mara are cast as the Holbrookes, and their performances are equal parts hilarious and empathetic, grounding the characters in a strange believability. Herzog is disinterested in explaining how the sisters' extreme co-dependency developed, dropping us into their lives in middle-age as we watch them navigate the present day while somehow unaware of its norms and expectations. In this aspect, the film resembles Poor Things (2023), finding humor in its characters’ naïveté and lack of common sense, while also reflecting society’s inanity back at itself. While everything the sisters do baffles those around them, the Maras sell how the Holbrookes’ actions make sense to them nonetheless, creating the sense that forcing them to individuate would be cruel and unnatural.

In Noah Segan’s The Only Living Pickpocket in New York, we follow a protagonist who spent his life navigating the world in a street-smart way, but now finds much of that world unrecognizable in his later years. John Turturro plays Harry Lehman, a pickpocket who traverses the city every day lifting wallets, watches, and other pawnable goods off unsuspecting commuters to care for his paralyzed wife Rosie. But in an era in which people’s money is increasingly digitized and their designer items are often not as authentic as they look, he faces diminishing returns. He thinks he’s found a perfect crypto-bro mark, but when it turns out the kid actually does have some seriously shady connections, Harry must race to return what he’s stolen to at least save the people he cares for. In complete candor, it’s difficult for me to disentangle my sentimentality as a native New Yorker from my critical appraisal with films such as this, where the texture and tapestry of the city is so lovingly captured. In his daylong odyssey back and forth across four of the five boroughs, Harry encounters many memorable characters (and friendly faces played by Steve Buscemi and Giancarlo Esposito) who are all just trying to get by and know the value of a favor (or a $100 bill). Segan may not wind up receiving the same kind of appreciation and hype that filmmakers like the Safdies do for tapping into a ‘70s New York movie sensibility, but he does subtly accomplish the task with a very welcome earnestness.

Just Like Weather

Revivals by Olivia Crough

Since beginning in 1963, the New York Film Festival has included older films in short-lived sections such as “Special Selections,” “Retrospective,” and “Masterworks.” Its contemporary emphasis on international restoration began with the 2013 addition of the “Revivals” section, during a decade that saw the ascendance of both 4K digitization and a model of film curation and distribution focused on overturning the canon with overlooked and rediscovered fare. This year’s section comes at an odd inflection point in repertory culture—chains are screening “underground classics” and Letterboxd has 30 million users—but its 12 films and two shorts programs are truly an embarrassment of riches, thanks to dedicated archival efforts in India, Hong Kong, and Ukraine, among other places.

The hottest ticket has been Ken Russell’s The Devils (1971), a carnal drama about 17th-century Ursuline nuns who accuse a priest of witchcraft with a maximalist set designed by a young Derek Jarman. The film is famous for its widespread censorship and condemnation by the Vatican; under Warner Bros.’ young Clockwork division, Russell’s original version has been restored with long-lost scenes of vestal penetration that would make Verhoeven’s Benedetta blush. It is shown on a gorgeous new 35mm print, but, to my eyes, its spectacle of corruption and cruelty pales when held up to the section’s other lavish period piece, L’Innocente (1976). Luchino Visconti’s final work, it is a late-19th-century melodrama set in airless parlors, with a fading Giancarlo Giannini as an aristocrat in a monstrous state of decay.

The other headline restoration is Michael Powell and Emeric Pressburger’s lesser-known technicolor masterpiece Gone to Earth (1950), seen here in its world premiere. Martin Scorsese and Thelma Schoonmaker led the film’s reconstruction, a difficult feat after the negative was butchered by David O. Selznick for its original American release. Adapted from a 1917 romance novel by the poet Mary Webb, the film’s heroine, Hazel (Jennifer Jones), is a pagan child of the Welsh highlands who is encroached on, spiritually and physically, by the English men who hunt and partition the land. Much of Gone to Earth was shot on location in bucolic Shropshire, where Hazel briefly, sublimely, scales the hills before being swallowed by the mined earth.

Hazel is echoed by Jacquie (Jacqueline Vandal), the wayward 20-year-old protagonist of Paula Delsol’s The Drift (1963), a true rediscovery that compels an overdue revision of the history of women in the Nouvelle Vague. Set in the sun-bleached Midi rather than Paris, the film’s unjudgmental depiction of Jacquie’s meandering sexual encounters led to condemnation by French censors. Delsol’s script is sharp and very funny and, as Erika Balsom recently wrote, it is feminist not for an image of liberation but because it shows “how dead-ends, failures, and acts of self-sabotage are woven into the texture of a woman’s experience.”

The condition of being adrift runs as a thread through more of these films. In Alexander Kluge’s Yesterday Girl (1966), the girl is Anita G., an East German Jew who moves to the West and wanders between cities, witnessing the hypocrisy of the country’s supposed rebirth and redemption in the postwar Wirstchaftswunder (economic miracle). Played by his sister Alexandra, she is surrounded by people and things hollowed out by commodification, and Kluge—who was a prolific writer until his death this year at 94—seems interested in how that process has extended to language itself. Where Delsol’s Jacquie believes in something called love, Anita lands in prison because she cannot find meaning in the words “freedom” or “a good life.” Similarly, Hou Hsiao-hsien’s mesmerizing Goodbye South, Goodbye (1996) is set during the capital-intensive “Taiwan Miracle.” Small-time gangster Gao (Jack Kao) is sad and reaching middle age, unable to cash in and caught between visions of an ever-receding future. Much of the film takes place on forms of transit, where Hou inserts long first person takes without narrative function, set to Lim Giong’s Taiwanese alt-rock and industrial techno score.
