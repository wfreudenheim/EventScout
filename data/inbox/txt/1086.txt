TWO QUESTIONS FOR TERRE THAEMLITZ

Terre Thaemlitz is an award-winning multimedia producer, writer, public speaker, educator, audio remixer, DJ, and owner of the Comatonse Recordings record label. Her work combines a critical look at identity politics—including gender, sexuality, class, linguistics, ethnicity, and race—with an ongoing analysis of the socioeconomics of commercial media production.

We’re excited to be partnering with Public Records to present the latest iteration in Thaemlitz’s ongoing series of solo piano works on November 15 at Roulette, a joint presentation between the 2026 Triple Canopy Symposium and 2026 Durations festival. Her performance will be followed by a conversation with Triple Canopy contributing editor Ciarán Finlayson. Use the code TRIPLE10 for 10% off all Durations shows, including the festival pass.

Ahead of the concert, we asked Thaemlitz, who is well known for her house productions, to reflect on her relationship to the piano, as well as the notions of legibility and authenticity that guide her work. Thaemlitz’s responses have been edited for length and clarity.

Triple Canopy: What relationship do you see between your electronic works and solo piano works?

Terre Thaemlitz: The most direct relationships would be the frequent use of piano in both my house and electroacoustic projects, starting with my first release “Raw Through A Straw” in 1993. For most of my career, I have used digital keyboards, combining improvisation captured by MIDI, digitally stepping in notes one by one, and other computer editing. As someone who cannot play keyboard in a traditional sense, and who has no training, I have always played with how the act of releasing a recording on physical media will automatically lend it credibility. What was basically cat-on-the-keyboard chaos would typically be filtered through listener expectations to be heard as a skilled player who chooses to eschew convention. This was a cultural tendency I had already worked with during my college years studying visual art, where I first developed clear critiques of the cultural functions of authenticity, originality, and creativity.

This premise was expanded through my Rubato Series of digitally composed piano solo albums covering Kraftwerk, Gary Numan, and Devo. In the liner notes I was clear that the recordings were digitally composed and played on a synth, but reviewers typically overlooked this and received them as acoustic piano. During an interview, Numan once even referred to my covers of his songs, saying I played piano better than him. The functional illusion of talent goes a long way.

Of course, my interest in doing this is related to a deconstruction of dominant culture and the musical mainstream. It is not to climb a cultural or economic ladder by fooling people. I am always open about the fact that I cannot play instruments in a conventional sense, and try to explain where my critical interests lie. And stylistically my performances are obviously not in song form.

When it comes to actually using an acoustic piano, my first unedited piano solo release was part of my 2012 album Soulnessless: the 30-plus-hour minimalist piece “Canto V: Meditation on Wage Labor and the Death of the Album.” That was recorded in a series of roughly six-hour sittings which were consolidated into a single unedited audio file. There was also an unedited piano solo included in my 2017 album Deproduction, followed a few years later with a roughly 10-minute recording included in a 24-hour performance called “Time Piece,” curated by Berno Odo Polzer for the 2021 edition of MaerzMusik in Berlin.

Around that time, I was incredibly depressed and exhausted by the constant destruction of my electronic studio through forced software updates and other things, which was a major reason for my drop in studio productions over the years. On a sonic level, the sounds of glitches and errors had also shifted with the improvement of audio algorithms and processor speeds. From the software side, those 1990s and early 2000s digital sounds are no longer possible. That era’s cultural tensions between digital audio and mainstream music have also entirely disappeared. Everything is digital now.

Today, I suppose the closest thing to that aesthetic would be media works that emphasize the errors of AI-generated audio and visuals. But even that is now passé, and was doomed to ultimately fall into the same trap of just helping big businesses improve their algorithms all the quicker. I despise AI, and think its propensity to amplify mistakes and diversions at an exponential rate—typically doubling down on its errors—is truly dangerous. For example: the US military’s use of AI targeting in Iran resulting in the deadly bombing of a girls' school. Those deaths and others are real, not rhetorical.

In daily life, I hear how AI composes and renders sounds in background music at restaurants and shops. There is a lot of AI-generated jazz and relaxation piano out there. While it is not my main objective, I suspect the way I play piano is not easily replicated by AI. If so, I do not consider that any testimony to human authenticity or ingenuity, but rather a possible sounding of the culturally minor, if only for the moment.

TC: What do you make of the trope of the “solo piano performance” (such as famous examples by Keith Jarrett, Thelonious Monk, Charles Mingus, etc.) and the appeals to intimacy and authenticity inherent in the form? How do you see this new piano work engaging—or not—with these expectations?

TT: I consider all of my performances as having a relation to the transgendered stage, consciously failing to deliver on the tropes of flamboyancy and extravagance typically associated with drag shows, instead opting for anti-spectacle and boredom. For me, the value of drag performance—its cultural use value—rests in the fact that it remains something other than the thing it emulates. It is about facsimile, which makes it a relevant site for critiquing notions of authenticity and originality, despite today’s dominant rhetoric around drag culture being nothing but claims to authenticity.

I think we would agree that most everybody can immediately feel what you mean by that word “trope,” but if you dig into that feeling of consensus you quickly realize everyone is thinking something different. For example, the most frequent online comments people make about any of my Rubato Series is either, “boring, it’s just piano,” or conversely, “good, despite being just piano.” That tells me that piano solo as a format remains culturally awkward and unpopular, regardless of the trope. In that sense, it seems like a reasonable enough site for doing the types of themes and culture jamming I have performed in other audio marketplaces. Maybe not. This is only my fourth such performance, so I can’t really say for sure.

Sonically, my playing doesn’t fall into melody, and structurally, it doesn’t fall into song. The pieces sit in the realm of ambient. I have never been a fan of free improvisational jazz or similarly chaotic new music. I understand the historical value in relation to rejections of conventional musicology, but I find the genres physically unpleasant to listen to. Like a Fluxus performance, it may read okay on paper, but I don’t want to be there when it happens.

Conversely, I am leery of playing too prettily, because then everything falls back into that trope of affect you mentioned. It quickly becomes New Age, which is a continual concern when producing my electroacoustic and ambient albums. Fortunately, within a live setting, even if the sound momentarily gets too pleasant, that gets tempered for the audience by the unpleasantness of sitting still for an hour.

I try to structure things so that the audience has to do a little work as listeners, but to get them to do that means it has to sound like there is work being done. Like any drag performance, the piano pieces benefit from preparation and run-throughs, which I do daily for a month before each show. I am not interested in just taking a piss by randomly smashing piano keys. That would be too easy, too expected, and too readily dismissed. The point would be made in the first thirty seconds.

To avoid this, I play within the parameters of a previously established improvisational system. I develop a different system for each show. To undercut any sense of uniqueness, and in rejection of the trope of “once-in-a-lifetime-moment” recordings like Jarrett’s The Köln Concert, there is no recording of the shows allowed. However, I do record the month of prep sessions beforehand. They will eventually be self-released through Comatonse Recordings as collections containing four to five separate takes, giving a sense of what happened at the live performances while still refusing to settle on a single take as “the one.”
