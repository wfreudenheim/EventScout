READ ON OUR SITE

Presented as part of the series “German Punk” curated by Sukhdev Sandhu, Sara Stevenson, and Zach Feldman for Anthology Film Archives, Bernd Sahling’s documentary It’ll Be OK (1987-90) profiles Michael, a young East German musician trapped in a generational cycle of poverty, addiction, and premature parenthood. Sahling started filming the documentary as a student, capturing the peculiar atmosphere of the GDR’s tail-end through glimpses of underground parties across East Berlin. Because he started filming before The Fall of the Berlin Wall, he still required permission to film in West Berlin for a few weeks in early ‘89. On top of documenting Michael’s family drama, Sahling includes interstitial images of political protests, border guards at work, and advertising posters throughout It’ll Be Ok.

Sahling, who was also a photographer, introduces the film with stills from Michael’s childhood. As he zooms into the first one, which shows Michael as a child sitting on a horse, his mother, Anna, speaks about her son’s difficult early years. She states that as a young boy, Michael protected her from sexual violence at the hands of his alcoholic father. As the film progresses, it becomes evident that this trauma had a severe impact on Michael, who gets into trouble at school and ends up incarcerated in juvenile hall. By the time he’s let out at 16, he complains that the state caged him and unjustly expected him to support himself with little education. Sahling then captures Michael and Anna as they are forced to emigrate to West Germany in search of better economic conditions and freer living conditions over the course of the shoot.

His rough, boxy cinematography suits Anna and Michael’s cramped apartments. In her West Berlin kitchen, Anna and her daughter occupy every inch of the shot. This confrontational approach also suits the punk scene Sahling documents. Structurally, the elliptical nature of It’ll be OK, which reflects Sahling’s visits to see Anna and Michael as they settle in West Berlin, parallels the disconnect between mother and son; while both have relocated to a new city together, it’s evident their lives exist on separate planes. Michael plays bass for the metal-tinged punk group The Bloody Brains; although they never released a record, It’ll Be OK captures them rehearsing and recording songs. Sahling makes it clear that punk represents Michael’s opportunity to pursue creative possibilities he’d been barred from his whole life. But his mother, worn down by her job as a waitress, disapproves of his friends’ vulgarity and resents his quest for free expression.

As the film progresses, it becomes clear that neither Michael, Anna, or Sahling ever get along with one another. All exist under constant pressure. Michael’s nerves are on edge each time he’s filmed, and Sahling pushes him to reveal things that he’s uncomfortable sharing. Eventually, Michael pushes back at Sahling. Their confrontational dynamic recalls the jittery, probing nature of Frederick Wiseman’s documentaries in the ‘70s. It’ll Be OK abruptly ends with a confessional interview with Michael, and while Sahling never returned to film Michael and Anna’s lives after this event, the film’s title holds out hope that things could turn around for them.

It’ll Be OK screens tomorrow night, Monday, November 9, at Anthology Film Archives as part of the series “German Punk.”
