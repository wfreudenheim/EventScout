Black America: The Last 10 Years
A Discussion Moderated by Carol Jenkins
FREE; RESERVE NOW

In-person reservations are currently full, but please register for the livestream.

In 2016, Barack Obama, America's first Black president was completing his second term and Donald Trump became elected as the next president. It was also the year that CUNY TV launched the show Black America. To celebrate the 10th anniversary of this award-winning show that profiles Black activists, academics, business leaders, sports figures, elected officials, artists, and writers, Carol Jenkins, noted journalist, activist, author, and host of the show, moderates a special live event looking back at the last decade, taking the current temperature and asking how has the state of affairs of Black America changed?

After a reading by poet Cornelius Eady of the poem he wrote for Mayor Mamdani's inauguration, Jenkins leads a panel discussion with distinguished guests. Featuring Kimberlé Williams Crenshaw, professor at Columbia Law School and UCLA School of Law, executive director of the African American Policy Forum, and author of a new memoir, Backtalker; Frederick Joseph, bestselling author whose work spans poetry, nonfiction, children’s literature, and young adult fiction, and a noted philanthropist; Marc Morial, president and CEO of the National Urban League; and Nell Irvin Painter, historian, author, and visual artist, professor emerita at Princeton University, and author of The History of White People. 
 
Presented with CUNY TV and the Ph.D. and M.A. Program in Black, Race, and Ethnic Studies.

Wednesday, December 9, 6:30 p.m.
Culture and Representation in America: The Impact of CETA 
FREE; RESERVE NOW

Who gets to shape the artistic and cultural life of a community, and who gets to tell its stories? Between 1974 and 1981, the Comprehensive Employment and Training Act (CETA) employed more than 30,000 artists and cultural workers in nearly 200 cities, towns, and Indigenous territories across the United States. Although CETA was not passed with artists in mind, it created the most significant federally supported arts workforce since the New Deal’s WPA, and its impact extended far beyond the artworks produced during its lifespan. 

Inspired by the exhibition ART/WORK: The Practice and Politics of CETA and the Arts, 1974–1981 at the CUNY Graduate Center’s James Gallery, this panel considers the legacy of publicly supported artistic labor and its relationship to cultural representation and civic life. From the development of community-based arts organizations to the role of photography in documenting social change, the conversation explores how artists have worked within and beyond conventional art-world institutions, shaped, and at times contested, the identities of neighborhoods and communities, and contributed to the cultural infrastructure we inhabit today. 

Following introductory remarks by exhibition curators Molly Garfinkel and Jodi Waynberg, panelists Makeda Best, chief curator of photography at The Museum of Modern Art; Bella Desai, vice president for public programs and exhibitions at The New York Public Library’s Research Libraries; and Drew Thompson, curator, writer, and educator at Bard College, join historian Prithi Kanakamedala, a professor at CUNY Graduate Center, who moderates the discussion. 

The James Gallery will remain open for viewing until 8:30 pm.
