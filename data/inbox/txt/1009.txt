The Problem of the Inner Self
 
7 November 2026
247 East 82nd Street
New York, NY 10028

 

Is there an “inner self”? To what extent have the realms of religion, philosophy, and literature tended to presume the reality of a soulful interiority at the heart of human experience? At a time when the idea of the self is increasingly entangled with external forces and encroachments, this roundtable conversation seeks to reframe the assumptions we bring to the notion of human interiority. In an effort to rethink assumptions about essential identity or static subjectivity, this roundtable will approach the inner self as a problem—not so much an obstacle or dilemma, but rather a problematique: the term Fredric Jameson has used to refer to a “complex set of problems that are intertwined and touch on certain limits.” How have our technologies, spiritualities, and historical and political assumptions clarified or complicated our ideas about the inner self? What alternatives to the doctrines of innermost selfhood might we conceive? And to what end? Can there be an inner self without an outer self and its participation in otherness?

Convened by R. John Williams and Beverley Zabriskie.

THE PARTICIPANTS

Branka Arsić was born in Serbia and received her PhD from the University of Belgrade. She taught at Central European University in Budapest and the State University of New York at Albany before joining the Columbia University faculty in 2012. She has also taught at the Center for Women’s Studies in Belgrade. Her scholarship specializes in the literature of the nineteenth-century Americas and its scientific, philosophical, and religious contexts. Her book on Henry David Thoreau, Bird Relics: Grief and Vitalism in Thoreau, won the MLA’s James Russell Lowell Prize for the outstanding book of 2016. She was named a Great Immigrant by Carnegie Corporation of New York in 2018 and received a Guggenheim Fellowship in 2019 in the field of American literature.
 

Marie Howe is the author of five volumes of poetry, New and Selected Poems, which won the 2025 Pulitzer Prize for Poetry; Magdalene: Poems; The Kingdom of Ordinary Time; The Good Thief; and What the Living Do, and she is the co-editor of a book of essays, In the Company of My Solitude: American Writing from the AIDS Pandemic. Her poems have appeared in The New Yorker, The Atlantic, Poetry, Agni, Ploughshares, Harvard Review, and The Partisan Review, among others. She has been a fellow at the Bunting Institute at Radcliffe College and a recipient of NEA and Guggenheim fellowships, and Stanley Kunitz selected Howe for a Lavan Younger Poets Prize from the American Academy of Poets. In 2015, she received the Academy of American Poets Poetry Fellowship, and from 2012-2014, she served as the Poet Laureate of New York State. She is a member of The Academy of Arts and Letters.
 

Lawrence Kramer grew up in Philadelphia and New York and was educated at the University of Pennsylvania and Yale. His teaching career has been spent primarily at Fordham University, where he is Distinguished Professor of English and Music. He works at the crossroads of words and music, both as an author and as a composer. Recent books include Experience: A History (2026), Experiencing Sound (2024), and Music and the Forms of Life (2022). His chamber opera The Dreaming of the Bones premiered in New York City in September 2026. Kramer’s writings seek to understand music, especially classical music, in its fully humanistic dimension: not only as sonic architecture, but also as a means of expressing how we experience our involvement with history, culture, society, and the chances and mischances of life. Since the publication of The Hum of the World: A Philosophy of Listening in 2019, Kramer has steadily widened his scope, moving from the experience of sound and the concept of life to the phenomenon of experience itself in Experience: A History. Kramer lives in Rhinebeck, New York, with his wife, Nancy Leonard.
 

Morgan Stebbins, MDiv, LMSW, DMin, LP

Morgan’s interest in the inner life began with a repeating series of dreams when he was 10 years old. In these dreams he was chased by a giant dinosaur and then died. This brought up a number of unsettling questions. Before he could answer them, he happened upon Carl Jung’s essay Psychology and Religion when he was 15, and although he didn’t understand a word of it, the die was cast. He has since studied and practiced different forms of religion, academia, and therapeutic modalities in between stints as a wilderness guide, bike racer, Olympian, and med tech. He has lived Europe, India, South Africa, and the U.S. He became a Zen monk in 1979, joined the Berkeley free clinic for psychoanalysis in 1980, and later wrote his thesis at Berkeley on modern cult phenomena by infiltrating a martial arts cult which he had joined. His Master of Divinity thesis was on Jung and Lacan, called The Structure of the Unconscious in Image and Word. His thesis at the CG Jung Institute was on the 10 Commandments As Path to Individuation, and his doctoral thesis was called The Hermeneutics of Psychological Translation. Morgan is a supervising analyst and faculty member at the New York-based Jungian Psychoanalytic Association, where he formerly served as the Director of Training. He maintains a private practice in Cold Spring, NY, where he also has a sand tray practice, and also online. He has written several chapters, such as Jung, Gender, and Imagining Sexual Diversity, and also Uncertainty and Healing in the Archetypal End-Times, as well as a Jungian guide to Lacanian concepts. He just published his first book, called Transforming Compulsion: A Jungian Guide to OCD, and he’s working on the next, called Redeeming Narcissus. His motivating interest is to bring the spiritual life to modern people through a practice with the symbolic language of the unconscious.
 

R. John Williams is Professor of English and Film and Media Studies at Yale University. He is the author of The Buddha in the Machine: Art, Technology, and the Meeting of East and West and has published on a range of topics, including religion, futurology, systems theory, psychoanalysis, and film and television. His recently published book, Out of Mind: Media, Meditation, and the Problem of the Inner Self (University of Chicago Press), examines the role of communication technologies in the evolution of contemplative practices during the twentieth century.
