PROGRAMMING POSSESSION: YASMINA TAWIL ON BAM'S "THE SPIRIT COMPELS HER" SERIES

BY STEPHANIE MONOHAN
OCTOBER 29, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Just as misogyny transcends cultural, linguistic, and religious divides throughout history, so has the concept of spiritual or “demonic.” In depictions of possession on film, women are typically the victims of such malevolent loss of corporeal control. The films in BAM’s upcoming series, “The Spirit Compels Her: Possession and the Demonization of Women,” run the gamut from supernatural horror to social dramas, but they all explore how possession functions as an expression of feminine rebellion and/or as a patriarchal tool for keeping women who dare to transgress societal norms or defy expectations in line.

The series, of course, includes William Friedkin’s The Exorcist (1973), the premier American entry in the demonic possession canon, but it’s the array of international films across the genre that is so notable. While possession and the presence of demonic influence is treated quite literally in some of the entries here, in others it is an accusation weaponized against women and girls who refuse marriage, strive for independence, or simply were in the wrong place at the wrong time. Ultimately, the program questions whether the women in these films should be more afraid of demonic entities or of their own communities.

I spoke with BAM Film programmer Yasmina Tawil about how the series came together, cinema’s treatment of possession, and which films are gems that shouldn’t be missed.

Stephanie Monohan: What were the origins of the program?

Yasmina Tawil: The inspiration for the program was The Sealed Soil [1977]. When I first saw that film, I loved its message about pushing back against traditional patriarchal society and culture, but what really struck me was that it’s a non-horror film where you see possession and an exorcism. It's also the first time I'd ever seen a Muslim exorcism. Every religion has a concept of demons and spirits, and believes that you can become possessed by them, but you rarely see the non-Christian versions on screen.

From the minute I saw The Sealed Soil, I knew I wanted to do something with the film related to possession, exorcism and religion. But a stronger theme that emerged for me is how a lot of these stories are about women. Possession is used either by the characters or by the film’s writer to demonize women for their independence or for wanting to be different from what society wants them to be. Whether they’re good or bad is kind of irrelevant.

SM: It’s such an international group of films—there are only two U.S. films in the whole series. Was that the main consideration when you started to select what to program?

YT: In general, I really like my programming to be very wide and international, so it's par for the course for me. But it all comes from the root idea of wanting to look at possession from different religions and different cultures. If I had done all American or all Western films we would have had 10 movies about Catholic exorcisms and I just didn’t think that was interesting.

I also really liked the idea that possession isn’t necessarily a demon being inside of you. In some of these films, witchcraft is treated as a form of possession, because often the accusation is that someone has been taken over by a spirit that is making them perform witchcraft, and people who play with spirits are more likely to let them in. In Tiger Stripes [2023], there’s a very different form of possession where the main character turns into a tiger and it’s all an allegory for going through puberty.

SM: Another thing I love about the assortment of films here is that there’s a spectrum of ambiguity in how possession is depicted. Sometimes it’s truly demonic, sometimes it’s clearly characters trying to punish a woman for transgressing, and other times it’s unclear whether something supernatural is happening. 

YT: I went back and forth as to whether these should be separated into different concepts, but overall I felt that when we look at the world at large, it’s all coming from the same place of demonization, whether that demonization is coming from the characters in the film or from the writers themselves. Which is not to say that the writers are being misogynistic. I think a lot of them are trying to express the misogyny in our world through the concept.

In the dramas where it’s unclear if they’re really possessed or not, the idea that your community or your family can turn on you in a second is almost scarier than a possessed girl projectile vomiting. Requiem [2006] is so deeply unsettling because it’s twofold: there is demonization coming from her family around the fact that she wants to be independent and go to college, and she’s starting to change and become less conservative than her parents. But it is also established that she’s been struggling with something, presumably a mental health issue, since high school that flares up from time to time. Regardless of the cause, the idea that you’re not believed or trusted by your own community, or you don’t know what’s going on in your own head, is scary and also a very real thing that people experience.

SM: Something I found interesting when I was reviewing the list of films in this program is that half of them were produced in the 1960s/1970s, and the other half came out in the 2000s/2010s. It feels cyclical in the same way that there are waves of feminist activism often accompanied by a backlash. Do you see these films as being in conversation historically? 

YT: It is very obvious that filmmakers were responding to what was happening in the world. The thing about programming is you can only pull from what’s been made and there just happens to be a heavy concentration in those two eras, so it wasn’t something I was trying to do going in but it’s definitely there. Some of them are more obvious in that they’re responding to a very specific moment in time. The Sealed Soil feels very pointed, since it was made in pre-revolution Iran. Jennifer’s Body [2009] also feels very pointed in retrospect. I remember when Jennifer’s Body came out, there was basically a smear campaign against it because it was a “weird girl horror movie with that bimbo Meghan Fox” so it couldn’t be good; and, in reality, it’s a fantastic movie. I think people hated it because it responded to the way that women were treated in the early 2000s. That era was really mean towards women in such a casual way.

SM: Were there any films that you would have liked to include but just didn’t fit the thematic parameters of the program’s concept?

YT: The big one was The Wailing [2016], which I absolutely love and we’ll find an excuse to play another time. The exorcism scene in that movie is great and it’s so interesting to see how it’s treated in traditional Korean culture and spiritualism. But the possession in that film is more like a virus. And while the main character’s daughter becomes possessed, the daughter is not the only one being possessed and the reason for her possession doesn’t have anything to do with her being a woman or girl going against the grain.

SM: Were any of the selections discoveries for you? 

YT: The Rites of May [1976] was a discovery for me because Mike de Leon is a filmmaker I only recently learned about. I was introduced to him because we ran the restoration of Kisapmata [1981] last year as well as Batch ‘81 [1981] and I had no idea that this movie [The Rites of May] that was perfectly situated for my program existed. He has such a great way of doing very scary drama that is really shocking and gets under your skin.

Another discovery for me that I’m really excited we’re showing is Il Demonio [1963]. Funny enough, when I went to book Alucarda [1977], I told the rights-holder about the concept for the program and he said, “You’re showing Il Demonio, right?” So I watched it and absolutely love it. It’s a beautiful black-and-white Italian film and it’s ultimately a relatable story about unrequited love. What’s special about this version is it’s a 4K restoration. When they restored it—I believe from the original negative—they found scenes that had been cut out of other versions, including this amazing “spider walk” scene that I couldn’t believe had been cut. So, it's playing in its full glory.

SM: Before we go, I want to highlight The Sealed Soil since you mentioned it was the impetus for the program. The story of its production is fascinating because Marva Nabili shot it in under a week, smuggled it out of Iran, and finished it in New York. It was only officially released in the United States last year—people should really go see it. 

YT: It’s really important to the history of Iranian filmmaking. Nabili is the first known female filmmaker from Iran. It’s amazing that it was made. It's amazing that it still exists. It’s amazing that it got restored and is out again. I worry sometimes when I put a film like this into a program like this that it will get lost among better-known Western films. It’s also around Halloween, so of course people want to see The Exorcist and Alucarda, but hopefully those get people’s attention and push them to check out something they’ve never heard of before.

“The Spirit Compels Her: Possession and the Demonization of Woman” runs October 30-November 5 at BAM.
