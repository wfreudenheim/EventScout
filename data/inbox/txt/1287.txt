L'INDISCRET

BY MADELYN SUTTON
NOVEMBER 24, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

François Reichenbach’s L’Indiscret (1968) opens on Kim, a young woman waiting for a train. The camera follows as she joins her paramour, Jean-Jacques, in a private car and continues to record as the two lovers don wedding apparel before embarking on a cruise ship, borrowing a pair of rings from their bemused fellow passengers and tying the proverbial knot. Throughout these sequences, the soundtrack captures only the surrounding sounds and mechanical noises of the train and the boat. Reichenbach never discloses the young couple’s conversations, thus establishing the voyeuristic position of his camera. Following the improvised nuptials and a brisk, sparkling celebration, Reichenbach approaches the couple and asks for permission to film them. But, he also confesses that he has, actually, already been recording their whirlwind romance, moved as he was by the love radiating from them and thus revealing to the audience that we were, in fact, voyeurs alongside him and his cameraman. Kim explains that she and Jean-Jacques aren’t married after all, but are being paid to pose as such for a photo essay. Flattered by Reichenbach’s charm, the couple agrees to continue to be filmed. The game, it seems, is officially on.

The rest of the film unfolds as a hypnotizing travelogue tracing a dizzying collage of apparently real conversations with ordinary people encountered dockside in the various Mediterranean villages the cruisers tour; surreptitiously filmed behavior on the cruise ship that swings from cheeky to childish to disgusting and back around again, mostly on the part of our lead couple and Sylvie, a swiftly seduced teenager recovering from a bout of mental illness; and the often touching and ridiculous pronouncements Reichenbach peppers throughout. Quick cross-cuts and alternating passages in color and black-and-white, as well as the occasional intrusion of the grim Atlantic City sounds of the harpsichord, keep the audience alienated and function as constant reminders that the film on view isn’t a documentary. And yet the film continues to pose as such in many ways, toying with the genre’s tropes and cinema vérité’s rhetorical tools. The director’s presence, shown carrying his camera before cutting to what he’s filming, and the glimpses via handheld camera of a tedious gala or a failed attempt by Jean-Jacques to steal a kiss from Kim assert a certain reality effect in their clumsiness and apparent guilelessness. Some sequences are even accompanied on the soundtrack by the subjects commenting on the director’s whims and whimsy. “What counts is to make a film from the heart,” Reichenbach tells his stars. His statement, as alluring as it is, is also meaningless.

Reichenbach was a prolific French filmmaker whose directorial credits include the Short Film Palme d’Or–winning documentary La Douceur du village (1964); the Best Documentary Academy Award winner Arthur Rubinstein – The Love of Life (1970); and, together with Eddy Matalon, the 1967 short film for Serge Gainsbourg and Brigitte Bardot’s “Bonnie and Clyde,” which accompanied the song on Scopitone jukeboxes. He was also, notably, a cinematographer and producer on Orson Welles’s F for Fake (1973), a rumination on authenticity and forgery that, according to Jonathan Rosenbaum, was “not a documentary” but “a new kind of film” closer to the personal essay than what the genre usually indicated. L’Indiscret was made quite early in Reichenbach’s wide-ranging career and serves as a fascinating exploration of what a “film from the heart” might be, as well as the creative uses and subtle abuses of such claims.

By the end of L’Indiscret, one is left with the impression of a bizarre, heated episode in which reality was perhaps the least present or impactful element. After all, the cruise ship is the place of exception par excellence, untethered from the rules and regulations (and relationships) of terrestrial life. At the end of the voyage, Kim disembarks, buoyant with relief; Sylvie gazes at the water more lost than ever; and Jean-Jacques “drives a car too fast,” per Reichenbach’s narration. The film’s ultimate revelation is about the dangerous seductions of the documentary format for its subjects and its makers, all of whom were pulled in by the camera’s winking eye for a beautiful and brutal 84 minutes.

L’Indiscret screens this evening, November 24, at L’Alliance New York as part of the series “Version restaurée.”
