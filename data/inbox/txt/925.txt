AFTER DREAMING

BY CHRIS SHIELDS
OCTOBER 25, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Fluidity. Distortion. The Primacy of the image These are some of the ideas that came to my mind while watching After Dreaming (2025). The narrative of Christine Haroutounian’s film, which concerns the ongoing violence and cultural trauma in Armenia, is not a happy one, but its languid visual beauty transcends the grim realities it explores.

After a well digger is mistaken for an enemy and killed, Claudette, a young relative of the murdered man, is sent away by her family. Atom, a soldier, is tasked with escorting her on the trip while the funeral takes place. The two embark on a road trip in which they face the social, political, and spiritual landscape in the aftermath of the Second Nagorno-Karabakh War, which ended in 2022, though small skirmishes continue despite peace talks between Azerbaijan and Armenia. They build a relationship, all the while Atom shields her from the truth about her murdered grandfather. The “Dreaming” of the film’s title is not a misnomer, as Hartoutounian invokes both stark realism and surreality, often subtly blurring (literally) the lines between the two.

How one sees is just as important as what we see in After Dreaming. The images Hartoutounian presents are often hazy and undulating, with a single point of focus vignetted by clouds of light and darkness. Her attention to the image is explicitly reflexive in certain moments, and what it projects is key to understanding the Armenian culture, myths, and psyche the film explores. At one point, the viewer is confronted with the pages of a pornographic magazine and at another point a bridal photo shoot. The two sequences rhyme, creating a diode through which a single current flows, charging the social/visual possibilities for women (sex objects or brides). In a marvelously brilliant stroke, the two discreet sequences thematically unite in the last pose of the bridal shoot: the bride stripped of everything but her lingerie and veil (perhaps a sly allusion to Marcel Duchamp). Beyond this latent tracing of misogyny, there is also sorrow for young brides without grooms. War has led Claudette to ask God: “Where have all the men gone?” Toward the end of the film, there is another vision of brides, now on a television broadcast en masse. That image imbues her sadness with a national dimension.

After Dreaming’s centerpiece scene is a 16-minute musical performance featuring dazzling choreography of both performers and camera. Atom and Claudette arrive at a group marriage ceremony and after they consummate their own union, a hypnotic, longform folk music celebration begins.The multi-musician, tetrachord-based improvisation is both musically determined and open to spontaneity and variation. The sequence and performance therein seem to embody the film’s reckoning with the past and the expressive possibilities of the present. The luminous, idiosyncratic visual poetry of Hartoutounian’s film is searching for freedom in a highly repressive milieu, as light sneaks through the stark shadows in many of its wandering frames. This noble effort is echoed by one of the film’s most striking lines: “Through work one ascends to the heavenly kingdom.”

After Dreaming screens this afternoon, October 25, at Asia Society as part of the series “Our Century: Spiraling Paths Through Armenian Cinema.” Christine Haroutounian will be in attendance for a Q&A.
