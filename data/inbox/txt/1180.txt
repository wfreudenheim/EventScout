THE ANGEL LEVINE

BY EMILY ROSE APTER
NOVEMBER 15, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

It’s New York City in the late 1960s, and Morris Mishkin (Zero Mostel) is an aging Jewish tailor with a sick wife, a bad back, and—god forbid—a goy for a son-in-law. Mishkin attributes his misfortunes to a divine curse placed on his family. “I don't expect too much help from God,” he tells the Black woman staffing the bottlenecked welfare office. “I rely on you.”

The Angel Levine (1970) opens in carnivalesque fashion—first as farce and only later as tragedy. A waltzy harpsichord swells above the opening scene, infusing it with Old World frenzy and inciting the incident out of which the fablelike plot spirals. Mishkin witnesses a man (Harry Belafonte) slip a fur coat off the back of a woman’s chair and under his own leather jacket, only to be struck and killed by a yellow cab while fleeing. That same night, after tending to his wife, Mishkin finds the thief at his kitchen table. He introduces himself as Alexander Levine, a Black Jewish angel-on-probation, granted 24 hours back on earth. His task: to restore Mishkin’s faith. Only then will a miracle take place and the angel earn his wings.

Czech surrealism meets gritty New Hollywood, set over the course of one, long, never-ending day. Mishkin starts as a skeptic—on the surface, of Levine’s angel status but, more revealingly, of whether he could really be a Jew. Mishkin prompts Levine to recite the Hamotzi (he does with an impeccable Yiddish accent) and asks if he is circumcised (Mishkin takes his word for it). For Mishkin, proof lies in a narrow set of cultural conventions that have historically discredited the breadth of embodied Black-Jewish experience. But with Belafonte (a mixed-race Black socialist) and Ján Kadár (a Slovak communist who claimed he only “acted as a Jew” when interned in a Nazi labor camp) helming this story, the question of what makes a Jew grows more encompassing as the film goes on.

Based on a 1955 short story by Bernard Malamud, The Angel Levine was brought to the screen by Belafonte's independent production company. Born in Harlem to Jamaican immigrants, Belafonte was himself a quarter Jewish (his paternal grandfather was of Sephardic ancestry). Throughout his life, Belafonte was an outspoken critic of imperialism and apartheid, as well as a mentee of Paul Robeson and a confidant of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. In the mid-1950s, he was blacklisted by the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC). Levine marked his return to cinema after a forced, decade-long hiatus.

For Belafonte, art could be fiercely political—insofar as it was made by the values it espoused. On Levine, he convinced the labor guilds to employ young people of color and assembled a politically committed ensemble of collaborators. While Belafonte was drawn to the material for its candid look at Black-Jewish New York, Blackness in the Malamud story functions more as a symbolic status than a lived material reality. To flesh out Levine and other Black characters, Bill Gunn, one of the few Black screenwriters working in Hollywood at the time, joined as the film’s co-writer. Fresh off the screenplay for Hal Ashby’s The Landlord (1970), Gunn enlivened The Angel Levine with his trademark spirit of play and tenderness, as well as his incisive eye for social hierarchies. Notably, he introduced the character of Sally (played by Gloria Foster, later known for her role as The Oracle in The Matrix), Levine’s forsaken lover, with whom love flounders in both earthly and metaphysical realms. (That same year, Warner Bros. permanently shelved Stop!, what would have been Gunn’s directorial debut, for its explicit scenes of queer, interracial group sex; to this day, the film remains unreleased and available only in bootleg circulation).

Kadár, Levine’s director, joined the project likely through his relationship with Yiddish stage actress Ida Kamińska, who was cast as Mishkin’s wife. Born in Budapest, Kadár studied filmmaking in Czechoslovakia until forced into a Nazi labor camp in 1939 (the rest of his family was killed in Auschwitz). Upon his release, he joined the Communist Party and worked making Socialist-Realist documentaries and satirical comedies. The Angel Levine was his first English-language film after immigrating to the U.S. in 1968. The transition brought him into the orbit of American communists. Among them was Brooklyn-born Zero Mostel, who’d come up teaching for the New Deal Public Works of Art Project and performing comedy in nightclubs. Mostel too was targeted by HUAC and restricted from working. By the time he joined The Angel Levine, however, he had gained international fame for playing Tevye in the 1964 Broadway production of Fiddler on the Roof and Max Bialestock in Mel Brooks’s The Producers (1967).

It is tempting to probe the Mishkin-Levine relationship for an allegory of so-called “Black-Jewish relations”—a phrase that appears in nearly all writings on both the film and its source material. The term itself springs from the Great Migration period, during which migrants from Eastern Europe, the Caribbean, and the American South lived proximately in working-class enclaves of Harlem and Central Brooklyn. As a catch-all, however, it often implies a transhistorical coalition, which at once dichotomizes Black/Jewish experiences and flattens the specificities of each lived, racialized reality. Although Jews made up swaths of the Communist Party and labor movement, as James Baldwin lays out in his seminal “Negroes Are Anti-Semitic Because They're Anti-White” (1967), Black people largely encountered Jews in the position of landlord and shopkeeper—as well as social worker, teacher, and tailor. Plus, the notion of an essentialized Black-Jewish alliance elides the fact that antiblackness helped to instantiate the assimilation of Ashkenazi Jews into middle-class American whiteness.

The Angel Levine concerns itself narratively with such fault lines—and emergent solidarities—and the imperfect ways that structural forces are negotiated inside relationships. In the first scene between Mishkin and Levine, Mishkin calls him a schvartz (the Yiddish slur for Black people). Such casually deployed (and culturally specific) racism makes clear the way Mishkin’s entrenched anti-blackness stems not just from his skepticism of Levine’s Jewishness,but from the way whiteness affords him real, material advantages. “Every white mother up there was going through them gates,” Levine decries. “But me? They put me on probation.”

If there ever was a Black-Jewish coalition to speak of, then it fractured materially and mythologically just before The Angel Levine went into production with the Teachers’ Strike of 1968. September of that year, the mostly Jewish United Federation of Teachers (UFT) shut down the NYC public school system, with 54,000 teachers on the picket line and over one million students out of school. They protested not management directly but the newly-minted Community Control Board, stewarded by Black and Puerto Rican parents in the Ocean Hill-Brownsville neighborhood of Brooklyn. Up until 1960, Ocean Hill-Brownsville was majority first- and second-generation Ashkenazi immigrants; in fact, it’s where Zero Mostel was born in 1915, his parents having fled Galicia in the decades prior. By the post-war period, Jews with the means left en masse, while new migrants settled from the South and Puerto Rico.Yet Jews maintained positions as teachers, administrators, and Board of Education members. In 1967, outraged by under-resourced and racially segregated schools, as well as the lack of community representation, parents occupied a Board of Ed meeting. They initiated control over curricula, budgets, and day-to-day operations, and subsequently transferred out 19 Jewish teachers and administrators. Mayor John Lindsay formalized the community control experiment by decentralizing the district, prompting UFT to declare its historic strike.

The significance of the Teachers’ Strike lies not just in the tactical divide it represented between traditional labor and self-organized communities, nor solely along lines of identity, but in the long-standing struggle of working-class communities of color to govern their own institutions. The transition from civil rights to Black Power was underway, breaking out of the former’s rights-oriented framework and into the latter’s commitment to staunch self-determination. White progressive Jews who had participated in civil rights (members of UFT included) failed to accept this militant approach to racial justice and the threat it posed to the powers that be.

The late ‘60s also marked a turning point in the Zionization of the American-Jewish political apparatus: in 1967 Israel occupied the West Bank and East Jerusalem, while the following year the right-wing extremist Jewish Defense League was founded in NYC. As the pro-Israel lobby grew its political influence, the weaponization of antisemitism emerged as a pervasive political strategy—one that the president of UFT embraced without hesitation. For revolutionary groups like The Black Panther Party (supporters of Community Control), increased Zionist aggression only fortified solidarity with Palestine.

The notion of an enduring Black-Jewish alliance thus says more about white Jewish racial anxieties—nostalgia, even, for a time in which Jews fought more meaningfully on the side of the oppressed—than it does about actual Black experiences. And the specter of that myth hovers above this urban fairy tale. Rather than reinforce such an essentialized bond, the film draws attention to racialized stratifications within New York’s working classes. When Mishkin cannot afford his wife’s seven-dollar medication, for instance, Levine creates a diversion at the pharmacy, swipes the pills from the countertop, and unveils the bottle to Mishkin. The gesture sparks a fiery debate between them about the warped nature of theft and honesty. Mishkin insists that he always pays for what he gets, and yet he refuses to use the last of his food money to reimburse the pharmacist. Levine points out his hypocrisy: “I wonder what makes you white folks so honest, and all us Black folks so dishonest hm? [...] Ain’t it because somebody’s cheating us all the time, is it? You pay when you have the money. And when you don’t have the money you steal.”

In a comradely gesture, Mishkin instead uses the money to buy Levine a whiskey. Tenderness grows between the two men. And yet friendship, solidarity even, flits between possibility, fantasy, and tragic absurdity. Time and again, Mishkin fails to break from the myopia of his own suffering. The film takes up these negotiations with a tonal mishmash of melodrama, satire, and hyperrealism. And it is such formal and narrative flashpoints that occasionally knock the characters loose from the fixedness of their individual paths and toward something like genuine recognition.

Mishkin finds himself searching for Levine at the historic Commandment Keepers synagogue in West Harlem, home to the country’s largest Ethiopian and Afro-Caribbean Jewish congregation. A Black rabbi, garbed in a tallit and yarmulke, is engrossed in group study. But Levine is nowhere to be found. In a sort of clumsy solitude, Mishkin ambles through the city streets toward an empty lot along the Harlem River. Whether a miracle has occurred is difficult to say. If faith has emerged, it was not from the rejection of skepticism but from the embrace of contradiction. In the face of sweeping structural oppressions—and through the mire of individual subjecthood—the viewer is left wondering if something like salvation might yet be possible.

The Angel Levine screens tomorrow, November 16, at the Roxy on 35mm as part of the series “The Ravishing Cinema of Bill Gunn.”
