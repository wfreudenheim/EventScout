IL DEMONIO

BY AYANNA DOZIER
OCTOBER 30, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

There are few more dangerous threats to a community than an uninhabited woman who wants. For time immemorial, single women with power—be that sexual, mystical, communal, or agricultural—have been figures marked as destructive to themselves and the communities they exist within. The wanting woman is a sinful woman and is positioned as an inverse to her ordained role as a servant to the lord, the church, her husband, and her family—having, let alone expressing, wants beyond this is simply an abomination to god’s will for women. It is this common trope of the possessed woman that is the basis of Brunello Rondi’s solo feature film debut, Il demonio (1963).

Lusciously shot on black and white film by cinematographer Carlo Bellero across several towns in the Basilicata region of Southern Italy, Il demonio mixes folklore with the ethnographic research of anthropologist Ernesto De Martino. De Martino’s field research on myth, religion, and ritual in early 20th century Southern Italy informs Rondi’s eerie take on the possession narrative, in which the actual demons are not the ones plaguing our protagonist but those that drive the townsfolk toward social conformity.

Daliah Lavi delivers an unruly hysterical performance as Purif (diminutive name for “Purificazione,” which translates to “purification process”) that is shockingly modern for 1963. Il demonio opens with Purif placing a blood curse on her ex-lover in an effort to sabotage his forthcoming nuptials. Labeled as a demoness, she is forced to undergo a variety of unsuccessful exorcisms by her family, local mystics, and ultimately the church. It is the last exorcism where Lavi delivers a spellbinding spider walk—a decade before one was filmed for 1973’s The Exorcist (though that sequence was not seen by audiences until the Director’s Cut in 2000). Unlike the more common image of the spider walk, which is rushed and framed as a jump-cut of uncanny monstrosity of the human form, Lavi’s depiction is slow and sensual; the camera becomes embodied and turns upside down to follow Purif’s perspective and, as a result, shifts the audience’s perception to that of being possessed.

Purif rarely seems as distressed by her cahoots with the Devil as others do in her commune. In fact, she brazenly goes to the church to pray to Lucifer in the presence of others. Perhaps it is the lack of shame that makes her so dangerous, for Il demonio addresses this tension in a pivotal sequence of public confession. In a large circle, townsfolk bring their cursed objects and confess their sins to the Bishops and their community. One man confesses to having lustful thoughts toward his teenage daughter (it’s heavily implied that he sexually assaults her) and is forgiven by the Church because he feels shamed. Purif, though she confesses her arrangement with the Devil and is physically tormented by it in various scenes, is less interested in the absolution of her possession via confession alone. Purif recognizes the power she has in her body through this possession and finds that the pleasure of colluding with the Devil outweighs the harm.

And therein lies the problem, to be at ease with sin (that is your flesh) blocks control of your body by the church and becomes a model for dissidence for others to follow. In many ways, Purif demonstrates just how—to quote another revival film, Ken Russells’s The Devils (1971)—sin can become as catching as the plague. And, if not addressed, will pervert others toward the will of their wants and not the Church’s.

Il demonio screens tomorrow evening, October 31, at BAM as part of the series “The Spirit Compels Her: Possession and the Demonization of Women.”
