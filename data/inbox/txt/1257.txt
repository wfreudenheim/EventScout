Susan Hiller’s “Bad Dreams”

Kirsty Bell

Susan Hiller, Channels, 2013. Multi-channel video installation, dimensions variable. Courtesy of the estate of Susan Hiller, Lisson Gallery. Photo by Frank Sperling.

November 21, 2026

October 17, 2026–January 10, 2027

Review

KW Institute for Contemporary Art, Berlin

The central conundrum in Susan Hiller’s work is the physical manifestation of the otherwise invisible. In this compact retrospective of the artist, who died in 2019 aged seventy-eight, the assembled works utilize what Hiller called “social facts”: first-person testimonies or adopted cultural artifacts such as movies, found photographs, or domestic objects. These materials are called on to articulate “ghosts,” which she defined as “cultural discards, fragments, and things that are invisible to most people but intensely important to a few.”

The exhibition begins with the familiar, if not overfamiliar. A furnished living room, reeking of new carpet, with the studied interchangeability of countless Airbnb interiors: minimalist arm chairs, designer side tables, leafy plastic plants, fake candles. The only thing that seems out of place is the television, on which an image of bright red flames flickers. Headphones provide a soundtrack: a faint crackling of fire, a breathy voice thick with static and conspiracy. The nearby wall text claims that the TV replaced the fire as the repository for figments of the imagination: when transmission finished at the end of the day, some viewers reported seeing strange images appearing as if from inside their TV sets. This is what the voices describe: the uncanny emerging from within the most familiar setting.

The work is titled Belshazzar’s Feast after the biblical story in which mysterious writing appears on the wall during a banquet and King Belshazzar calls upon “the wise men of Babylon” to decipher it. While Hiller’s original work was made in 1983–84, she stipulated that subsequent installations should use up-to-date furnishings. But does a TV set still make sense within this generic contemporary interior, in our era of twenty-four-hour content streams, where programming never switches off? If the TV replaced the open fire as a blank space for projections of the imagination, what replaces the TV? Where does the uncanny reside now that the glitch and fuzz of analog transmissions have been transformed into smooth and uninterrupted digital image streams?

Hiller’s works traffic in interruptions of the ordinary; ruptures in the fabric of the everyday where doubt and mystery reside. In Resounding (Infrared) (2013), a large-scale projected image of signal interference—red and black blobs, turquoise and magenta fuzz, a silver crackling line of sound—is accompanied by a spoken soundtrack describing encounters with UFOs. In each case a common activity (“we were standing on the street chatting when …”) is interrupted by the appearance of something unexpected, unfamiliar, and most importantly, unidentified, usually in the form of a conglomeration of bright lights. For Hiller, who initially trained as an anthropologist, it is in the aggregate that the wonder appears: the similarities between one account and another as each grapples with the limitations of language to account for something that goes beyond “normal” experience. The acts of transmission and translation that her works engage to visually manifest unseen phenomena also form the core of her fascination. The slippage in translating image into words, or words into image, and the very limitations of language and narrative to explain things of another order.

The centerpiece in KW’s sunken main hall is Channels (2013), a vast bank of stacked analog television sets, their screens showing flat blue, the fuzz of white noise, or a green oscillating line. A compelling visual structure, the point again is the soundtrack: first-person testimonies faintly audible above over the TV’s hum and static, this time recounting near-death experiences. Hiller was again interested in the common patterns of this experience—“I felt I was floating …”; “I don’t want to die …”—which offered brief glimpses beyond that final boundary: death.

Various smaller wall-bound objects adopt the material language of domesticity. A series of paintings on patterned wallpaper designed for little boys’ bedrooms, featuring trucks, helicopters, Ghostbusters, and explosions, are partially obscured with all-over automatic patterning. The earliest works are “Studies for ‘Home’” (1968) with toy kitchen sets where miniature canisters labeled “teeth” and “fingernails” sit alongside “tea” and “sugar,” or a macabre-looking knife replaces a ladle. A category shift can tip a situation over from the normal into the extraordinary. The question is where the boundaries are drawn.

“Bad Dreams” is an immersion in the paranormal, occluding Hiller’s other enquiries into the marginal or overlooked. The double-screen projection (Wild Talents, 1997) shows excerpts from horror movies in which young boys on one screen, and young girls on the other, engage in acts of telekinesis, bending forks, breaking mirrors, or inducing spontaneous combustion. These movie sources tap into a nostalgic fascination with a 1970s era horror aesthetic, but do they hold in the present day? Are the sources of horror now not quite different, from the sinister unknowns of AI to the impending disasters of climate catastrophe? Zombies and vampires rather than poltergeists? Now that screens are never blank and glitch and fuzz have been noise-canceled away, would we even notice an unidentified flying object when out walking the dog? Can doubt and mystery still be indulged without tipping straight into paranoia? The photographic prints of levitating bodies here, found by Hiller back in the early days of the internet, seem drained of strangeness and fall flat. Dream Screens, commissioned by DIA in 1996 for its “Artists’ Web Projects,” presents a single computer monitor alone in one room, providing access to a catalog of dreams from the time when the internet was a privileged repository for such esoterica. Today, in the era of the deep fake, where the strange is likely to be met with mistrust, where does the potential for transcendence reside?

Kirsty Bell is a writer, critic, and lecturer living in Berlin. Her next book Mother’s Mother will be published by Fitzcarraldo Editions in March 2027.
