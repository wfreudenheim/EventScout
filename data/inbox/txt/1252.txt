GALAXIE

By MAX LEVIN
NOVEMBER 20, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

The luminaries in Gregory J. Markopoulos’s Galaxie (1966) range from W.H. Auden, Allen Ginsburg, and Susan Sontag to Maurice Sendak, Jasper Johns, and Jonas Mekas. In approximately three-minute segments (one 100-foot roll of 16mm film edited and composed in-camera), Markopoulos filmed his subjects in their home or studio, often with a representative prop or object. Shirley Clarke has a Felix the Cat toy and Paul Thek smolders with glazed insects and one of his Technological Reliquary meat pieces. Dr. Hendrik Ruitenbeek (one of the earliest psychiatrists to depathologize homosexuality) holds an original letter of Freud’s. While many appear austere or even bored, the art historian Gregory Battcock offers the most charismatic on-screen presence, rolling around on a zebra skin rug and taking calls on the telephone.

In addition to superstars in Markopoulos’s circle, Galaxie also gravitates to those who brokered power and influence behind the scenes. Parker Tyler appears first, the poet and critic who wrote about Markopoulos as “an instinctive craftsman of unusual and rare ability.” Midway through is Jerome Hill, the filmmaker and philanthropist who helped fund Anthology Film Archives. The final subjects in the film are the taxicab heirs Robert and Ethel Scull, who amassed a preeminent collection of post-war art and are seen beneath Andy Warhol’s Flowers.

The otherwise silent film is scored by a Hindu bell that sounds between portraits, with each proceeding segment followed by an additional toll. Uneven and accumulating, Galaxie is a portrait-trance film that registers the style and stature of mid-1960s art and culture through the eyes of a visionary director who would soon fall out with that world.

In 1967, Markopoulos began a self-imposed exile in Europe, and in 1974 published a broadside in Variety decrying the New York film scene and specifically dissociating himself from New American Cinema and Anthology Film Archives. The latter had just lost its space at 425 Lafayette Street, and Markopoulos anticipated inferior screening conditions to follow: “I do not wish my work (and I have been making films for nearly thirty years, privately) to be shown in some lower East Side establishment.” Thereafter, Markopoulos began removing his films from distribution in order to control their screening conditions and also because of a new project: cutting up prints (in some cases destroying originals) to assemble Eniaios (1948-1990), a 24-cycle, 80-hour magnum opus composed of rhythmic sections of black and clear film leder interrupted by fragments of Markopoulos’s filmography. Cannibalizing his own work placed Markopoulos further at odds with Mekas, who insisted on protecting the films and did not return them. This resulted in the circumstance that certain versions of Markopoulos’s films “survived only in those copies [held by Anthology]” according to the British curator Mark Webber in his introduction to Film as Film: The Collected Writings of Gregory J. Markopoulos (2014).

Footage from Galaxie appears in Cycle 13 of Eniaios, which first screened in 2024. A short length of black / white / black separates Thek and Auden, followed by clipped views of the ruins of the Theater of Dionysius. In Markopoulos’s gargantuan supercut of his life’s work, the maverick visual artist and the protean poet are preserved together, re-edited after the death of both subjects and nodding to intergenerational legacy. In Galaxie, the fullness of the 30 portraits commingle with the knowledge that organizing glimpses of people and places across time would become Markopoulos’s modus operandi. His work is concerned with the epic, erotic energy produced by the afterimage, which disperses, according to Markopoulos, “like the effects of the sun… Does this sustain what we call eternity?”

Galaxie screens this evening, November 20, and on November 30, at Anthology Film Archives on 16mm as part of the series “Faces: Moving-Image Portraiture.”
