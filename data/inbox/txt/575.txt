Ocean Reveries
By D. Graham Burnett

Attention matters. We know this. We worry about our “attention spans.” We fret about the “attention economy.” The sea matters, too. We also know this. Even when the ocean’s out of sight, its churning depths and inner workings will decide the planet’s fate.

Important as they are, both the sea and our attention can slip through our fingers. In what follows, I propose we cup our hands and hold them—together.

In the last 15 years, a new kind of technologically sophisticated, computationally intensive, and financially ruthless business model (what I and others have called “human fracking”) has placed the attentional behaviors of human beings under remarkable and destructive pressure. In this context, a reframing and reconsideration of many aspects of our cognitive and sensory and social and environmental lives in explicitly attentional terms may have emancipatory and transformative potential.

The Sea Before Us
By Jessica Riskin

The oceans, which gave birth to life on Earth, also continually generate simple, tiny beings that create and recreate the surface geology of the planet, including islands, mountains, and coastlines. This was the radical idea of Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, the French naturalist who coined the word “biology” (biologie) in 1802 and published the first theory of evolution in his 1809 magnum opus, The Zoological Philosophy. Lamarck was a revolutionary thinker who brazenly usurped God’s monopoly on Creation, ascribing it instead to mortal beings, down to the very humblest of them. Life began, according to Lamarck, not when God created the world, but when forces such as electricity and heat acted upon the elements of inanimate matter, spontaneously generating tiny, rudimentary beings—for plants, the simplest algae, and for animals, the globular infusoria visible under a microscope that he called “monads,” using a philosophical term derived from the Greek monas meaning “unit.”

The Village Beneath the Waves
By David Gruber

“Holy fucking shit!” I exclaimed from the port side of CETI-1, a 40-foot research vessel, while sailing off the island of Dominica. Behind me, the writer Elizabeth Kolbert, who was serendipitously reporting on our whale research that morning for The New Yorker, overheard my awestruck expletive. I am not a frequent public F-bomb dropper. But this occasion seemed like an appropriate exception.

I’m a marine biologist and the lead of Project CETI (Cetacean Translation Initiative), a transdisciplinary nonprofit and National Geographic Society program dedicated to translating the communications of whales. My team and I were doing what we normally do each morning: locating sperm whales to understand how they communicate and conduct their social lives. Our lead whale biologist, Dr. Shane Gero, has studied these creatures for over two decades. That morning, we came across 11 whales, two family lines known as Unit A. They had gathered unusually close together, moving up and down in a marguerite formation—a tight, flowerlike circular grouping whales used to protect one another, often by keeping calves or vulnerable animals at its center—without making their usual deep dives. Their behavior seemed more defensive than usual.

In a sudden change of behavior, there was intense thrashing and blood appeared in the water. The fear across the CETI team was palpable. We thought that perhaps a member of the whale unit was in grave danger.

Then, among the turbulent and foaming waters, a tiny sperm whale head appeared in the middle of the commotion.

FLOODLINES: WHERE CITIES AND OCEANS MEET

Friday, September 25 at 6 pm

Presented in partnership with the Wellcome Trust, FLOODLINES is an evening of music, poetry, science, and conversation about what happens when waters rise and how cities heal. Here, the flood is both a literal threat and a metaphor for social and infrastructural saturation.

Hosted by the acclaimed writer Hanif Abdurraqib, with music by Hurray for the Riff Raff and poetry from Eileen Myles, the night will feature curator and design theorist Paola Antonelli leading a conversation with leading scientists, filmmakers, architects, and activists, along with libations and installations in the garden and a DJ set by Madame Gandhi.
