THE PERSONAL AND THE POLITICAL

BY MACKENZIE LUKENBILL
OCTOBER 17, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

San Francisco’s Multi-Media Resource Center, operational throughout the 1970s, was a fertile crescent for gay and lesbian experimental film, bolstered to an even more mythological status due to its unlikely beginnings. The Methodist Reverend Dr. Ted McIlvenna, moved by the discrimination and violence toward gay men and women that he had encountered around his parish of Glide Church, convened a progressive caucus of religious leaders and queer civilians (“30 clergy and homosexual persons,” according to the Erotic Heritage Museum’s obituary of McIlvenna) in the Bay Area in 1964, founding the Council on Religion and the Homosexual. His experience at CRH lit an inquisitive fire in the Reverend. He began to dream of the possibilities of educating his community on the lesser-talked about aspects of human sexuality. The result was the National Sex Forum, founded in 1968. Among the NSF’s first projects was to produce educational films, on which McIlvenna collaborated with the like-minded Minister Laird Sutton. The Multi-Media Resource Center was established as a distributor for the NSF’s work, but it soon became the host for a number of sex-minded experimental work as well. Releasing films by Coni Beeson, Jan Oxenberg and Barbara Hammer, MMRC became a monumental, if overlooked, force in shaping lesbian moving image work in the United States.

Anthology Film Archives’s sprawling survey on the MMRC, organized by Screen Slate contributor Lucy Talbot Allen, collects an array of MMRC’s output—from the confessional, to the didactic, to the experiential—augmented with other contemporaneous West Coast queer analog experiments. The “Personal and the Political” program, collecting works from MMRC’s Anne Severson and George Csicsery, among others, is steeped in community discussion around safe sex, gender dynamics, sex work, abortion, and the female body. The primary interests of these films are both in spreading crucial information about health and letting the underrepresented speak; in this sense, MMRC could be seen as the link between the anarchist Portapak collectives of the late ‘60s, such as France’s Les Insoumuses and the revolution of HIV/AIDS videomakers in the ‘80s and ‘90s.

The most striking MMRC film in the program is perhaps Self-Health (1974), directed by a collective of women (Catherine Allen, Judy Irola, Allie Light, and Joan Musante) in association with the San Francisco Women’s Health Center. Framed within a constrictive space that seems to be a home, illuminated by blasts of oddly over-exposed white light, the film documents a group of women learning to perform an examination of the cervix upon each other in real-time. The film only breaks from its vérité conceit at the end, when an off-screen voice announces: “We’re learning from our own bodies: teaching ourselves and each other how each of us is unique and the same, and what we need in order to be healthy.” Despite the varying tones in these films (Anne Severson’s I Change I am the Same [1969] is under a minute and very effectively goofy), this line could well be a rallying cry for all of the titles in MMRC’s catalog.

“The Personal and the Political: Sexuality, Romance and Women’s Lib” screens tonight, October 17, at Anthology Film Archives as part of the series “Sexual Cinéma Vérité: The Multi-Media Resource Center in Context.”
