CROCODILE DOSE

BY NICOLAS PEDRERO-SETZER
NOVEMBER 18, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Crocodile Dose (2026), the debut feature from Jason Waters, doesn’t encapsulate the moment it was made in so much as explode it. A teen-comedy in the style of Superbad (2007) and Project X (2012), Waters’s film follows three college freshmen on summer break who are on a quest to gather drugs, liquor, and “bitches” ahead of a much-anticipated reunion bash. While the film’s basic scenario isn’t novel, the manner in which Waters weaponizes digital media to convey it is. What Crocodile Dose showcases is a new brand of filmmaking (if the term still applies) that embraces the look and logic of present-day digital ecosystems. It is fractured and frenetic, and unlike most things out in cinemas now even if it exists as a template for a cinema-to-come.

Curtis (Chase Mackay), Jimmy (Graham Cox), and their go-with-the-flow sidekick Abe (Waters), occupy virtually every frame of Crocodile Dose. Even when not in the same room, the editing is such that a FaceTime call from one to the other means they’ll end up inhabiting the same screen space. The on-screen ubiquity of these faces (filtered through old camera lenses, Snapchat filters, webcams, and more) stresses the degree to which their constant communication belies their broken fraternity. As a whole, Crocodile Dose can be taken as a treatise on the weird ways in which Gen-Z spends all of their time searching for intimate connections that their incessant online contact precludes. The film’s last leg, which sees Curtis enter a full INLAND EMPIRE-like dimension after his phone dies and he has a tiff with his buddies, doubles down on this, bringing into view just how incapable of processing the world on individual terms an entire generation has become as a result of constant technological mediation.

The assaultive visual quality of Crocodile Dose does wonders to underline the alienating effects of contemporary technology; of course, much of it is there to incite laughter, yet the longer one has to sit with Crocodile Dose, the more difficult it becomes to laugh at what is essentially a meme-ified portrait of modern youth. While frivolity has always been a staple of late-teenagedom, Waters’s endless supply of it makes evident something scarier about his protagonists: their pervasive inability to deal with life on serious terms, whether it be due to brainrot, digital atomization, or other environmental stressors. Crocodile Dose is—because of, and not despite, its internet-brain-laden humor and patterning—an authentic representation of the sense that there is no more difference between IRL and online, and how that collapse has set forth all new sorts of social obstacles for young people everywhere in search of leisure, laughs, and love.

Crocodile Dose screens this evening, November 18, at Alamo Downtown Brooklyn preceded by Heather Landsman’s ee.
