FACES: MOVING-IMAGE PORTRAITURE
November 20 – December 1
Exploring the tradition of portraiture in the cinema, this series surveys the variety of approaches filmmakers have taken to depicting the human face – and by extension, the mysterious relationship between the face and the depths of thought, emotion, and personality that human physiognomy variously suggests, reveals, or conceals.

More often than not, when films are referred to as “portraits” what is meant are fictional or non-fictional narrative accounts of a particular character or subject: films that recount a subject’s history, that tell their story, that delve into their psychology or document their interactions with their environment. This series instead focuses on films that function more literally as portraits in the art-historical sense – that follow in the tradition of the painted, drawn, sculpted, or photographed portrait, but that introduce the elements of motion and time, as well as montage.

Including everything from Andy Warhol’s SCREEN TESTS (perhaps the most famous and ambitious collection of filmic portraits) and Gregory Markopoulos’s majestic GALAXIE, to Yoko Ono’s FILM NO. 5 (SMILE) and FLUXFILM NO. 16 (the first version of BOTTOMS), to the work of other artists who have demonstrated a sustained engagement with the form (like Gérard Courant, Friedl vom Gröller, and Joan Logue), the series also features short portrait films by an enormous variety of filmmakers, including Stan Brakhage, Narcisa Hirsch, Tehching Hsieh, Kurt Kren, Maria Lassnig, Gunvor Nelson, Edward Owens, Ernst Schmidt Jr., and many others. All these artists have been captivated by the mysteries and the paradoxes of the human face, and preoccupied with the cinema’s capacity (or the limits thereof) to convey its complex beauty and to ponder its status as both portal and screen.

Gregory Markopoulos
GALAXIE
1966, 82 min, 16mm
A pinnacle of the art of filmic portraiture, Gregory Markopoulos’s GALAXIE comprises thirty short portraits of men and women from across the spectrum of the mid-1960s cultural scene in New York City. Its subjects include many of Markopoulos’s fellow experimental filmmakers – Storm De Hirsch, George & Mike Kuchar, Jonas Mekas, Shirley Clarke, Amy Taubin, and Ed Emshwiller – as well as figures from the literary and art worlds, including Parker Tyler, Jasper Johns, W.H. Auden, Allen Ginsberg, Kenneth Kelman, Maurice Sendak, Susan Sontag, and others. In keeping with Markopoulos’s short-form masterpieces MING GREEN, BLISS, and SORROWS, each of the individual portraits in GALAXIE was edited entirely in-camera, a near-miraculous feat given the intricate use of multiple layers of superimposition, all of it achieved by rewinding and making additional passes through the single 100-foot (ca. 3-minute) roll of 16mm film during the shoot. Initially intending to produce only a handful of portraits, Markopoulos ended up filming 40 different chapters, and ultimately incorporated the first 30 into the completed work (presented in the order they were created). Each of the portraits represents an exquisite visual record of the encounter between subject and filmmaker, while the film as a whole provides an extraordinary opportunity to observe the gradual development of Markopoulos’s approach to portraiture and in-camera editing.
Fri, Nov 20 at 6:30 and Mon, Nov 30 at 8:45.

Friedl vom Gröller
MAX TURNHEIM
2002-24, 57 min, 16mm, silent
Few contemporary filmmakers have demonstrated such a sustained commitment to the form of cinematic portraiture as Friedl vom Gröller, whose work is represented throughout this series. While most of her films are notably (and proudly) small in scale, MAX TURNHEIM is an exception, an ever-expanding collection of portraits of a subject she has returned to repeatedly throughout the years.

“An allegorical reflection upon physiosocial existence in the world. This to-date 13-part, long-term portrait of Max Turnheim brings the abundance of Friedl vom Gröller’s gaze to complete fruition. The film conceives its portrait through the compression of an entire phase of life, including separation from the parental home, the finding of an individual path, and a renewed longing for middle class institutions. The point of departure for all this content is a face seen over the course of time.” –Alejandro Bachmann
Fri, Nov 20 at 8:45.

Yoko Ono & John Lennon
FILM NO. 5 (SMILE)
1968, 49 min, 16mm-to-digital, silent
“[S]hot with a high-speed camera…FILM NO. 5 (SMILE) reveals John Lennon’s face, recorded at 333 frames per second for an extended duration, outdoors, in color, and accompanied by a sound track of outdoor sounds recorded at the same time the imagery was recorded. FILM NO. 5 (SMILE) divides roughly into two halves, one continuous shot each. During the first half, the film is a meditation on Lennon’s face, which is so still that on first viewing I wasn’t entirely sure for a while that the film was live action and not an optically printed photograph of Lennon smiling slightly. Though almost nothing happens in any conventional sense, however, the intersection of the high speed filming and our extended gaze creates continuous, subtle transformations. […] While the second shot is more active than the first, the amount of activity remains minimal by conventional standards (and unusually so even for avant-garde film). It is as though those of us in the theater and Lennon are meditating on each other from opposite sides of the cinematic apparatus, joined together by Ono in a lovely, hypnotic stasis.” –Scott MacDonald, “Yoko Ono: Ideas on Film”

ANDY WARHOL’S SCREEN TESTS + CONRAD VENTUR’S SCREEN TESTS REVISITED
These two programs pair Warhol’s seminal and often exquisite SCREEN TESTS – the silent, black-and-white, three-and-a-half minute filmic portraits he made between 1964-66, which grew to encompass 472 separate reels, portraying 189 individual subjects (many of them sitting for multiple portraits) – with Conrad Ventur’s 21st-century remakes. For Ventur’s (ongoing) project, he has sought out as many of the surviving SCREEN TESTees as possible, and restaged their portraits, resulting in a poetic tribute to Warhol’s films and to the transformations wrought by time.

CINÉMATON, PROGRAM 1
Gérard Courant
CINÉMATON
1978-85, 80 min selection (20 films, ca. 4-min each), Super-8mm-or-video-to-digital
“Since the 7th of February, 1978, I have made portraits on film of artistic and cultural personalities that I call CINÉMATON. There are ten rules for the cinematon, ten commandments that must not be deviated from: 1) A camera on a tripod; 2) A camera that does not move; 3) A close-up of a face; 4) No sound; 5) No change in focal length; 6) No change in the framing; 7) Running-time of 3 minutes and 25 seconds; 8) One take only; 9) No cutting during the shooting and no editing; 10) The person that is being filmed can do whatever he/she wants.
The aims of the CINÉMATON are multiple: the basic aim is to constitute an archive on art and especially on the film, theater, and entertainment scenes in those moments when the subject that is being filmed proposes a great moment of truth about herself or himself. Each CINÉMATON is preceded by three introductory title cards with the chronological number of the shooting of the portrait, the name of the person that is being filmed, his or her nationality, his profession, the date, hour, and place of the shooting. CINÉMATON has theoretically no end.” –Gérard Courant

NO FOOD NO MONEY NO JEWELS

SPECIAL SCREENING – FILMMAKERS IN PERSON!
NO FOOD NO MONEY NO JEWELS (AND MORE!)
November 20

Eve Sussman, Simon Lee, Angela Christlieb
NO FOOD NO MONEY NO JEWELS
2026, 35 min, DCP
A group of factory workers – Bear, Rabbit, Owl, Pig, and Donkey – are caught in a cycle of ridiculous labor, endlessly moving water around a scaffolding tower. Shot on a theater stage, where the characters are unable to exit, and at the Frankfurt Zoo, its own kind of stage with caged protagonists, NO FOOD NO MONEY NO JEWELS follows the trials and tribulations of workers clocking in at a makeshift factory. The film is both a literal and cinematic collage, merging vérité footage with a constructed theatrical set: a shifting arrangement of life-size found photographs make up the backdrop for each scene. The script combines original text, channelings of famous people, and rewrites of stories from House at Pooh Corner. In NO FOOD…, food becomes an object of obsession as the workers kill time in the break-room, search for treasure, and collude against the boss. What emerges is a story about work, authority, and the desire to escape. The film culminates in an epiphany: “Never stop dancing,” as the characters waltz and the theater, with them in it, spins off into space.

SNEAK PREVIEW!
Angela Christlieb
[SURPRISE FILM]
2026, 34 min, DCP
“This film is based on diary fragments and anecdotes created since my first stay in New York in 1997 – a personal journey into the world of imagination, interwoven from dream journals, cinematic fragments, and real memories. The film begins in the present: New York, a city in a state of political upheaval. Everything seems familiar, yet nothing is normal anymore. From here, an associative journey opens inward – into an in-between world where reality, dream, and memory overlap into a fever dream of images, voices, and thoughts. A time loop leads back to 1997. In a first-person narrative, we encounter a woman living in a Russian house in East Brooklyn – surrounded by Latino gangs, salsa music, and a jealous Russian man. As she tries to write a screenplay, heat, noise, and unrest keep her awake. At night, she zaps through TV channels and becomes fixated on programs about paranormal phenomena. With each passing night, objects and furniture begin to take on a life of their own – the boundary between dream and reality dissolves.” –Angela Christlieb

PERMUTATIONS

CINEMATIC CORRESPONDENCES: THERESA HAK KYUNG CHA AND HER FILMIC INFLUENCES
November 19
This special screening is presented in conjunction with “Theresa Hak Kyung Cha: Multiple Offerings”, a retrospective on view at Artists Space through November 21. The program features cinematic works by artists and filmmakers represented in “Apparatus, Cinematographic Apparatus: Selected Writing”, a seminal volume edited by Theresa Hak Kyung Cha (1951-82) and published in the 1980s by Tanam Press, an independent, artist-led publishing entity. Alongside these films will be a few examples of Cha’s work from the mid-1970s, including her notable structuralist piece PERMUTATIONS, a 1976 work composed of six shots filmed at one-second intervals that examines what the artist calls, “Film Time and Film Space.” The artist’s sister appears in a frame that echoes the format of a passport photo; Cha herself appears once. Through the repetition and permutation of these images, the sequence creates a rhythmic flicker, generating an optical sense of movement.

Cha was deeply influenced by both canonical and contemporary films from around the world, and by cinema theory, and actively explored the distinctions between the film, its author, and the spectator. Among the filmmakers whose work illuminates these concerns is Dziga Vertov, whose 1929 film MAN WITH A MOVIE CAMERA offers a radical exploration of cinematic perception and the possibilities of the medium, using dynamic editing, multiple exposures, split screens, and self-reflexive images of the camera and filmmaking process. Jean-Marie Straub and Danièle Huillet’s 1977 film EVERY REVOLUTION IS A THROW OF THE DICE brings together Stéphane Mallarmé’s poem “A Throw of the Dice Will Never Abolish Chance” with a collective recitation staged at Père-Lachaise Cemetery, beside the wall where the last defenders of the Paris Commune were executed in 1871. The film’s interweaving of literary text, historical memory, and the material conditions of its own performance resonates with Cha’s sustained interest in the relationship between language, image, and the conditions of viewing. Taken together, the program brings into view the formal, literary, and cinematic correspondences that run through Cha’s work.

Co-presented by Anthology Film Archives and Artists Space, with an introduction by a special guest. For more info about the Artists Space exhibition visit the Artists Space website.

ZORN II

AMALRIC <–> ZORN
November 13 – 25
This series focuses on the collaboration between two figures who are vitally important within their respective art forms, and close to our hearts here at Anthology: filmmaker and actor Mathieu Amalric and composer, conductor, and musician John Zorn.

For those who have been engaged with cinema and music over the past fifty years, neither of these artists require an introduction. Amalric has graced the screen since the mid-1980s, in films by everyone from Otar Iosseliani, Arnaud Desplechin, Raúl Ruiz, Alain Resnais, and Kiyoshi Kurosawa, to Julian Schnabel, David Cronenberg, and Guy Maddin; while the quietly astonishing films he has directed himself – including THE BLUE ROOM, BARBARA, and his recent masterpiece, HOLD ME TIGHT – have proven him to be not only a collaborator of these artists but their peer. John Zorn, for his part, has had a truly immeasurable impact on NYC culture, thanks above all to his surpassingly dynamic, genre-defying music, but also to his extraordinary visual art (underappreciated until a revelatory 2025 exhibition at the Drawing Center) and his transformative work as a producer, organizer, and creative catalyst.

As acclaimed as Amalric and Zorn’s work has been, their ongoing collaboration hasn’t been properly celebrated in the U.S. Upon meeting in 2008, the two formed an immediate and sustained bond, which has (so far) resulted in three separate documentaries. These films, directed by Amalric between 2010-22, are at once fond and in-depth portraits of a particular artist; utterly transfixing investigations into the process of composing, conducting, and performing; and rapturous meditations on the nature of music and collaboration (between Zorn and his various creative partners, especially – in ZORN III – the singer and conductor Barbara Hannigan).

Anthology has long been devoted to the work of both Amalric (who we hosted for a retrospective in 2015) and Zorn (who has been dedicated to Anthology throughout its existence, and who we’re proud to count as our Composer-in-Residence) – so it’s high time we organized screenings of the films they’ve made together. Alongside this enthralling and invaluable trilogy, we’ll be screening a selection of Amalric’s other music- or performance-themed films, including his experimental biopic BARBARA (2017), three additional films made with Barbara Hannigan, and MAÎTRES ANCIENS – COMÉDIE (2022), his filmic document of a theater piece adapted from a novel by the great Austrian writer Thomas Bernhard.

This series is presented with the generous support of Villa Albertine and Unifrance & LaScam.

Mathieu Amalric
ZORN I (2010-2016)
2017, 54 min, DCP
“The tense, but smiling face of maverick composer legend John Zorn listening to music might just restore your faith in human beauty. Every muscle moves in harmony with sounds and chords, it’s like seeing a soul wandering off into another realm. Mathieu Amalric has been filming this zestful musician for more than 12 years now. They first met when the actor and filmmaker narrated Zorn’s vocal album Shir Hashirim live in Paris. Those familiar with Amalric’s haptic cinema will immediately understand what drew him to Zorn and vice versa. They connect through their constant and obsessive drive for feelings and sensations, their tireless energy devoted to the art, their hunger for life. In the style of a home movie, Amalric accompanies Zorn at concerts, city walks, inside the sound studio or at practice.” –Patrick Holzapfel, VIENNALE

Mathieu Amalric
ZORN II (2016-2018)
2018, 59 min, DCP
“In the second part of his ongoing portrait of the prolific musician, Amalric shows the artist as a social man. Like a sponge, Zorn takes in whatever surrounds him: people (mostly colleagues from the New York Underground scene), dreams, technology, cinema (screening films by Maya Deren or Harry Smith during his performances), places and the emotions evoked by sounds. […] Amalric chooses a more structured approach than in the first part. Text inserts reveal details about the composer’s life and his philosophy. The camera gets more in tune with Zorn’s eruptive movements, as he goes onstage at sold-out concert halls as if he were just discovering the instruments there during an evening stroll. He plays each note as if it was the first or last, and Amalric tries to film him accordingly.” –Patrick Holzapfel, VIENNALE

Mathieu Amalric
BARBARA
2017, 98 min, DCP. In French with English subtitles. With Jeanne Balibar, Mathieu Amalric, Vincent Peirani, Aurore Clement, and Grégoire Colin.
Amalric’s seventh feature-length film as a director is both a heartfelt tribute to the legendary chanteuse Barbara (1930-97), and a vehicle for another peerlessly talented French luminary, the actress and singer Jeanne Balibar. Amalric’s films are remarkable for their intuitive freedom, and their emphasis on emotional textures and states of feeling rather than on straightforward narrative construction or easily grasped meanings. BARBARA is a case in point: eschewing anything like a coherent, birth-to-death account of the events of her life, the film instead orchestrates fragments of the singer’s life and performances into a free and quasi-abstract meditation on Barbara’s identity and cultural presence. And by casting Balibar not as Barbara per se but more self-referentially as an actress making a film-within-the-film about the singer, his film becomes an even broader investigation into the concept of biographical filmmaking, representation, and identity. An experimental biopic in the tradition of Stanley Kwan’s CENTER STAGE, Peter Watkins’s EDVARD MUNCH, and Paul Leduc’s FRIDA, it features a typically riveting, charismatic performance from Balibar.
Sat, Nov 21 at 7:45 and Mon, Nov 23 at 9:15.

ZORN III

Mathieu Amalric
ZORN III (2018-2022)
2022, 82 min, DCP. With John Zorn and Barbara Hannigan.
“Why can’t Mathieu Amalric stop filming John Zorn? It’s because consciously or unconsciously, he understands that he can only get closer to this man if he imitates his restlessness. It’s this Sisyphean tirelessness, the fact that we must imagine Zorn (whose surname translates into German as anger) as a happy man, which is at the core of his art. He makes music in order to be alive. This time, however, Zorn is not the only protagonist, as the Canadian soprano Barbara Hannigan enters the wild world of the composer. Much more straightforward than its previous iterations, the film captures Hannigan’s rehearsals for a performance of Zorn’s song cycle ‘Jumalattaret’ together with pianist Stephen Gosling. The work pushes her vocal capacities to the limit, with the sudden shifts in register, extended high notes and bird-like chirping demanding everything from her.” –Patrick Holzapfel, VIENNALE
Sun, Nov 22 at 5:30, and Wed, Nov 25 at 6:45.

Mathieu Amalric
MAÎTRES ANCIENS – COMÉDIE
2022, 80 min, DCP. In French with English subtitles.
“Like Reger – the character [Austrian novelist and playwright] Thomas Bernhard placed on a museum bench facing a painting by Tintoretto, an ‘Old Master’ if ever there was one – an actor finds himself alone and thwarted after a notorious virus forces the indefinite closure of theaters. He wanders through an empty auditorium, paces the now-useless backstage areas, and bursts out into a street haunted by masked passersby. Having persuaded a firefighter to keep watch over him and his explosives, he pours out – to the guard and the surrounding silence alike – his loathing for, among other things, Heideggerians, a lack of punctuality, people who admire rather than respect, and the State’s appropriation of children. At the intersection of cinema and theater, despair and the will to survive, 1980s Austria and 2020s France, Bernhard’s text takes on a new resonance: ‘And as for culture in this country, your stomach exists only to turn.’” –Marie Hermann, FIDMarseille

HOLD ME TIGHT

Mathieu Amalric
HOLD ME TIGHT / SERRE MOI FORT
2021, 97 min, DCP. In French with English subtitles. With Vicky Krieps, Arieh Worthalter, Anne-Sophie Bowen-Chatet, and Sacha Ardilly.
“Propelled by a fierce, tour-de-force performance by Vicky Krieps, who is fast becoming one of the most sought-after actors on the international scene, HOLD ME TIGHT is an ingenious memory piece exploring the grief of separation and the paradoxical comforts and claustrophobia of the domestic home. As Clarisse flees her family at daybreak and hits the open road, leaving her husband and two children behind, the film shuttles between time and space, becoming a mental landscape whose contours are gradually revealed like fleeting moments of reminiscence – those that ultimately make up a life. With its beguiling, puzzle-piece structure that builds to a shattering denouement, HOLD ME TIGHT powerfully keeps its audience in a state of hypnotic reverie as we share in the protagonist’s profound sense of disorientation, the sort of disembodied surrealism that uncertainty can unleash. PROGRAM 3: NATHANIEL DORSKY’S ‘ARBORETUM CYCLE’
“For the past several years California experienced an extreme drought. But this past winter good fortune brought a bountiful amount of storms and liquid refreshment. The spring that followed took on magical and celebratory qualities of energy, joy, fullness, and rebirth. In walking distance from my apartment is San Francisco’s Arboretum located in Golden Gate Park. I decided that I would make a film now on a single subject and that subject would be the light – not the objects, but the sacredness of the light itself in this splendid garden. What I did not know is that the great beauty of this magnificent spring would bring forth not one, but seven films, each one immediately following the previous. I began to photograph on the second week of February and finished the editing of the seventh film during the last days of December.
These seven films spontaneously manifested as the stages of life: early childhood, youth, maturity, old age, and death. ELOHIM was photographed in early spring, the week of the lunar new year, the very spirit of creation. ABATON was photographed a few weeks later in the full ripeness of spring, the very purity and intoxication of passion. CODA was photographed in late spring, in the aftermath of this purity, the first shades of mortality and knowledge. ODE, photographed in early summer, is a soft textured song of the fallen, the dissonant reds of death, seeds, and rebirth. SEPTEMBER is indeed, Indian summer, the halcyon swan song of earthly blessings. MONODY, shot in the fading autumnal glory is an energized declaration of the end. And EPILOGUE, photographed in early December, rests in quietude, the garden’s energy now descending into the dark, damp earth.” –Nathaniel Dorsky, 2017

LOST & FOUND: CINE(MA)S LATINOAMERICANOS RE-UNIDOS
November 18
New York City, despite its status as a world capital of cinema, regularly misses out on screenings of many key international films. Though the exhibition of Latin American cinema in the city has drastically increased over the years, a considerable number of influential movies from the region still fail to premiere locally. In 2017, Anthology Film Archives and Cinema Tropical joined forces to create an ongoing series – programmed by Matías Piñeiro and Carlos A. Gutiérrez – of monthly screenings featuring remarkable Latin American films making their local premiere. We paused the series (formerly entitled “If You Can Screen It There”) following the pandemic, but we’re delighted to revive it now, rechristened as “Lost & Found: Cine(ma)s Latinoamericanos Re-Unidos”. Far from minor works, the films included here are by some of the region’s most important filmmakers, have garnered major awards at international festivals, and provide an important window into the often-overlooked world of Latin American cinema.

Co-presented by Anthology Film Archives and Cinema Tropical. Programmed by Matías Piñeiro and Carlos A. Gutiérrez.

Karla Badillo
OCA
Mexico/Argentina, 2025, 109 min, DCP. In Spanish with English subtitles.
Winner of the Silver Hugo Award in the New Directors Competition at the Chicago International Film Festival, this atmospheric and mystical debut feature by Karla Badillo tells the story of Rafaela, a young nun from a dwindling congregation who embarks on a pilgrimage after the arrival of a new archbishop. Along the way, she encounters travelers from diverse walks of life, and her journey becomes a lyrical and often poignant meditation on faith, doubt, and resilience in a world shaped by both spiritual and material forces. Featuring a stellar ensemble cast of professional actors – including Natalia Solián (HUESERA: THE BONE WOMAN), Cecilia Suárez (THE HOUSE OF FLOWERS), and Raúl Briones (LA COCINA) – alongside non-professional performers, the film offers a quietly critical lens on devotion and institutional life, reimagining the spiritual cinema of Luis Buñuel through a contemporary, distinctly female perspective, and tracing the winding path between belief, destiny, and self-discovery.

COMING SOON:
• DAY WITH(OUT) ART 2026: SEEDS IN A DYING COLONY – FREE SCREENING!
November 30
For Day With(out) Art 2026, Visual AIDS presents seeds in a dying colony, a program of four newly commissioned videos – by Edgar Hudson, Bruna Kury & Matheus Mello, Sido Lansari, and Micaela Cyrino – that move through the afterlives of colonialism and the ongoing HIV epidemic. Across fractured histories and landscapes marked by extraction, the works trace how systems of power shape the body, memory, and experiences of care. The program responds to a moment marked by deepening neocolonialism and mass desensitization to violent, preventable death, including recent spikes in HIV cases and deaths. The title draws from the work of Frantz Fanon, whose writings on colonial violence and the racialized psyche have been taken up by some AIDS historians and theorists to understand how global health systems, formed through colonial rule, ultimately determine the order, quality, and conditions under which people receive healthcare, while also overwriting indigenous knowledge of healthy, embodied living. Rather than isolating HIV as a singular issue, seeds in a dying colony gestures toward the tension between systems of domination that appear enduring and the latent power and ancestral knowledge that remains in their undoing.

• EVERY CONTACT LEAVES A TRACE – FILMMAKER IN PERSON!
December 4 – 8
“For most of my adult life,” director Lynne Sachs writes, “I’ve collected business cards strangers have pulled from their wallets and placed in my hand. I sometimes remember the precise moment they were offered to me, other times they are a mystery.” In EVERY CONTACT LEAVES A TRACE, Sachs shuffles and sorts her hundreds of cards, chooses seven, and decides to contact their owners and film their encounters. What follows is a years-long journey to trace the imprint left by Sachs’s contact with these people – including her hairstylist, a Chinese filmmaker and festival programmer, her former therapist, and a textile artist whose designs inspire the illustrations and animations that punctuate the film. But EVERY CONTACT LEAVES A TRACE is not a “where are they now” film. Instead, it’s a sometimes playful meditation on memory, reality, and forgetting. Lynne Sachs will be here in person for selected screenings!
