Alex Margo Arden’s “Nothing Personal”

Francis Whorrall-Campbell

Alex Margo Arden, Signed Otherwise, 2026. Ink on ballet slippers, display case, plinth, 143.4 cm × 45 cm × 45 cm. Courtesy of the artist and the Nicoletta Fiorucci Foundation, London.

November 19, 2026

October 9, 2026–January 23, 2027

www.e-flux.com

Review

Nicoletta Fiorucci Foundation

The premise of Alex Margo Arden’s “Nothing Personal” is simple. Across each of the rooms in a ritzy Chelsea apartment-cum-exhibition space overlooking the Thames, Arden has reconstructed and restaged objects associated with the estate of the late singer and British pop culture icon Amy Winehouse.

With the assistance of auction catalogs, press photographs, and interviews, Arden identified and found copies of items now held by collectors of memorabilia. There is a selection of discontinued makeup from the same brands and in the same shades that Winehouse wore; a pile of jigsaw puzzles hinting at a mindfulness at odds with her public persona; decorative mirrors bearing the faces of James Dean and Marilyn Monroe; magazine covers and comic pages—each an exact replica of one seen in photographs of the singer’s home, or sold after her death at auction. The largest work, Library (all works 2026 unless otherwise stated), consists of over 400 books sourced by Arden, each in the same edition as the copy listed in the several auctions of Winehouse’s estate. A rare book might be easy to find, if expensive, but locating the 2004 Simon and Schuster printing of Joseph Heller’s Catch-22, or the British School of Motoring Theory Test Questions for both 2000/01 and 2002/03, requires dedication teetering into obsession.

Arden is a practiced detective of the charity shop and online resale platform. Her 2023 exhibition “Rock Paper Scissors” recreated the collages which the playwright couple Kenneth Halliwell and Joe Orton made in books from the Islington Public Library between 1959 and 1962. Acquiring editions of the same books borrowed and returned by Halliwell and Orton, Arden was able to restage every cut and paste to produce almost perfect facsimiles of the defaced (and “obscene”) frontispieces for which the couple spent six months in prison. Almost perfect, for these copies are not quite identical. The editions Arden used continued to circulate in libraries and second-hand bookstores for sixty more years, while Orton and Halliwell’s were frozen as evidence, an authentic and unfalsifiable proof of their criminal vandalism of public property.

Orton, the younger by seven years, achieved critical and commercial success after their release from prison; in 1967, a jealous Halliwell bludgeoned him to death in their Islington flat before committing suicide. Orton had always maintained that homophobia was the cause of their harsh sentencing in 1962. But alongside the offense of producing lewd images, they defied the serialized reproducibility of the book, producing objects that were worthless to the library. The collages shifted the books’ value from their place within an economy of exchange, to a system based on the uniqueness of their testimony.(1) This new “aura,” to use Walter Benjamin’s term, helps the artwork slip away from the commodity form by denying its possibility to be exchanged for another object with identical purpose or manufacture. At the same time, in Orton and Halliwell’s case, the aura of their collages provides evidentiary weight of their guilt, a social value based in the object’s authentic record of the maker’s hand. While Arden’s collages return Orton and Halliwell’s work to a kind of seriality, her attempt to copy paradoxically emphasizes the uniqueness of the original; the aesthetic, psychological, and economic value of her works is parasitically drawn from the scandalous aura of the first.

With “Nothing Personal,” Arden’s scope here is more focused on the interaction between notoriety and property. Two works in the show recreate objects that were never owned by Winehouse, but nevertheless garnered huge interest in two auctions of her possessions. Sent Otherwise consists of a pair of ballet slippers inscribed in ballpoint pen with messages such as “Love you Amy, sleep tight” and “Goodnight Camden’s sweetheart.” The original pair were left outside Winehouse’s Camden home by grieving fans, and were sold with the main cache of her possessions by California auction house Julien’s in 2022. Signed Otherwise reproduces a birthday card written by Mark Ronson and Adele for Winehouse’s twenty-fifth birthday party. Winehouse never showed, and so her stylist and friend Naomi Parry took the card on her behalf. When Parry sold this card along with other possessions given to her and another friend, Catriona Gourlay, Winehouse’s father sued, claiming the pair had not been the rightful owners of these objects. These last works are placed in the foundation’s library, where the empty shelves have been filled with acrylic ice cubes resembling the mounted numbers sometimes used in museum displays. These cubes are the only objects that have no referent in Winehouse’s estate, but are purely Arden’s gesture; a final absurd visual joke that underpins both the critique and emotion of the whole show. The literalness of Arden’s reproductions is comic in their heavy-handed commitment to the pursuit of meaningless objects. Yet arranged as stand-ins for relics of a celebrity who had so little privacy—in life and in death—the bit becomes tragic, the labor melancholic.

The title “Nothing Personal” is itself a pun. No object in the show was the personal property of Winehouse (rather, other versions of the same mass-produced goods), but their connection to her is facilitated by the depersonalization of things that were hers through auction sales and paparazzi photographs. Arden is captivated by the ghoulish market for celebrity memorabilia, even as she acts against the central logic that produces these objects’ value. Her simulacra enter a different value system in becoming artworks, yet they also refuse to register the aura of Arden’s own hand. Enticing because they reproduce Winehouse’s possessions, these objects are valuable precisely because they are not Winehouse’s possessions, but artworks that can be traded in association with Arden’s brand (“Nothing Personal” was the result of the Foundation’s inaugural prize in partnership with Frieze art fair). In removing any material connection to the singer, Arden produces a portrait of Winehouse which turns the public’s obsession with celebrity into a strange afterimage, present only in its absence. And also a portrait of the market for a different kind of ghost—the aura of the artwork.

(1) Orton and Halliwell’s collages are carefully preserved at the Islington Local History Centre. Presumably many other books from the library that the pair did not deface have been disposed of or deaccessioned.

Francis Whorrall-Campbell is an artist and writer based in the UK.
