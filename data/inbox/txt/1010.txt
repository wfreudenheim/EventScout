Mike De Leon’s Filipino folk horror film is a disturbing entry into the religious horror canon

ITIM

BY ALEXANDRA COBURN
NOVEMBER 1, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

With the theatrical rerelease of Ken Russell’s The Devils (1971), Catholic horror imagery has become unavoidable for anyone who spends a lot of time talking or reading about film. The ornate and extreme qualities of Catholicism already provide a wealth of images to draw from for directors seeking to evoke the often horrific history of the Church. After all, the crucifix is Catholicism’s most indelible icon, and it often depicts, in gruesome detail, the brutalized body of Jesus Christ. Mike De Leon’s folk horror film Itim (1976), made in the midst of the martial law imposed by dictator Ferdinand Marcos in the Philippines, is as disturbing an entry into the religious horror canon as The Devils, if a more opaque film. Rather than heighten the already macabre images associated with the Church, De Leon uses the more commonplace visual language of Filipino Catholicism to grapple with the hazards of unwavering belief.

The film opens with Jun, a young Manila-based photographer, returning to his hometown of San Ildefonso during Holy Week to spend time with his ailing father. Naturally, he becomes enamored of a devout young woman Teresa who appears to be possessed by the spirit of her missing sister, a Catholic nun. But the film’s structure is dreamlike and baroque, more invested in faithfully portraying the specificities of Filipino Catholic rites than in elucidating the plot. De Leon lingers on shots of apostle statues and sequences of the town’s women chanting the Pasyon; images of the Virgin are also ubiquitous in the characters’ homes, whether they are present at the Lenten services or not. In that way, De Leon’s film feels like a documentary, and Jun is an effective vehicle for this approach. Like the audience, he is more divorced from the rituals, and so they do feel genuinely magical and otherworldly when they unfold onscreen.

One of the most disturbing scenes of the film, in which Jun stumbles across a group of men reenacting Jesus’ walk to Golgotha while carrying a literal cross and self-flagellating, has nothing to do with ghosts at all. In another particularly chilling scene, Jun wanders through his home while his father moans in his sleep. De Leon’s use of the labyrinthine structure of the house harkens back to classic Gothic films like Rebecca (1940) and The Haunting (1963)—it’s easy to spot figures in the dark that aren’t actually there.

Jun seems to want to render concrete the uneasy energy in San Ildefonso; to find a ghost would be to force a climax, but that resolution never comes. Instead, the viewer is left with the creeping sensation that what’s happening under the surface—to Jun and his father, to Teresa, and to the town at large—will probably never break through. And, the question of Teresa’s possession is never really answered, but does it matter? The film demonstrates that whether one is acting allegorically or literally, the pain is real. What was done to Teresa’s sister cannot be karmically rebalanced via Teresa’s suffering. Attempts to recreate history to remember it more vividly are really just a way to play those events out again in earnest.

Itim screens tomorrow evening, November 2, at BAM as part of the series “The Spirit Compels Her: Possession and the Demonization of Women.”
