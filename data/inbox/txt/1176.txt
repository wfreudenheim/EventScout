SLITHER

BY JUSTIN LALIBERTY
NOVEMBER 14, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

In between The Godfather (1972) and The Gambler (1974), James Caan starred in a decidedly different, and funnier, crime film. Howard Zieff, who gained notoriety in the ‘90s for making legions of adolescents sob with My Girl (1991) made his directorial debut with the type of shaggy crime comedy that became commonplace in the ‘70s and influenced filmmakers for decades to follow. Slither isn’t a laugh riot, nor does it unflinchingly display violence. It's a leisurely paced, talky road movie of sorts that’s as much a hang out movie as it is a feature-length chase sequence.

Slither is hardly reliant on plot beats. Ultimately, it’s a vibes-forward movie about an ex-con (Caan) and the various personalities he meets on the road after getting parole. Naturally, he hears about some money that has been stashed away. Before he can collect the cash, he ends up crossing paths with a criminal bandleader (Peter Boyle) and a gun-toting drifter (Sally Kellerman). If this sounds like a Coen Brothers movie, it’s because it might as well be the urtext for one. Zieff’s film, from a script by future Big Trouble in Little China (1986) scribe W.D. Richter, is precisely the type of zany character comedy with light crime elements that the Coens, and plenty of imitators, would popularize in the ‘80s and ‘90s. Slither might not be as fondly remembered as, say, The Big Lebowski (1998), but I wouldn’t be surprised if that film’s creators had seen Zieff’s film ahead of writing theirs.

Though it has a lot in common with other crime films of its era, the film that Slither most resembles isn’t really a crime film at all: Jerry Schatzberg’s Scarecrow (1973), a similarly shaggy, distinctly American portrait of disaffected loners coming together for some sort of dream. Neither film is critical of its characters] errors—past or present. Both films are refreshingly patient, not just in terms of pacing, but in giving the people on screen a grace that most films refuse to grant their characters. Caan’s ex-con in Slither isn’t necessarily trying to go back to prison, but he’s not avoiding it either. He, like every other character in the film, is a searcher. If he’s not looking for money, he’s looking for love, or an approximation of it. What sets Zieff’s film apart from so many of its contemporaries, and even the films that followed it, is that we never really find out if he—or anyone else—finds what they’re looking for. And that’s for the best.

Slither screens this evening, November 14, at Metrograph on 35mm as part of the series “Gelgud & Hamrah present No Concessions!” Cartoonist Nathan Gelgud and writer A.S. Hamrah will be in attendance for an introduction and book signing.
