Dear readers, 

The historian Johann Chapoutot, an expert on Nazi Germany, has emerged as an unlikely celebrity in recent years due to his ability to draw similarities between the fall of the Weimar Republic and the state of contemporary France. “There is no more chapoutesque a line than to point out that a similar convergence of the extreme center and the far right, at the behest of a radicalized bourgeoisie, is at play in France today,” Simon Leser writes in a new piece for our website. 

The comparison may seem extreme, but nearly a decade of Macronist minoritarian rule has dramatically worsened inequality and deepened public debt. The protests that have erupted across the country this fall began as the electorate’s reaction to deteriorating conditions of secondary education but “morphed, under clouds of tear gas, into a general movement against police violence and regressive social policy.” The response, as Leser outlines, has been brutal: “weaker than ever, afraid of national and international embarrassment, and unable or unwilling to face the students’ demands—however legitimate—the government has again paired a strategy of repression with one of discredit.”  

In the midst of this crisis, France’s left party, La France Insoumise (LFI), led by Jean-Luc Mélenchon, offers an “increasingly plausible challenge”: 

> The government’s response against the student movement has put LFI in a new and unexpected position. The left opposition is in effect, now, the only major political party backing a movement whose demands are supported by a serious majority of the population. Mélenchon is the sole candidate who stands with the unions against the authoritarian state.

“Right On for the Darkness,” Leser's full account of the political crisis in France, is available to read now. And for a longer view on LFI, you can also listen to The Dig's interview with Jean-Luc Mélenchon—recorded last spring in the n+1 office. 

Yours, 
The Editors
