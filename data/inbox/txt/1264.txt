BIRDS, ORPHANS AND FOOLS

By SARAH FENSOM
NOVEMBER 23, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

In 1919, Milan Rastislav Štefánik, one of the major figures in the founding of Czechoslovakia and an outspoken opponent of Bolshevism, died in a plane crash. The aircraft, it was long rumored, was mistakenly shot down by the Czechoslovak army. Yorick (Jiří Sýkora), the protagonist of Juraj Jakubisko’s tireless, anti-authoritarian Birds, Orphans and Fools (1969), claims that Štefánik is his father. A statue of the Slovak politician looms in the ramshackle Bratislava apartment Yorick shares with a flock of birds and his photographer roommate Andrzej (Philippe Avron). Štefánik’s austere, monumental tomb also sits on Bradlo Hill, which the characters visit a number of times throughout the film (“your wings broke and you crashed,” says a speaker at a memorial event there). Made in the immediate wake of the 1968 Warsaw Pact invasion that quelled the Prague Spring reforms, Birds, Orphans and Fools is a richly symbolic and formally radical carnival of a film in which political turmoil and hopelessness masquerade as an exuberant, unmistakably ’60s bohemian brand of toxic positivity. Behind a shield of make believe, folly, and romance, Jakubisko slowly loads his weapon, revealing a scathing and ultimately violent work about Soviet authoritarianism shooting political change out of the sky.

In the film, Yorick is the central figure of a devil’s threesome with Polish virgin Andrzej and Martha (Magda Vášáryová), a young Jewish girl with a buzzcut, whom he wakes up next to after attending a gay party in a sailor suit. After a tense beginning to their arrangement, the three establish a routine of impish carousing that finds them drinking, chasing each other, and tinkering with instruments; bathing in fizzy wine and drawing on their bodies; donning and doffing military, clerical, and traditional folkloric garb; inhabiting and destroying antique cabinets and furnishings; and entertaining their elderly landlord (Míla Beran) and children from the orphanage next door. They bond over the fact that they too are orphans, their families all casualties of clashing political forces. Though the parentage Yorick claims is ultimately false, his name (that of Hamlet’s jester) rings true in both his philosophy and fate. He tells his partners that freedom can only be achieved by being “a fool,” and it's through this foolishness that the trio creates a seemingly safe form of chaos within the chaos of the fictional, war-torn Slovakia Jakubisko portrays them in. But after Yorick serves a one-year sentence in prison, he returns to find Andrzej and Martha settled in their own, more permanent home, expecting a child. Having sobered up in jail and lost his “courage to play the fool,” the jealous Yorick goes on a murderous spree that finds Martha dead and himself in the Danube, the statue of Štefánik used as a weight to plunge him deep into the icy waters.

Jakubisko was awarded the Little Lion for young artists at the 1968 Venice Film Festival for his second feature, The Deserter and the Nomads, and dubbed “the Slovak Fellini.” His work during this period recalls the Italian master’s sweeping phantasmagoria, the acerbic political commentary and antagonistic giddiness of New Wave counterparts like Jean-Luc Godard and Věra Chytilová, and forecasts the nothing-is-sacred nihilism of filmmakers like Želimir Žilnik and Radu Jude. But after showing once, Birds, Orphans and Fools was banned by the communist authorities due to its critical messaging and representation of figures like Štefánik, whose image had been heavily suppressed during the regime. Jakubisko’s next film was seized before it was even completed, unreleased like Birds until the Velvet Revolution in 1989. Before the regime’s end, Jakubisko was allowed to work, but not in the same way. His wings, too, were clipped.

Birds, Orphans and Fools screens this evening, November 23, at Czech Center New York. Actress Magda Vášáryová will be in attendance for a post-screening conversation.
