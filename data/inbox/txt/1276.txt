A comprehensive history of video art in Europe has yet to be written. While exhibitions and publications have long focused on a handful of pioneers, primarily from North America, the beginnings of video art in Europe as a whole have received little attention to date.

Hidden on Tape. Early Video Art in Europe at the ZKM | Karlsruhe offers a new perspective on this history: with more than 120 works from some 30 countries, the exhibition traces the emergence of artistic practices in television and video from the 1950s through the 1990s. It reveals the multiple beginnings of an art form that developed in a politically divided Europe under vastly different cultural, social, and economic conditions. The exhibition brings together internationally renowned artists with those whose contributions have so far gone largely unnoticed. Some of their works remained unseen for decades, hidden away in the archives of museums and broadcasting institutions, or in artists’ studios. They were tracked down through a multi-year research project led by ECAL/École cantonale d’art de Lausanne (HES-SO) and Université des créations - Paris 8 in collaboration with the ZKM | Karlsruhe and an international network of scholars. Several of the rediscovered tapes were restored by the ZKM’s Laboratory for Antiquated Video Systems, making them accessible once again.

The emergence of video art in Europe did not follow a uniform trajectory. Even before portable video equipment became available in the late 1960s, artists were experimenting in television studios or altering broadcast images by manipulating television sets. Access to studios, cameras and video recorders, and production funds, however, varied considerably from country to country, shaped by political systems, cultural institutions, and economic conditions.

Yet artists did not work in isolation. Networks of individuals and organizations gradually formed, enabling ideas, texts, and tapes to circulate through festivals, exhibitions, publications, and informal channels. These exchanges crossed national and ideological borders. Hidden on Tape brings to light the richness and diversity of early video art across Europe, revealing unexpected connections and shared artistic concerns.

The exhibition builds on the research project The Emergence of Video Art in Europe, 1960–1980, led by François Bovier (ECAL/École cantonale d’art de Lausanne and Université de Lausanne) and Grégoire Quenault (Université des créations - Paris 8) in close collaboration with Stéphanie Serra and an academic advisory committee (Slavko Kacunko, Laura Leuzzi, Chris Meigh-Andrews, Miklós Peternák, Lorella Scacco, and Tomasz Załuski). Its findings will be published in Early Video Art in Europe. A Plural History (Les presses du réel, 2026). A separate publication accompanying the exhibition is in preparation.

Hidden on Tape returns long-overlooked works to view, opening up new ways of writing the history of video art in Europe and raising the question of what stories remain to be discovered.
