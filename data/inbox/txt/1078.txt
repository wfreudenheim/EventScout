EINSTÜRZENDE NEUBAUTEN: LIEBESLIEDER

BY CAT BECKSTRAND
NOVEMBER 6, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Klaus Maeck and Johanna Schenkel’s documentary Einstürzende Neubauten: Liebeslieder (1993) is a fractured, immersive portrait of the pioneering industrial group. It assembles concert footage, site-specific performances, gallery installations, music videos, and interviews into an assemblage of Einstürzende Neubauten’s first decade of output. Instead of tracing a tidy chronology, it captures the philosophies that anchor the band, who approach music as performance art, sculpture, and spatial intervention.

Einstürzende Neubauten’s most striking qualities are discussed in depth throughout the film. They are fascinated by the resonant properties of space and developing performances for unique environments. They haul recording gear along the Kurfürstendamm, into supermarkets and elevators, taking the documentary crew into a water tower where they had previously recorded in near-total darkness only using a handheld flashlight to light the space. Long time band member Alexander Hacke demonstrates the acoustics of the tower’s bell, generating a sound that is simultaneously deep, delicate, and terrifying through a simple gesture. Their instruments are equally transgressive: power tools, steel springs, concrete bricks, fire, and countless other pieces of machinery are used as sound sources. Captured in archival footage, a television music journalist describes the band as “rock music devolving into pure noise.”

The group’s commitment to site-specific performances runs throughout their career. Outside of the usual DIY suspects—abandoned buildings, construction sites, bridges—they played in more unique locations, such as caves and the Mojave Desert, where the band lost some of their equipment to the sand. In 1986, the Pedagogical Institute of Nurnberg invited the group to play at the Zeppelinfeld Tribune, the site of hundreds of Nazi rallies still within living memory. When asked if he’s trying to “exorcize” the grounds, frontman Blixa Bargeld is evasive, refusing to play into the same faux-mysticism that had plagued the space four decades earlier. His intention was not to desecrate the grandstand: in Bargeld’s mind, the right to exorcise belonged to the people who used it with its original purpose, not his generation. Einstürzende Neubauten treats the environment as an active participant in their work, allowing its resonance and history to shape each performance.

Unsurprisingly, the band takes an intentional, methodical approach to achieve each performance’s on-stage destruction. Members of the group discuss the logistics of touring, partnering with local theaters and fabricators to construct new objects in each city. Footage of their 1990 interactive exhibition “Ten Years of Einstürzende Neubauten” at Artspace in San Francisco shows patrons whacking away at the band’s handmade instruments in the gallery, dissolving the boundary between performer and audience. Einstürzende Neubauten dismantles our preconceived notions of how music is made, intentionally deconstructing traditional instrumentation to put something new in its place.

Einstürzende Neubauten: Liebeslieder screens tonight, November 6, and November 11 at Anthology Film Archives as part of the series “German Punk.” Tonight's screening will be introduced by musician and former Einstürzende Neubauten member Gudrun Gut.
