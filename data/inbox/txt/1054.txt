THE QUESTIONS LITIGATORS AND ENFORCERS SHOULD BE ASKING TECH COMPANIES

As tech companies rapidly develop and deploy generative AI products, litigators and enforcers seek to understand how these companies make decisions about the safety of their products. Yet litigators aren’t necessarily equipped with technical knowledge about how these products are built, how safety evaluations are conducted, and where responsibility lies across the companies involved. In order to pursue accountability for harms, they need to know what kinds of questions to ask. To meet this need, this summer our Public Technology Leadership Collaborative (PTLC) and Tech Justice Law (TJL) co-organized a three-part series of workshops aimed at training enforcers and litigators who are building cases around generative AI products. Experts helped participants understand the broad landscape of the generative AI tech stack, unpacking how AI safety decisions are implemented and distributed at various points during AI product development, and the kinds of questions litigators and enforcers should be asking of tech companies. On Points, PTLC Project Director Becca Ricks and TJL litigation Counsel Tiffany Gillis Brown lay out the key takeaways that left participants with a stronger technical grounding in the issues and a clearer understanding of where AI safety decisions are being made.

OUR STATEMENT ON THE MIDTERMS: A REFERENDUM ON THE TECH INDUSTRY

The results of yesterday’s midterm elections stand as an affirmation of American democracy, and a repudiation of corruption, cruelty, and nativism. They also represent a forceful rejection of the tech industry’s agenda. Through their ballots, Americans spoke out against the industry’s vision of an unstoppable AI-dominated future. As they have done in their communities with increasing urgency over the past year, people made clear their objection to unchecked data center development and oligarchic power. The tech industry wants Americans to believe that AI expansion is an inevitability, something the public must simply accept and adapt to. Voters showed they don’t buy that story.

Voters also showed that their consent, and the future of their communities, is not for sale. Tech billionaires plowed record amounts of money into races across the country, thinking they could buy a favorable environment for their priorities. Americans rejected that. Instead, voters across the political spectrum expressed strong preferences: against the influence of obscene private money in public matters, against data center build-outs, for an affordable economy, and for a different, and more accountable politics.

Technology can be a positive force in society, but it will take strong, democratic governance to guard against new technologies like AI being further used to concentrate power, discipline workers, accelerate surveillance, or raise prices. To that end, the American people have sent a clear message to the next Congress: regulate AI, hold tech companies accountable, and deliver the kind of material improvements that impact everyday life. It’s time for policymakers to answer the call.

Plus: In Truthout, Austin C. McCoy explains why it’s essential that the fight against big tech’s AI takeover continue well beyond the midterms. In considering the parameters of that fight, he delves into our recent report The AI Factory, which explores Pennsylvanians’ opposition to the tech industry’s narrative of technological determinism and unpacks a range of tactics for local and state-based movements.

And: Techcrunch and Fortune cover a related panel featuring Maia Woluchem, director of our Trustworthy Infrastructures program and a coauthor of the report, where she situated data centers in “a long legacy of industrial obfuscation.” Also speaking to Fortune, report coauthor Ana Carolina de Assis Nunes reflects on why opposition to data centers in Brazil isn’t as politically united as it is in the US. Foreign investment still carries prestige in parts of Brazil, she explains, especially for people who see it as a chance to join the global economy: “For people who are so marginalized and pushed away from global systems, it’s just like ‘oh, Google is coming here, that’s great.’” (If you’re in the area on November 12, de Assis Nunes will be discussing the report in an event at Arizona State University.)

More people need the means and capacity to situate, question, and shape how AI is used in their daily lives. AI civics starts by recognizing the technology not as a skill to be mastered but as a domain of public life — one where people have rights, responsibilities, and avenues for collective action. This Friday, November 6 at 6:00 p.m. ET, join us online or onsite at the Stavros Niarchos Foundation Library (SNFL) in New York City to discuss how AI civics can help us meet this moment and realize a more democratic vision of public interest technology. Together, we’ll explore how people are contesting unchecked AI acceleration; how they are challenging the ways AI systems are being pushed into their schools, workplaces, and communities; and how communities can come together to build on these efforts — at a moment when policymakers are starting to listen. “These victories for critics of AI and data centers (and crypto too) are part of a broader shift against Silicon Valley over the past half decade. There’s a global techlash under way: Antitrust investigations in the US, strict privacy regulations in Europe, social-media lawsuits and bans around the world. Still, for the most part, Silicon Valley has been able to use its wealth and influence to beat back any substantive opponents or regulations in the US. This week, that playbook failed.” [“Americans Really Don’t Like Data Centers,” by Matteo Wong, The Atlantic]
