FUCKTOYS

BY LAUREN LEVITT
OCTOBER 3, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Annapurna’s Sriram’s Fucktoys (2025) is about Jesus as a queer, transmasc whore in a godless world. The film opens with AP (played by the director), Jill of all sex trades, having a reading with Tarot Woman (Big Freeda). One of the cards that she pulls is the Hanged Man, immediately evoking Christ. Tarot Woman tells AP that she has a curse, a warning that is repeated three times throughout the film, and that needs to make a sacrifice to lift it. Tarot Woman offers to sacrifice a baby lamb to lift the curse for $1000, so AP sets off on her moped through Trashtown (a thinly veiled New Orleans) to raise the necessary funds.

Fucktoys is high camp, but it explores serious themes. Trashtown is a violent place, and much of this violence centers around AP’s gender ambiguous romantic friend Danni. When we first meet Danni, he is involved in a group fist fight. Later, he gets in a fight with an escorting client when the client demands that he sign a non-disclosure agreement. Furthermore, he tells AP that, when he was in prison, he shared a cell with “the Mechanic,” who killed and mutilated sex workers. This violence against sex workers is something AP herself will encounter by the end of the film.

There is no god in Trashtown, but there is care. AP cares for her friends, and they care for her. AP gives one friend $20 she earns from a domination session with a client, and another friend gives AP the number of an escorting client after she is fired from a go-go dancing gig at an orgy. AP and Danni care for each other throughout the film, and they are cared for by two parental figures. AP’s mother Luna offers Danni and AP sustenance when they visit her at her donut shop, and she comforts AP in the film’s final moments. AP’s escorting client Robert tells her that he feels like a father figure to her after she asks him to pick her up when her moped is stolen, and he later tenderly bandages Danni’s face. Yet, this care can only assuage but not prevent the violence the characters must navigate.

Fucktoys screens this evening, October 3, at Anthology Film Archives as part of the series “Avatars of the Whore.”

LIFTING THE CURSE: A CONVERSATION WITH ANNAPURNA SRIRAM

BY AYANNA DOZIER
OCTOBER 3, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Raunchy and uproariously funny, Fucktoys (2025) is actor Annapurna Sriram’s feature directorial debut. The film, also written by and starring Sriram, wraps its much-acclaimed festival and select-theater run this year. For an undistributed film, Fucktoys has achieved a fanbase that is nearly unheard of and has seen its screenings sell out in minutes, with attendees driving four-plus hours to catch a screening (as I witnessed when I programed the film for the 33rd Massachusetts Multicultural film festival earlier this year). In many ways, the film has already received instant “cult” status from its admirers and its reception mirrors the near instantaneous fan response of Todd Haynes’s Superstar: The Karen Carpenter Story (1987), which was also achieved during its festival run.

And like Superstar, censorship has followed Fucktoys, though not for any copyright issues but rather for its title and the film’s frank examination of a woman of color working sex across various industries that include stripping, escorting, and S/M scene play. Sriram’s character is set on this journey by a psychic (played by Big Freedia) who informs her she needs to earn $1,000 to lift the curse plaguing her life. In what has become a passing joke for Sriram since the film’s debut, the curse in the film is analogous to the patriarchal structural barriers that affected the film from its production to its release, including the digital censorship of the film’s marketing as evident in Meta briefly deleting their Instagram account for promoting “inappropriate content,” citing the film’s content on “sex work.”

Working sex labor in Fucktoys becomes a metaphor, then, for the never-ending hustle that some women of color perform to make a living and to navigate the barriers (or curses) shaped by what bell hooks writes as white supremacist imperial patriarchal capitalism. Fucktoys is a genre-blending, earnest, sex-positive, and humorous attempt to break that curse for its protagonist and its director. The film is clear that you cannot out-hustle the system, but you can find ways to fight back with the help of your community.

Ayanna Dozier: Can you walk us through what the road to making Fucktoys was like and what your inspirations were in determining its genre-blurring tone? 

Annapurna Sriram: I wrote the script almost 10 years ago. The landscape, just for context, was before the contemporary interest [in working sex on film], so pre-Euphoria [2019-2026], Zola [2020], Pleasure [2021], Anora [2024], et cetera. At the time, I was really thinking more about what I wanted to do as an actor and what was missing for me in the roles that were being offered. I wanted this opportunity to express this whorey but sentimental, funny and sexy woman. And I was like, no one's going to cast me like that. Because in this industry, you're waiting for a white man to see you as a muse.

I come from a theater school background. I wanted it to have the realism and the grounded performance of a Stephen Adly Guirgis play where there’s these larger-than-life characters, but they’re in these intimate situations. But then I also wanted the aesthetics of the film to reflect films like Pee-Wee's Big Adventure [1985], After Hours [1985], and Liquid Sky [1982]. I wanted Fucktoys to exist in a highly imaginative world. So, what can I create that can justify these aesthetics, but then also how can I create these scenes for these grounded interactions to [exist within that imaginative world]?

It took me two years from when I wrote to when I found my producer and François [Arnaud] [who plays The Mechanic] in 2019. We were trying to find a director, with the $100,000 we had raised and were going to make it as an ultra-low-budget movie for under $250,000. My producer, François, and I had this meeting and they told me, “Look, the movie is in your head. I don't know if we're going to find a director who will understand the tone, style, aesthetic, and the scenes.” We were looking for, basically, a femme woman of color director who’s also going to understand this [script], the camp aesthetics and the genre blend.

I was like, “I'm not a director!” I had a lot of imposter syndrome around stepping into that role where you are running the ship. I think that was my own internalized misogyny. Women are not championed to be in those roles. We're seen as the muse, we're seen as the actress, the starlet.

AD: Right. It is difficult to both possess the vision of what you want as a performer and to have to be the one to invent that role for you to do, let alone direct it. I can only imagine the difficulties that directing brought to juggling the various hats you wore on set. What was the production like? 

AS: Anytime something went wrong on set, it felt like the curse [in the film] was actually tormenting me in real life. Hurricane Ida had just hit New Orleans, the Omicron variant of the Covid-19 virus was spiking, and it was during Mardi Gras … Making an indie film comes with its own set of difficulties. It's going to be hard. Shit is going to happen. The trucks are going to break down, someone's going to have to drop because they’re going to take another job. It’s like playing a game with the difficulty set to the highest level.

The other piece is that this was my first time directing. I've only made a short film prior, and I never made a film that went through the festival circuit; I wasn't a creature of the industry, you know? So, one of the hardest things for me was getting people to trust me. Being a female director and also having a very specific vision means that I'm holding everyone to a very high standard on my set. And I think that's really hard for some people to have to listen to a woman who also is untested.

We filmed it over the course of a year in 2022. We filmed in multiple units, which I’m very impatient. I’m an Aries. I like things fast, but [now] I'm actually very grateful looking back that we had that time because I could watch footage, and I could build in what the pickups needed to be. I could take my time, like, really making sure I had everything in hand that I absolutely needed for the film to be cut. And, the movie that exists is the film as written. There's only like one mini scene that is not in the movie, but everything else is as scripted.

I’m also lucky that my actors were so good, because I felt a lot of relief either watching them in scenes or acting in scenes with them. Their talent meant that I had to do so little to get up to get the performances that I got in the film. I think that being an actor, I know what actors want. They want freedom. They want to feel like their part, even if it's small, is going to make an impact and each of the performances do that in the film.
