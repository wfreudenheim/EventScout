THE LITTLE MERMAID

By BERNARDO RONDEAU
NOVEMBER 22, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

A landlocked nation under a revitalized authoritarian regime, Czechoslovakia surprisingly green-lit an adaptation of Hans Christian Andersen’s aquatic fairy tale in celebration of the author’s jubilee. Folkloric works, like historical dramatizations, were usually considered safe by totalitarian regimes at the time in part because such films eschewed depictions of present-day reality and because the supposed familiarity of such stories meant the entire populace could enjoy them equally.

What’s even more peculiar about The Little Mermaid (1976) is who helmed this cinematic fantasy: Karel Kachyňa. By the time of the film’s production, nearly a decade into “normalization” brought on by the Warsaw Pact’s Operation Danube, which effectively shut down the Prague Spring in 1968, Kachyňa’s standing was shaky. He had lost his post at the Prague Film Academy (FAMU) after a short by one of his students was seen as a critical allegory of the Soviet army’s recent invasion—it didn’t matter that the film’s script about mysterious intruders arriving unannounced at a tower block dated to 1967. Kachyňa also had to renounce his collaborator of 12 features, the newly blacklisted screenwriter Jan Procházka, who would subsequently pass away in 1971 at age 42. But despite this bleak context, Kachyňa created a work of surreal wonder.

Combining Black Sea vistas with the craggy sandstone façades of Prachov Rocks, Kachyňa and cinematographer Jaroslav Kučera create a dreamlike vision of a world under the waves, emphasizing verticality at the sea bottom. Costume designer Šárka Hejnová garbs the mer-people in pastel tunics ranging from blue to green, but it’s their nest-like headpieces that may be her greatest contribution. Knotty tangles of colorful hair, roots and seaweed, these organic crowns defy gravity and evoke both the buoyancy of hair in water and regal authority. 

Nearly 50 minutes unspool almost entirely in the submersed kingdom of the Little Mermaid (Miroslava Šafránková), her father the King of the Seas (Radovan Lukavský), and their court. Filming in subtle slow motion, Kachyňa and Kučera render the movements of these characters floaty and wraith-like, accompanied by composer Zdeněk Liška’s mesmerizing electroacoustic symphonic score. Decorated with detritus from the world above like swords, sculptures and pottery, this languid utopia is part prehistoric and part museum. The Little Mermaid unsurprisingly eschews acidic allegories in favor of the sumptuously lyrical. That said, it’s hard not to notice that the film’s narrative is ultimately about the coexistence of two discretely different worlds. While the earth world is violent and vulgar, the ocean world is refined and peaceful. And in order for the Little Mermaid to walk among humans not only does she lose her singing voice, but she also experiences piercing pain in her new feet. Such is the great price of crossing over to the other side.

The Little Mermaid screens this afternoon, November 22, at the Museum of Modern Art as part of the series “Shadows of the Spring: The Normalization Era in Czechoslovakia.”
