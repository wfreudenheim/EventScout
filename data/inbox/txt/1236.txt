Dear readers,

Around the time Leigh Claire La Berge’s mother began exhibiting signs of dementia, she bequeathed La Berge a collection of her journals. A historian of medicine and intellectual disciple of Michel Foucault, La Berge’s mother had maintained a decades-long private archive of her domestic and romantic life, her scholarship as a historian of medicine, and her enchantment with the life and work of fellow social historian Michel Foucault—whose transgressive presence, La Berge remembers, loomed large throughout her own adolescence: 

> My first memory of him was an innocuous interaction, a chance encounter that passed me by, but which has since assumed an outsize proportion in my own intellectual genealogy. By age 13, I had stopped growing and was destined, it seemed, to have feet larger than average for my height. “But why average something?” Mom casually asked while I was trying on new tennis shoes one summer afternoon in a Foot Locker. “Average height? Average weight? Average foot size? What kind of knowledge is that?”
>
> “To know what’s in the middle,” I replied. In retrospect, I can see how conceptually adrift I was, how poorly seventh grade had prepared me for poststructuralism. “But why is it meaningful to know what’s ‘in the middle’?” Mom returned. “There was no ‘middle’ in the 18th century,” she concluded, as I selected my new shoes. She didn’t use Foucault’s name, but the inquiry had all the markings of the man who had become her teacher. Averages make sense to so many of us who are average. But what kind of style, creativity, difference, and freedom does a society forfeit in an era of averages?

In a new essay, La Berge returns to her mother’s journals in the present, finding in them a surprising record of freedom, difference, and near-Foucauldian transgression. “Unlike Foucault, I’m not a student of discourse, and unlike my mother, I’m not a student of history,” writes La Berge. “But one way to remain with my mother is to become a historian of her.” 

“Michel and Mom” is written with the same generosity, intelligence, and amiable bathos that n+1 readers might remember from La Berge’s essay in Issue 48. (“You raise your child awash in Francophilia, initiate her into the Kingdom of Michel, and then suddenly, she turns on you,” La Berge writes. “Foucault was a bald, café-sitting Parisian homosexual philosopher. I was a blond, café-sitting homosexual with, at least, a philosophy BA.”) It is also out from behind the paywall in Issue 54. As ever, subscribers can read it in print, alongside everything else in the issue (and, very shortly, in Issue 55). Consider a subscription or a gift subscription—and in the meantime, happy reading. 

Yours,
The Editors
