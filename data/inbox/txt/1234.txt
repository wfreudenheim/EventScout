SENTIENT EARTH

BY KATHLEEN LANGJAHR
NOVEMBER 19, 2026

READ ON OUR SITE

Billowing endlessly across the frame of Bernadette Corporation’s video installation The Earth's Tarry Dreams of Insurrection Against the Sun (2010) are the 95 thousand barrels of oil released into the Gulf of Mexico every day for three months during the BP Deepwater Horizon spill. Displayed on two monitors turned on their side and propped against a wall, the footage was streamed live from BP's “spillcam” video feed when the work was first presented at the artist collective’s 2010 solo exhibition at Galerie Neu in Berlin. In its current installation at Amant, the monitors play a looped section of the feed, displaying a flow of crude projecting continuously rightward, suggesting its enduring impact on the gulf's future ecosystem and leading the eye into the gallery at 932 Grand, one of three spaces at the contemporary art complex presenting the ambitious exhibition “Sentient Earth.” The 28 artists selected for the exhibition offer critical responses to “the planetary-scale transformations ushered in by the accelerating exploitation of the earth's resources,” culminating in a condemnation of the economic systems driving the extraction of resources on an untenable scale while emphasizing our proximity to the limit of the planet's capacity to maintain ecological equilibrium, referred to in climate science as the tipping point.

BP's spillcam stream “captures the nonstop flow of images” that “made evident the cruel and unbearable impotence of viewers who found themselves…glued to their screens, mastering the image of the horrendous leak but not being able to do anything to stop the flow,” writes the scholar T. J. Demos in his book-length manifesto, Against the Anthropocene, an analysis and critique of visual culture's engagement with our present geologic era. The moving-image works featured in “Sentient Earth” take up a similar mantle, rebuking capitalism’s expansionist mandate and the media systems designed to convert images of catastrophe into detached spectacle.

About 20' from The Earth's Tarry Dreams is a screen on which Yuyan Wang's Green Grey Black Brown (2024, pictured at top), also a found-footage video drawn from online sources, is projected. A vertiginous assemblage of clips stitched together with hypnotic, doomscroll-esque editing, Wang's video examines the mechanized overhaul of the earth's terrain on varying scales. Molten flows of rock and bubbling mud pools segue to churning landfills, and closeups of artificial plants adorning detailed landscape dioramas are interwoven with the rhythmic movements of the industrial machines that produce the botanical facsimiles. Nature, artifice, and refuse blend into an inextricable amalgam. The video's mesmerizing quality is augmented by Wang's soundtrack, a remix of Yes’s prog-rock hit “Owner of a Lonely Heart” slowed to a deep, ambient rumble. Footage featuring viscous dark liquids evocative of oil, the primary component of plastics, constitutes a visual through-line in the video, and viewers are invited to lounge atop a cushioned piece of astroturf, the fake grass developed by agrochemical giant Monsanto in 1965, offering a physical interaction with the substance linking the dregs of a prehistoric apocalypse with the ecocide of the present.

As the primary means for individuals to share information to a potentially global audience, albeit with varying degrees of censorship by the media conglomerates that control the means of dissemination, the presence of online footage in the exhibition's moving-image works is apt. Liu Yujia’s Treasure Hunt (2021) concludes with a TikTok stream recorded at a market in the Xinjiang region of China, where Han traders haggle with buyers over the price and quality of jade stones collected by Uyghur laborers in the Karakash riverbed. Jade mining is a millennia-old endeavor in China, and Karakash is a site long picked over by previous generations of prospectors. The film that precedes this denouement is a beautifully shot landscape film traversing the riverbed, the Kunlun Mountains to the south, and the Taklamakan Desert to the east. Liu’s film links the Uyghurs' undervalued quest for jade with the manual labor performed by the excavation team led by early 20th-century British archeologist Aurel Stein, who discovered the ruins of a fourth or fifth-century Buddhist stupa in the desert. A voiceover in English describes China’s long history of jade excavation and the fate of the stupa fragments discovered by Stein's team. Chinese subtitles translate the solemn British actor’s articulate narration, emphasizing the appropriative nature of Stein's claim to the sacred ruin.

Given the focus of extraction in “Sentient Earth,” there is a poetic resonance to the placement of the exhibition's most explicitly prescriptive video deep in its core. Flickering like a gem embedded in the far wall of the central gallery's back room, Cauleen Smith's Mines to Caves (2023) calls for a restoration of humanity's original conception of caves as sites of shelter and unity. “This is not Plato's cave,” Smith explains; not an illusion or simulacra. These caves are real. They are the caves of Lascaux and Chauvet, where the earliest humans inscribed scenes of communion among animals, humans, and landscape. Text overlays throughout the video list materials mined throughout human history, including several prominent within the history of art and visual culture, such as sandstone, graphite, and lapis lazuli, as well as substances vital to the circulation of images in the present, such as molybdenite, a mineral used in the liquid crystal displays of electronic devices.

The cave footage in Smith’s film was shot in Aspen’s Smuggler Mine, the oldest operating silver mine in the region, founded in 1880. Beyond their ecological significance, mines were an important site of struggle in the labor movement of the late-19th and early 20th-century United States, where hard-won rights that continue to benefit workers today were secured. In addition to minting coins and a variety of applications in medicine, industry, and the decorative arts—especially mirrors—silver was an essential material in the earliest forms of still photography and motion pictures, constituting a point of convergence in the tangled web of capital, labor, and image. Many shots in Smith's film were produced via a cameraless 35mm print process, enacting a technical resistance to this lineage. Like the robber barons of the gilded age, the tech billionaires of our information age pose a threat to all life forms within their reach, but their reach now extends past the parameters of the Earth itself, and the planet's carbon levels are far closer to a catastrophic threshold. Observing this work a few feet indoors from a balmy afternoon in the hottest October on record, Smith's bright, incantatory voiceover cast a pall of doom over the footage of wild plants and animals that traversed the screen as the Rolling Stones's iconic hook from “Gimme Shelter” played underneath. “Please just sit here in the cave with me,” she urges. “We must return this mine to the mountain.”

Sentient Earth is on view through February 14, 2027, at Amant.
