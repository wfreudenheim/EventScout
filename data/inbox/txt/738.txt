Cortex's unexpected second legacy

How many groups get a second chance? If hip-hop had never happened, then the story of French prog-jazz ensemble Cortex would be quite simple. Formed by pianist Alain Mion in the early ‘70s, Cortex was a five-piece group who released a killer debut of mellow, florid bangers, as well as a handful of very solid follow ups to modest success before dissolving at the dawn of the ‘80s. Mion continued to pursue his music over the following decades while his collaborators went their various ways. Barring any interventions, the Cortex catalog would have likely continued to live on with a small cult following of listeners who appreciate a bit of flash in their loungey fare. But the invention of the sampler kicked off a chain reaction that made the group legendary, decades after their dissolution. On October 12, they headline the Main Hall, joined by Adrian Younge and J.Rocc.

10.12 - CORTEX

It’s an eerie experience hearing the group’s debut album Troupeau Bleu in 2026. At first, the record feels like a lost gem, true, but nothing more. Bleu opens with “La Rue,” a lovely, largely-instrumental number that foregrounds plenty of jazzy solos, funky grooves and some late-game vocals from Mireille Dalbray. As soon as she gets on the mic, the bossa nova influence that permeates the record snaps into focus; Dalbray has an effortless flow that lands in a sweet spot between breathy intimacy and easy, bright projection. Her melodies show a strong Brazilian influence without being a direct copy. As with Louis OfMan in last week’s newsletter, the French sensibility - call it a je ne sais quoi - is front and center, even as the group flows between myriad influences. Mion said it simply in a recent interview: “Yes, basically to try to get pleasure from playing with good friends and to give our best to the music. A bit of groove, some funk, a touch of Latin, some good pop melodies, a good part of jazz, and some swing cookin'! That was our concept.”

The groove continues to unfurl with easy charm, and you can imagine Troupeau Bleu playing at innumerable dinner parties across Paris in the ‘70s. The influence of contemporaneous fusion gives it a distinctly elevated feeling, but unlike, say Mahavishnu Orchestra, there’s none of the raw, confrontational density that you’d find at the outer edges of the genre. Instead, Cortex lands closer to the soul-jazz of Herbie Mann or Herbie Hancock’s Headhunters. There’s no other way to say it - Cortex is smooth. 

And then it happens. Maybe when the title track comes on, if you’re a Lil B fan. Or when you hear “Prélude à 'Go Round,’” which is heavily sampled on Rick Ross’ “Amsterdam.” If you’re not responsible for any of that song’s 12 million-plus streams, you might recognize it from Tyga’s “Palm Trees” or this Madlib track. 

Cortex - Troupeau Bleu

Perhaps your hip-hop bonafides aren’t that robust. No matter. “Chanson D'Un Jour D'Hiver” - translated to “song of a winter day” - is prominently featured in Lupe Fiasco’s masterful “Mural” as well as Jeezy’s “Beautiful,” featuring The Game and, once again, Rick Ross. But it’s “Huit Octobre 1971” that takes the cake. Immediately recognizable from MF Doom’s exceptional “One Beer,” it’s also on Tyler, The Creator’s “Odd Toddlers,” Kanye’s recent “CIRCLES” and Wiz Khalifa’s “Visions.” Jaylib - the collaboration between J. Dilla and Madlib, used it on “No Games,” and Lil Yachty sampled the track on the appropriately-named “Cortex.” But we’re not even close to done - there are 51 more instances logged for the song’s entry on whosampled.com, which in total cites 220 instances of artists sampling Cortex. Of those, 203 are from Troupeau Bleu. 

What gives? Why is this album so insanely coveted by hip hop producers? There are a few reasons. First off all - the playing. As discussed, Cortex were funky, with grooves that sit perfectly in the pocket. They also cultivated an immaculate sonic profile, with a delicious blend of studio polish and live grit that makes these records come alive. They land right in between foreground and background, and while this is absolutely not ambient music, it nonetheless fulfils Brian Eno’s famous dictum to “be able to accommodate many levels of listening attention without enforcing one in particular; it must be as ignorable as it is interesting.”  It also helps that there are so many instrumental passages that make a perfect foundation for a rapper. Drop a needle anywhere in the LP’s run and you’re likely to hear a few measures that could be looped perfectly alongside a heavy boom-bap rhythm.

Also, the emotions. Over and over again, Cortex hits a note of forlorn grandeur, a bit ethereal and yet grounded. Samples of this caliber were the secret sauce for some of the ‘90s most iconic moments. Songs like Jay-Z’s “Dead Presidents,” Wu-Tang Clan’s “C.R.E.A.M.,” Nas’ “Memory Lane (Sittin’ in da Park),” Raekwon’s “Wisdom Body” and Mobb Deep’s “Shook Ones, Pt. II” each, in their own way, used deep, soulful jazz as a counterbalance to tales of violent crime and street life. Cortex hits many of these same notes, but with a twist. It would be inaccurate to call their songs sorrowful, but there’s enough pathos there to lend some gravitas. There’s also moments where the music takes full flight that are frankly stunning, both on their own merit and as repurposed samples. 
 

Cortex, 2024

It must be said - Dalbray does some heavy lifting here. Typically, producers will usually opt to avoid vocal passages, so as to leave room for the rapper. But Dalbray’s soft-yet-forceful, clear-yet-ethereal vocals sit in the mix like another instrument. And it helps that she's not singing in English. But it’s nonetheless striking how many of these productions lean on her voice, and how incredible it sounds. 

Interestingly, Cortex wouldn’t be discovered by producers en masse until the ‘00s, but you can imagine the excitement Madlib and co must have felt when they came across the group. After all, the sprawl of American jazz-fun-fusion-etc records had been well combed by that point; Cortex was something untouched.

It was inevitable that the Mion would get word of his unexpected legacy, and soon enough Cortex was back touring. This time, however, their audience is largely made up of people who came to them through hip-hop. But that doesn't mean the appreciation is reduced; in many ways, hip-hop’s digger culture is responsible for the rediscovery and canonization of a whole network of so-called “rare groove” and diamond-in-the-rough jazz. How many quality LPs were pressed, given a cursory marketing campaign and then forgotten across the ‘70s and ‘80s? How many artists poured their heart into a release only for it to just miss the moment or not capture the zeitgeist? In their moment, Cortex was one of the reasonably lucky ones, with early label support, regional recognition and occasional moments of larger exposure. But their output deserved a second look. Appreciated through a lens they could have never anticipated, they are now finding a new life half a century after their debut.
