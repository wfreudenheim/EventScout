On an October afternoon in 1965, as the founding story of video art goes, Nam June Paik bought a portable Sony video recorder, filmed Pope Paul VI’s motorcade from a taxi, and showed the tape that evening in a Greenwich Village café. The details remain disputed: the recording has never surfaced, and Sony’s battery-powered Portapak was not introduced until 1967. True or not, this origin story nevertheless condenses what video promised artists at the time: a camera in their own hands and the freedom to decide what to record, and where to show it.

What followed, once the technology caught up, is often described as the absorption of artists’ moving images into the museum, a process that began in the 1970s and accelerated in the 1990s, when image digitization and improvements in projection and playback technologies made large-scale moving-image installations increasingly practicable. Today, a contemporary art exhibition without single- and multichannel film projections is difficult to imagine, yet “video art” has become increasingly diffuse as a category within the broader field of artists’ moving images. To ask what happened to video art is therefore to ask what became of its promise as technological and institutional conditions reshaped the ways artists’ moving image work could address and assemble a public.

Marking e-flux Screening Room’s fifth anniversary, on Saturday, December 5, the one-day symposium “What Happened to Video Art?” brings together artists from different generations to consider how video’s early promise survives and changes within the expanding field of artists’ moving images. The program opens with an address by Martha Rosler, followed by three sessions with presentations by Peggy Ahwesh, Coco Fusco, Kenneth Goldsmith, Jen Liu, Alison Nguyen, Christian Nyampeta, Paul Pfeiffer, Rachel Rose, and Anton Vidokle. The discussions will focus on artist-organized distribution, how modes of exhibition alter attention and collective experience, and artists’ uses and misuses of storytelling.
