Dear readers,

Daniel Schlozman, professor of political science at Johns Hopkins, is happily familiar to n+1 readers. A frequent contributor to the magazine, Schlozman is also the author of When Movements Anchor Parties and, with Sam Rosenfeld, The Hollow Parties: The Many Pasts and Disordered Present of American Party Politics. Hollow Parties has quickly become a poli-sci classic since its publication in 2024, frequently invoked in op-eds and columns in general political conversation.

This week, n+1 publisher and coeditor Mark Krotov spoke with Schlozman less than 24 hours after the blue tsunami. As Schlozman says, “the scale of this victory is extremely impressive.” The triumph of DSA candidates across the country is an extraordinary result. So is the election of Scott Colom in Mississippi, and Angie Nixon’s close call in Florida. Abdul El-Sayed’s comprehensive defeat of the AIPAC machine on his own terms is a milestone at a moment of sickening Islamophobia. For all the pleasure of these victories, however, there are still plenty of unknowns.

Yours,

Mark Krotov: What can a Democratic majority like this accomplish in Congress?

Daniel Schlozman: This majority is big enough that it’s not dependent on the most conservative members of the caucus to pass things—but it doesn’t have anything like a veto-proof majority. Divided government can be very productive, and can also be a lot of nothing. That’s a choice. What does Donald Trump want to get done in the last two years of his administration, besides his monuments? And what do the people under him want to countenance?

The political scientist David Mayhew’s book Divided We Govern said that in the postwar years, divided government was pretty effective—but that’s not as true as it used to be. We’re a more partisan country. Still, there’s plenty of stuff around which the partisan lines are less strict, and where divided government can accomplish plenty: trade, energy policy, agriculture, the permitting reform bill that may go through in the lame duck. The other thing is that at times divided government can just keep the lights on, which means pushing through battles over the debt ceiling and the appropriations bills to keep the government open. We really don’t know which scenario is going to play out because Donald Trump, who’s an atypical president in lots of ways, has never faced this kind of lame-duck period. How will he respond?

The other thing that happens is that Congress investigates, and they’ve got lots of grounds to do that. Congress will begin to reassert its role in the public sphere. They’ve been extremely supine over the past two years, passing basically nothing except the One Big Beautiful Bill. They let Trump do all sorts of things, including violating the power of the purse, inventing fiscal justifications for spending on his ballrooms and whatnot—things that were absolutely at the core of congressional prerogatives. How much will Congress try to reassert its own traditional prerogatives, investigate, and then set the agenda for whatever’s happening in 2028? Will Congress send veto bait to the president? There’s only so much veto bait they can send. One strategy says that you send him things he’ll veto, which will draw useful contrasts. That hasn’t worked terribly well. If you actually get a bill that can pass through Congress under the filibuster pivot with a coalition this big, the president tends to like it. But we’ll see.

MK: We’re so used to this notion of Congress as a place where nothing happens. There are so many words to describe the process—churn, logjam, inertia, obstructionism. Is there any reason to think that those dynamics won’t pertain, given the size of the majority?

DS: There’s a world in which Trump is eager to make deals because he’s a deal guy, and congressional Republicans are afraid that he’s giving away the store. There are lots of futures, and that’s one of them. Big majority caucuses are ideologically diverse, and a lot of the Democrats who won yesterday are relatively moderate. At the same time, the Republican caucus in Congress is even more right-wing than it was because it’s the moderates who lost, and the remaining right-wingers are very determined to stop anything. They can probably tell Trump that they don’t want to work with him. Job one for them is to elect a new leader. Jim Jordan of the Freedom Caucus says he’s running, and he’s an extreme obstructionist.
