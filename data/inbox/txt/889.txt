November 6: How AI Civics Can Build Public Power Over Tech

More people need the means and capacity to situate, question, and shape how AI is used in their daily lives. AI civics starts by recognizing the technology not as a skill to be mastered but as a domain of public life — one where people have rights, responsibilities, and avenues for collective action. On November 6 at 6:00 p.m. ET, join us online or onsite at the Stavros Niarchos Foundation Library (SNFL) in New York City to discuss how AI civics can help us meet this moment and realize a more democratic vision of public interest technology. This timely conversation will feature Meg Young, director of Data & Society’s AI Civics program; technology reporter Khari Johnson; Sareeta Amrute, a D&S affiliate and associate professor of strategic design and management at The New School; and Hannah Sassaman, executive director of People’s Tech Project; and will be moderated by Tech Policy Press Editor-in-Chief Justin Hendrix. Together, we’ll explore how people are contesting unchecked AI acceleration; how they are challenging the ways AI systems are being pushed into their schools, workplaces, and communities; and how communities can come together to build on these efforts — at a moment when policymakers are starting to listen. RSVP and join us!

COMMENT TO THE FDA ON GENERATIVE AI-ENABLED MEDICAL DEVICES

In response to the FDA’s discussion paper on regulating generative AI-enabled medical devices, researchers at the NSF AI Research Institute on Interaction for AI Assistants (ARIA) submitted a comment drawing on research about how people use conversational AI for mental and behavioral health support. Data & Society is an institutional partner of ARIA, and the comment — which our researchers Meryl Ye, Briana Vecchione, Ranjit Singh, and board member Suresh Venkatasubramanian contributed to — is heavily informed by our work. A central finding is that the risks of conversational generative AI depend on how people come to use and rely on it over time, as well as on the quality of any single response. Thus, the FDA’s approaches to assessing the validity, effectiveness, and safety of such tools must incorporate this level of context and nuance; the comment offers five recommendations to that end.  

TESTIMONY TO NY CITY COUNCIL HEARING: EXAMINING THE RISKS POSED BY AI

Earlier this month, in testimony to the New York City Council Committee of the Whole at a hearing examining the risks posed by AI, Policy Director Brian J. Chen urged the Council to protect New Yorkers from the dangerous and reckless corporate practices currently being perpetrated by leading AI companies. He argued that industry self-regulation premised on narrow technical fixes is not nearly enough to protect the public from increasingly capable AI products and systems. City lawmakers can and should require more of companies developing and deploying agentic AI, including by mandating strong reporting, assessment, and oversight requirements. Read his testimony.

FIELD NOTES

 

“One study published last year estimated that roughly a fifth of the videos served to a new YouTube user were AI slop. The elderly are among the least likely to detect it and have the most time to consume it….For a generation that often struggled to contextualize much of what it saw online, AI created a hyperpersonalized fantasyland.” [“No iPad Till After Dinner, Mom,” Jessica Lucas, New York Magazine]

“Waymos, like data centers, provide a ready vessel for strong reactions to the broad sweep of technological change. The benefits and risks of AI can seem hazy, but cars are big and solid, and Americans love them. ‘It might be some custom LLM that causes someone to lose their job,’ Bryant Walker Smith, a law professor at the University of South Carolina who studies autonomous driving, told me. ‘But they’re never going to see that. They’re going to see the car going down the street without anybody in it.’  When a small team of Google engineers first began tinkering with self-driving Priuses, an Obama-era optimism about technology’s promise still prevailed. The product of their work arrives at a moment when its appeal is not quite as apparent as it might once have been.” [“We’re Learning to Live with Waymos. It’s Going Weird,” Molly Fischer, The New Yorker]

 

“Readers who raise suspicions about AI usually flag patterns in prose. But there is another pattern worth considering. Chatbots have learned from massive quantities of human writing, including representations of Africa shaped by the literary marketplace. It seems reasonable to expect that if you ask a chatbot to write an ‘African’ story, it will reproduce familiar conventions that have been rewarded for decades.” [“How to Be an African Writer in the AI Era,” Tope Folarin, The Atlantic]

 

“While there is so much debate about what AI can and cannot do for humans, there is too little talk about what it’s doing to humans. Studies have warned of a relationship between AI and cognitive decline in human beings. Human intelligence looks set to plunge in proportion with the rise of artificial intelligence. Perhaps our unused brains will atrophy, our heads will shrink and our fingers will turn into undulating prompt tentacles.” [“Apocalypse Now and Then,” Arundhati Roy, Equator]
